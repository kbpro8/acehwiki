**Van Boekholz** (also spelled **Van Boeckholz** in Van der Kemp's text) was a Dutch commissioner in the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]] who is best remembered for having turned away the British officer [[Major William Farquhar|Major Farquhar]] from [[Pontianak]] on the west coast of [[Borneo]] in the closing months of [[1818]]. Although a minor figure in P. H. van der Kemp's 1900 narrative, his action at Pontianak is one of the four immediate provocations that, together with [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]]'s destruction of Farquhar's treaty with [[Riouw|Riouw]], drove [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] to seek a British counter-stroke in the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay]] region — and so contributed, indirectly, to the founding of [[Singapore]] and to the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Acheh commission of 1819]].

## The Pontianak Incident

Van der Kemp introduces Van Boekholz in a single sentence, but a loaded one. The context is Raffles' alarm, late in 1818, at what he perceived as the systematic Dutch recovery of their old possessions in the archipelago: "To him the news reached that Resident Farquhar had been repulsed on Borneo's west coast by Van Boekholz, and that Farquhar's Riouw treaty had been cancelled by Wolterbeek." The passage makes clear that the Pontianak rebuff and the Riouw cancellation were, in Raffles' mind, twin evidences of a Dutch determination to close the archipelago to British enterprise. The Calcutta government accordingly wrote to London on 14 January [[1819]] hoping "that we may be in time to establish our political influence at Acheh to the exclusion of that of the Dutch or other foreign powers."

## Farquhar's Antecedents

[[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] was at this time the British Resident at [[Malacca]] and had recently returned from a tour of the [[Karimon Islands]], [[Lingga|Lingga]], and west Borneo. As Van der Kemp notes, "might he at Pontianak have been shown the door by our commissioner Van Boeckholz, at Lingga he had succeeded in concluding a contract with the sultan." Farquhar's mixed fortunes — expulsion at Pontianak, success at Lingga — convinced him of the strategic possibilities of the Karimon group, a judgement that he communicated by letter to Raffles on the afternoon of 19 January 1819, just as the lieutenant-governor of [[Bencoolen]] was preparing to leave Penang for the [[Acheh]] mission. It was that letter which triggered Raffles' sudden [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|detour to the Karimon Islands]] and, ultimately, the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]].

## Van Boekholz's Larger Significance

Although Van Boekholz appears in the article only as the author of Farquhar's repulse, the incident itself is one of the keys to understanding the chain of events that produced the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]]. Van der Kemp's argument is that Dutch firmness at Pontianak and Riouw, far from deterring Raffles, drove him to seek a free hand elsewhere — at [[Pedir]] in the first instance and at Singapore in the second. Van Boekholz thus occupies, in the article's interpretation, a small but decisive place in the chain leading to the British establishments in [[Sumatra|Sumatra's]] north and in the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay peninsula]].
