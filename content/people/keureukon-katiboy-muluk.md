The **Keureukon Katiboy Muluk** — more properly **katib ul-mulk** ("writer of the kingdom"), vulgarly **Katiboy Mulut** — was the title of the royal secretary or private secretary of the Sultan of [[Acheh]] in the period of prosperity of the sultanate. By the late nineteenth century the office had become, in Snouck Hurgronje's phrase, an "imaginary private secretary": the title was still preserved in the formulae of royal documents, but the actual work of writing and composing Malay correspondence had long since fallen into abeyance or been carried on by humble servitors of no rank.

The Keureukon Katiboy Muluk is mentioned in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* in two related contexts. The first occurs in Snouck Hurgronje's account of the III Mukims Keureukon (also called Mukim Lhee), a district of [[Acheh proper]] that belonged to none of the [[Three Sagis (XXVI, XXV, XXII Mukims)|Three Sagis]], but was claimed by both the [[XXVI Mukims]] and the [[XXII Mukims]]. The name of the district, Snouck Hurgronje explains, points to the efforts made by the holders of the hereditary title of **Teuku Keureukon** to gain the supremacy there. The title-bearer was descended from the former royal secretaries, and one branch of the family had succeeded in establishing itself in the Mukim Lhee, where, however, "they have never come to be recognized as its chiefs." The episode is cited as another example of the degeneration of offices in Acheh — the same process that converted the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]] and the [[Imeum of Chadé|imeums]] from religious or secretarial functionaries into petty adat-chiefs.

The second and more striking context is that of the formal documents of the Sultan. The title "could more easily pass from father to son than the art of composing and writing Malay letters; and besides, the importance and extent of such correspondence dwindled with the decay of the court." The work once performed by the Keureukon Katiboy Mulut — insofar as it did not altogether fall into abeyance — came to be carried on by common servitors of no rank called **Krani Pbteu** ("writers to our supreme lord"). Yet, since all official documents continued to be modelled on the pattern of those of the prosperous period, the words "His Majesty ordered this to be written by the Keureukon katibulmuluk" were still regularly found at the beginning of letters and edicts of the sultans of Acheh, long after the actual Keureukon had ceased to perform any secretarial function.

The case is well illustrated by the [[Letters patent with the ninefold seal to Panglima Meuseugit Raya|letters patent given to the Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] under [[Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan|Sultan Alaedin Muhamat Dawot Shah Juhan]] in [[1889]]. The document opens: "His exalted Majesty, the Prince of the world spoke unto the Keureukon Katiboy Muluk (or Mulut), bidding him draw up a document with the thunder-seal, to be vouchsafed unto Our uleebalang, who is called the Panglima of the Meuseugit Raya of Banda Acheh, the Seat of Peace." The fiction of the royal secretary is thus preserved even in a document which, in reality, can only have been composed by some humble *Krani Pbteu*. The Keureukon Katiboy Muluk stands, in this respect, as a typical example of the way in which Achehnese institutions survived in form long after they had ceased to function in fact — a theme that pervades the whole of Vol. I of *The Achehnese*.
