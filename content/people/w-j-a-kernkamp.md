**W. J. A. Kernkamp** — published as Prof. Dr. W. J. A. Kernkamp — was a Dutch scholar of Islam in the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Dutch East Indies]] who, in [[1946]], contributed the chapter *De invloed van den Islam* ("The Influence of Islam") to the volume *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]*, edited by Dr. [[C. W. Wormser|Wormser]]. His chapter, which runs from page 56 to page 108 of the volume, is the single densest source of Atjeh-specific material in the book: it draws heavily on [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *De Atjéhers* ([[1893]]–[[1894]]) and [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s *Atjèh* ([[1922]]–[[1923]]) to describe Atjeh's mosques, prayer houses, religious officials, calendar, feast cycle, life-cycle rites, and the partial reception of Islamic law alongside the older *adat*.

## Treatment of Atjeh

Kernkamp's chapter treats Atjeh as a *type specimen* of vigorous Indonesian Islam. The Atjeh material surfaces in nearly every section: in the discussion of the call to prayer and the use of a drum (*trom*) in Atjeh because the mosque (*[[Mesigit|mesigit]]*) is usually outside the *gampong* (village); in the description of the [[Meuseugit Raya|Meuseugit Raja]] of [[Kutaradja|Koeta Radja]] — the "eenige min of meer pompeuze moskee" (the only more or less pompous mosque) — built by the Dutch in Byzantine style at a cost of more than *f* 200,000 to replace the mosque burned in [[1873]], and dismissed by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as *\"een ledig monument onzer dwaasheid\"* (an empty monument of our folly); in the description of the [[Meunasah]] as village prayer house, council hall, men's lodging and school; of the [[Teungku|Teungkoe]] as the "mother of the *gampong*" beside the secular village head; of the [[Rangkang]] as the Atjeh equivalent of the Javanese *pesantrén*; of the [[Idja pinggang|*idja pinggang*]] hip-cloth as the sartorial *sjibbolet* (shibboleth) of Atjeh Islam for both sexes; of the [[Soerat te'h]] as the Atjeh equivalent of the Javanese *primbon* divination book; of the [[Pen (Atjeh)|*pen*]] (open/wide) concept underlying ear-piercing and gravestone placement; of the [[Boeròng|*boeròng*]] as the Atjeh variant of the *poentianak* (the vengeful spirit of a woman who died in childbed); of the [[Toean di boh]] fertility saint, of whom only the *magisch sterke geslachtsdeel* (magically potent member) is said to be buried in the grave; of the *ratib* of mid-Ramadan; of the Atjeh preference for the 27th (rather than the 21st) night as *Lailat al-Qadar*; of the [[Teungku Anjong]] *kanduri* on the 18th of Rejab; of the Atjeh proverb *\"de adat is als het binden van het dier, de hoekom als het snijden\"* (adat is like binding the beast, *hukum* like slaughtering it); and of the [[Pitrah]] and [[Djakat]] collections organised through the *imam* in the [[Gajoland|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas]] lands.

## Sources cited

In the closing bibliography of his chapter, Kernkamp names four principal sources for the Atjeh material: G. H. Bousquet's *Introduction à l'étude de l'Islam indonésien* (Paris, 1938); H. Th. Fischer's *Inleiding tot de volkenkunde van Nederlandsch-Indië* (Haarlem, 1940); [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s *Atjèh* (Leiden, 1922/3); and [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *De Atjéhers* (Batavia-Leiden, 1893/4) and *Verspreide Geschriften* Vol. IV (Bonn/Leipzig, 1924). He also cites R. A. Kern's chapter on the spread of Islam in Stapel's *Geschiedenis van Nederlandsch-Indië* (Vol. I, Amsterdam, 1938) and W. Juynboll's *Handleiding tot de kennis van de Mohammedaansche Wet* (4th ed., Leiden, 1930).

## Significance

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Kernkamp's chapter is the most productive single section of *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]*. His decision to organise his account of Indonesian Islam around a long series of Atjeh-specific illustrations — rather than around Javanese ones — means that the chapter functions as a digest of the Snouck–Kreemer synthesis for a general Dutch readership. The chapter's repeated returns to Atjeh also reveal the extent to which, in [[1946]], the scholarly picture of Indonesian Islam was still built on the Atjeh case.
