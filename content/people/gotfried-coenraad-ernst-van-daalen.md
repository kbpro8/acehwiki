**Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen** was a Dutch colonial officer and one of the most capable column commanders of the [[Aceh War]], whose brilliant [[1904]] campaign against the [[Gajoland|Gayo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas]] highlands — known to the Acehnese as the [[Colonne van Daalen naar de Gajolanden en Alaslanden (1904)|Van Daalen Column]] — made him, in the eyes of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], the obvious successor to the Aceh governorship. As Civil and Military Governor of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] from [[1905]] to [[1908]], however, he abandoned the conciliatory policy that had defined Van Heutsz's pacification system in favour of punishment and coercion — a deviation that provoked a fierce flare-up of resistance, a formal inquiry, a public rift with his patron, and his eventual removal from office. In Acehnese memory he was remembered as *Toean Obers Van Daalen* — *Toean Obers* being the Acehnese idiom for "the colonel" — and as "a man whose life had no price," a judgement recorded by the old hunter [[Datoek]] in Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]).

## Military career and the Gayo-Alas campaign of 1904

Van Daalen rose to prominence as a column commander during the [[Aceh War]], earning a reputation as an *uitmuntend colonnecommandant* (excellent column commander) for his energy, tactical skill, and relentless pursuit of the enemy. Before his [[1904]] campaign, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, the Dutch "wisten goed als niets" — knew effectively nothing — of the inhabitants of the Alas valley. In June [[1904]] Van Daalen — then still a lieutenant colonel, leading a column of 120 *marechaussées* on a four-month expedition — broke the last organised resistance in the interior, bringing the country "ineens geheel onder onze macht" (at once entirely under our power) in a single *zegetocht* (victorious campaign). Resistance was not extinguished without cost: the years that followed saw stubborn defiance, particularly in the early period, before a condition of order benefiting the population was established, and the campaign attracted later criticism in the Netherlands for the severity of its methods and the high number of casualties among the local population — criticism that the [[Atjéh-enquête|1907–1908 inquiry]] found to be largely exaggerated.

The same campaign traversed the [[Gajoland|Gayo]] lands, where Van Daalen installed the [[Kédjoerön Bédén|minor Kédjoerön Bédén]] — a child taken from [[Tamiang]] by his father, a previously deposed Kédjoerön, and paraded through [[Koeta Radja]] to sign the [[Korte Verklaring]] without the required consultation of the other *rédjë's* of [[Gajo Loeës|Gayo Loeës]] — as the legitimate territorial chief of the Gayo Loeës in [[1904]]. The expedition's name is preserved in the *van Daalengebergte*, the southeastern spur of the [[Wilhelmina-keten]] that penetrates deep into the eastern coastal plain, separating the basin of the upper [[Djamboe Aje]] from the high plateau of [[Serbëdjadi]] and forming the eastern rim of the great *Laoet Tawar* basin — and in the *systeem Graafland, van Daalen en van Heutsz*, the combined military-political doctrine credited with breaking the back of the Aceh War.

An alternative account, drawn from A. and H. Algra's *Dispereert Niet* (Deel XI, [[1946]]), records that Van Daalen departed in [[February]] [[1904]] with a force of approximately 200 *marechaussees*, charged with traversing the Gajo highlands — a territory larger than the Netherlands that had never been explored by Europeans, protected by high mountains and nearly impenetrable forests, with only a few passes guarded by fortified *bentengs*. His force crossed mountain passes comparable to the Alps, defeated a series of fortified positions each guarded by thousands of defenders, and at one point survived near-starvation by subsisting on a captured python. The expedition lasted 163 days, ending when Van Daalen reached [[Tarotoeng]] in the [[Tapanoeli]] residency on [[18 June]] [[1904]]. The campaign effectively completed the pacification of Aceh, eliminating the last significant area of resistance, and the leadership and endurance of his men were celebrated in Dutch military history.

## The Van Daalen Column in colonial memory

In Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]) Van Daalen is the second-named in [[Musket]]'s catalogue of the famous fighters and Marechaussee commanders of the Aceh War: "Gilles Graafland, Van Daalen, Campioni, Christoffel, Van der Maaten, Scheepens, Watrin, Webb, Colijn, Geertsema Beckering, Darlang and the sergeants Nussy, Salowono, Pongoh … a colourful array." The names of these *Marechaussee* commanders, the book records, "rang through the Indies like carillon music," and their "stories of brave deeds, adventures, and peculiarities went the rounds, as if they were legends."

The reputation of the Van Daalen Column is distilled in the judgement of the old hunter [[Datoek]] — a close comrade of Van Daalen — given in the chapter *Jagerslatijn*: "I know Toean Obers Van Daalen very well. That was a very great lord, 'a man whose life has no price!'" The Datoek judgement — "a man whose life has no price" — is the book's — and the Acehnese memory's — judgement on Van Daalen: a man for whom a human life had no value, the register of the column that had been conducted without mercy.

The same chapter offers one small humanising detail: "But, sir, that Toean Van Daalen was not without tenderness where animals were concerned." Van Daalen could get angry when he saw Acehnese going to the market carrying chickens by the legs, with the head down. The detail — Van Daalen's mercy for chickens — is the book's small marker of the humanising of the Van Daalen Column commander: the man for whom a human life had no value had mercy for chickens. The book's argument, voiced through Datoek, is that the Van Daalen Column reputation was the colonial army's — and the Acehnese memory's — judgement on Van Daalen, but that the man himself was not without tenderness where animals were concerned.

## Appointment as Governor of Aceh

In [[1904]], [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] became Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies and almost immediately proposed Van Daalen as his successor in Aceh, in succession to [[Jhr. J. C. v. d. Wyck]], despite widespread opposition from virtually every advisor. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]], the Adviser for Native and Arab Affairs, wrote a secret memorandum in [[1904]] warning that Van Daalen was "inhumane, ruwe, tactlooze manier van besturen en rechtspreken" — inhuman, rude and tactless in his dealings with local chiefs — and that he lacked the qualities needed for pacification. [[Hendrikus Colijn|Hendrikus Colijn]], then a captain and later advisor for the outer islands, privately favoured the more junior [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]] for the civil role, writing to Snouck Hurgronje in June [[1904]] that "Van Daalen handhaaft schitterend zijn roem als uitmuntend colonnecommandant… Toch geloof ik, dat hij eenige noodzakelijke kwaliteiten mist voor die civiele betrekking" (Van Daalen brilliantly maintains his fame as an excellent column commander… Yet I believe that he lacks some necessary qualities for that civil position). Minister [[A. W. F. Idenburg|Idenburg]] initially tried to postpone the appointment, suggesting Van Daalen be sent to lead an expedition in Boni instead.

Van Heutsz insisted. In a letter to Idenburg of [[21 May]] [[1905]] he formally appointed Van Daalen civil and military governor of *Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden*, arguing that no other candidate combined the necessary military experience and local knowledge. He acknowledged Van Daalen's faults — roughness, arbitrariness, and severity — but believed he had "learned and understood" the need for a more protective and forgiving approach.

## Governorship, 1905–1908

Upon taking office, Van Daalen pursued, instead, a policy of strict control and punishment. He imposed heavy fines on local chiefs, often for minor offences — *al te hard doorgevoerde represaille-maatregelen*, overly harsh reprisal measures — and deposited the proceeds into the local road-works fund (*wegenfonds*), a practice Van Heutsz had explicitly forbidden. He arbitrarily dismissed and imprisoned *oelèëbalangs* (territorial chiefs) without replacing them, leaving many districts without traditional leadership — a *scherpe besnoeiing der adat-inkomsten*, sharp curtailment of the customary revenues of the chiefs. He interned chiefs and their relatives at [[Sabang]] without legal grounds or judicial review, established a criminal register for chiefs recording even trivial infractions, and used the incarceration of women and children as a means of pressure. His style stood in direct contrast to Van Heutsz's pacification system, which rested on supporting and guiding the chiefs rather than punishing them; his motto, as paraphrased in official correspondence, was: "You cannot trust those fellows; you must beat and oppress them, and then they will obey."

The results were counterproductive. In districts such as [[Pidie|Pidië]] and [[Lho' Seumawe|Lho' Seumawé]], chiefs refused to cooperate and the population became alienated. In Pidië several *oelèëbalangs* had been dismissed and not replaced, so that the civil administrator effectively took over their functions — "an absolute impossibility" that undermined local governance, in Van Heutsz's later judgment, and a district in which chiefs were "exclusively punished and treated arbitrarily, against justice and equity." In Lho' Seumawé the civil administrator was so incompetent that the local headman openly told Van Heutsz the chiefs would not work with the administration because they felt unjustly treated, and that government influence had actually receded in the interior; the interior above Keureu'toë had not seen a military patrol or civil administrator for months, and local religious leaders (*teungkoe's*) had used the vacuum to turn the population against the Dutch. These policies provoked the *vrij hevige opflikkering van het verzet* (fairly fierce flare-up of resistance) of [[1907]] and [[1908]] that Van Daalen's successor [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] would have to overcome through a more conciliatory approach.

## The Atjéh-enquête and removal

By mid-1907, complaints about Van Daalen's rule had reached the press in the Netherlands and the Dutch parliament. In October [[1907]], a series of virulent articles under the pseudonym "Wekker" appeared in *[[De Avondpost]]*, accusing Van Daalen of gross misconduct and cruelty, and galvanising opposition from parliamentarians such as [[Van Kol]]. Van Heutsz initially defended his protégé, but under pressure from Minister Fock — and after receiving credible reports of abuses — he travelled to Aceh personally in November–December [[1907]], accompanied by the Army Commander [[Rost van Tonningen]] (who examined military conduct) and the Secretary-General [[D. F. W. van Rees]] (who assisted with administrative review), to conduct the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] in person. See that article for the conduct, findings, and immediate aftermath of the investigation.

The findings were devastating. In Van Heutsz's own words: "Wat toen aan 't licht kwam was meer dan ergerlijk" (What came to light was more than reprehensible) — arbitrary fines imposed contrary to his instructions, unjustified internment of chiefs and their families, persistent neglect of the principle of supporting local rulers, and a wholesale breakdown of cooperation between the administration and the indigenous elite. On [[24 December]] [[1907]], Van Heutsz sent Van Daalen a personal letter that amounted to a formal censure, writing:

> "Het kán toch niet juist zijn de landshoofden en oelabalangs door een meer bestraffend dan leidend en vormend optreden in de noodzakelijkheid te brengen hun gedrag jegens ons alleen af te meten naar de mate van vrees en ontzag, welke wij hun door voortdurende 'gevoelige correctie' met straffe hand weten op te dringen."

That is, it could not be right to force chiefs and *oelèëbalangs* to measure their conduct solely by fear and awe — imposed by continual "sensitive correction" with a firm hand — rather than by guidance and formation. Van Heutsz judged that Van Daalen had "misunderstood the fundamental principle" of pacification, and ordered the reversal of several specific measures.

Van Daalen requested relief from his post on [[28 December]] [[1907]]; his request was accepted, and he was formally relieved of his duties in May [[1908]]. The investigation's report was published in a redacted form in [[1908]], at the request of acting minister Heemskerk, omitting some of the most damaging details to avoid fuelling press attacks — though it had a sobering effect on public opinion and helped restore confidence in Van Heutsz's overall policy. In his farewell letter, Van Heutsz acknowledged the personal pain of admitting his error, but put the interests of the colony first.

## Later career and legacy

Despite the political and personal setback, Van Daalen's military reputation remained intact. After a period of leave in Europe, he returned to the Indies and was appointed Chief of the General Staff in [[1908]]; in [[1910]] he became Commander of the [[Royal Netherlands East Indies Army|Royal Netherlands East Indies Army (K.N.I.L.)]], a position he held until his retirement. He died in [[1930]].

Van Daalen's governorship remains a classic case of the tension between military pacification and civil governance in colonial history: even the most capable military commander could not succeed in a civil pacification role without tact, patience, and respect for local structures. The episode tested Van Heutsz's judgment and forced him to make a public — and personally painful — admission of error. In the broader narrative of the Aceh War, Van Daalen's fall marked the end of the purely coercive phase and the beginning of the more conciliatory approach restored under H. N. A. Swart.

## Significance

Van Daalen is significant in the AcehWiki corpus in two registers. As the historical actor Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen, he is the brilliant column commander whose [[1904]] Gayo-Alas campaign completed the military pacification of the interior and whose subsequent harsh governorship provoked the [[Atjéh-enquête]] and the public rift with Van Heutsz — a classic case study of the failure of coercive pacification when transferred from the military to the civil sphere. As the legendary *Toean Obers Van Daalen* of Merac's *Oost-Indisch Journael*, he is the second-named of Musket's Marechaussee commanders — the very great lord for whom a human life had no value, but who had mercy for chickens — and so the book's marker of the Van Daalen Column's reputation in colonial memory. The two registers coincide in a single historical person whose career joined the operational and the civil-pacification phases of the Aceh War, and whose fall marked the close of the one and the opening of the other.

## Account from *Atjèh* by J. Kreemer (Deel II)

[[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]] records a number of specific administrative details of Van Daalen's governorship ([[1905]]–[[1908]]) that are not in the earlier British and Dutch sources:

The **1904 expedition** was the Gajö-Alas campaign: Van Daalen entered the Gajö Loeüs on **2 June [[1904]]** and the Alasland on **29 June [[1904]]**, completing the subjugation of both highland areas in a single combined operation.

Under Van Daalen, the *wasé oelèëbalang* (the tribute paid to *oelèëbalang*'s as a percentage of trade-good values) was reformed by Gouvernementsbesluit (*Bt.*) 12 December **N°. 112** ([[1906]]), which fixed it at **10% of the value** of traded goods. Kreemer notes that this high rate caused a severe decline in the pepper trade of the Westkust, as cultivators and traders avoided regions where the *wasé* applied at this rate.

In October [[1905]], Van Daalen reformed the *Hasil-reglement* by Bt. 23 October **N°. 86** — tightening revenue extraction in ways that Kreemer judged to have damaged the Acehnese economy.

Van Daalen established the first **moekimschool** (village school) in [[1907]], with an initial enrolment of 38 pupils. He also introduced the *voorschotten* (advances) scheme in [[1908]], providing ƒ60,000 to Acehnese *oelèëbalang*'s and landschapskassen as advances on future revenue — a scheme his successor [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] would oppose and eventually discontinue.
