**Teuku Sabi** was an Atjèhnese *[[Uleebalang|oelèëbalang]]* who, returning from exile on [[Java|Java]], became in [[1944]]–[[1945]] the principal channel of contact between the nationalist movement led by [[Soekarno|Sukarno]] and the political leadership of Atjeh, exchanging telegrams with Sukarno that were published, in the ornate style of the period, in the *[[Atjèh Shimbun|Atjèh Shimbun]]*. Henri J. H. Alers's *Om een rode of groene Merdeka* cites this exchange as evidence that, until the very end of the Japanese occupation, contact between the Merdeka movement on Java and political Atjeh remained thin — confined largely to Teuku Sabi's personal correspondence rather than to any organised link between the two islands.

## Return from exile and contact with Sukarno

Alers records that as the *Indonesia Merdeka* idea gathered strength on Java toward the end of the Japanese occupation, the response in Atjeh — beyond the propaganda efforts described by [[Dr. A. J. Piekaar|Piekaar]] — was at first confined to a personal telegram exchange between Sukarno and Teuku Sabi, described as an *oelèëbalang* "returned from exile" on Java. The telegram, quoted by Alers from the *Atjèh Shimbun*'s special edition in the florid language typical of the period, declared that "the Atjèhnese *reuntjong* and Atjèhnese blood must be forged into a fortress of steel, to shatter the enemy should he dare an attack from West or North" — adding that "final victory is on our side, and Indonesia's independence is assured."

## Contrast with Daud Beureuh's reticence

Alers draws a deliberate contrast between Teuku Sabi's public embrace of the national Merdeka campaign and the pointed reticence of [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]], who is recorded as having stayed "on the sidelines" throughout this period. Alers suggests that Teuku Sabi's enthusiasm, like that of [[Teukoe Nja' Arif|Teukoe Nja' Arif]], may have reflected the hope among some *oelèëbalang*'s that alignment with the national movement could serve as a counterweight to the rising power of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] within Atjeh itself — a hope that the events of the [[Ulama–Uleëbalang social revolution in Atjeh (1945–1946)|social revolution]] of the following year would disappoint.
