**Maurice Henri du Croo** ([[8 April]] [[1887]] – [[23 April]] [[1951]]) was a Dutch colonial army officer, civil administrator, journalist, and writer who served in the [[Aceh|Atjeh]] region of the [[Netherlands East Indies]] during the [[1920s]]. He is best known for co-authoring the military book *[[Marechaussée in Atjeh (book)|Marechaussée in Atjeh]]* with [[Schmidt|Kolonel Schmidt]], and for his pseudonymous literary works as "Brammetje" and "Abraham Exodus". His career provides insight into the Dutch military and administrative presence in [[Aceh]] during the late colonial period, and his writings form part of the Dutch colonial literature on the [[Aceh War]] and its aftermath.

## Early Life and Education

Maurice Henri du Croo was born on [[8 April]] [[1887]] in [[Arnhem]], the Netherlands. He attended the H.B.S. (*Hogere Burgerschool*, higher civic school) in Arnhem. From [[1906]] to [[1909]] he studied at the [[Koninklijke Militaire Academie]] (Royal Military Academy) in [[Breda]], and was commissioned as a *2e Luitenant* (second lieutenant) in July [[1909]].

## Military Career in the Netherlands East Indies

In [[1910]] du Croo departed for the [[Netherlands East Indies]] as an officer of the [[K.N.I.L.]] (*Koninklijk Nederlandsch-Indisch Leger*, Royal Netherlands East Indies Army). In [[1911]] he was placed on non-activity at his own request and became an editor for the newspaper *De Locomotief* in [[Semarang]]. He returned to active duty in [[1913]] and served successively in [[Timor]] ([[1913]]), [[Endeh]] ([[1914]]), [[Koepang]] ([[1915]]), [[Soembawa]] ([[1917]]), and [[Bandoeng]] ([[1919]]). In [[1920]] he was detached as a student to the [[Hogere Krijgsschool]] (Higher Military School) in [[Den Haag]]; promoted to *Kapitein* in [[1921]], he completed his military studies on [[31 December]] [[1923]] with the classification "no. 2 Category A" and returned to the Indies. In [[1924]] he was attached to the [[Navy]] in [[Soerabaja]].

## Service in Atjeh and Civil Administration

In [[1925]] du Croo, at his own request, was posted to the [[Buitengewesten]] (Outer Provinces) — specifically to [[Aceh|Atjeh]]. There he participated in patrols in the *rimboe* (jungle) and was noted for maintaining a proper appearance even in the field. In [[1927]] he was appointed *Stafofficier* (staff officer) and was awarded the [[Ridder O.N.O. met de Zwaarden]] (Knight of the [[Orde van Oranje-Nassau]] with Swords), a decoration that recognised his service in the ongoing [[Aceh War]].

Alongside his military duties, du Croo was several times charged with the exercise of civil government (*civielbestuur*), including postings in the [[small Sunda Islands]] and in Atjeh. Fellow officer J. K. den Heijer recalled encountering him in Atjeh in the [[1920s]] "on patrol in the jungle, … a well-dressed man, not robust but tough, fit to go to a meeting or pay a visit in the same suit." Du Croo's experiences in Atjeh directly informed his later literary work, especially *[[Marechaussée in Atjeh (book)|Marechaussée in Atjeh]]*.

## Later Military and Administrative Roles

After a leave in [[1931]], du Croo returned to the Indies in [[1932]] and was promoted to *Majoor* in [[1933]]. He served in [[Magelang]] in [[1934]] and was promoted to *Luitenant-Kolonel* in [[1935]]. He retired in [[1939]] with the rank of *Kolonel* and was appointed head of the [[Luchtbeschermingsdienst]] (Air Protection Service) to organise it; on [[1 June]] [[1939]] he was relieved of that function at his own request and repatriated definitively to the Netherlands.

## Literary and Journalistic Work

Throughout his career du Croo wrote for newspapers such as *De Locomotief*, the *Soerabajaasch Handelsblad*, and the *Bataviaasch Nieuwsblad*. He published strategic articles in the *Indisch Militair Tijdschrift*. From [[1940]] to [[1942]] he was editor of the periodical *Oost en West*. Under the pseudonyms "Brammetje" and "Abraham Exodus" he produced a body of literary work drawing on his colonial experiences. His collaboration with [[Schmidt|Kolonel Schmidt]] produced two military-historical volumes: *[[Marechaussée in Atjeh (book)|Marechaussée in Atjeh]]* and *[[H. N. A. Swart|Generaal Swart]]* — the latter a biography of the [[Aceh War]] general [[H. N. A. Swart|General Swart]].

## Significance

Maurice Henri du Croo is significant as a figure who combined active military service in [[Aceh]] during the late colonial period with a literary and journalistic output that preserved and interpreted the Dutch experience of the [[Aceh War]] for a metropolitan readership. His career spans the transition from the active pacification era into the inter-war "consolidation" period, and his writings — particularly *[[Marechaussée in Atjeh (book)|Marechaussée in Atjeh]]* — constitute a primary source for the mentality and self-image of the [[K.N.I.L.]] officer corps in the late colonial Atjeh. His co-authored biography *[[H. N. A. Swart|Generaal Swart]]* likewise preserves the memory of the [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] administration under which the Aceh War was brought to its formal conclusion.

## Personal presence in the book

Du Croo appears in his own text in the first person, abbreviated *d. C.*, wherever he inserts his own recollections of Atjeh. He served in the province in the 1920s and drew on that service for several passages of testimony.

As acting *controleur* of [[Meulaboh]] in [[1925]] he was struck by the improbably numerous entries on the budget headed *onderstand* (support) to relatives of chiefs, to *[[Teukoe Gam]]* and *[[Teungkoe Poetoes]]*. It looked, he admitted, strange and wrong, and seemed to confirm the accusation that the resistance had been bought to rest. But diligent search in archives, reports and memoranda showed that each of those items had its own history and its own "the why and the wherefore", and that they had as a rule been just and wise policy, "a practical and inexpensive bridge to normal conditions."

The same year at Meulaboh gave him his sharpest illustration of what happened when Swart's pressure was withdrawn. He had nowhere, not even in muddy [[Timor]], encountered such a "varkensstal" as the *keudé* of Meulaboh — and yet one could see that it had been well, regularly and neatly laid out and built. The "drang-van-boven" no longer existed, the Governor who travelled everywhere "keutelig" and punished with expensive paint no longer went about, and neglect had followed: along the coast road the iron bridge railings had everywhere rusted away because they were no longer painted. Du Croo then held "keutelige" inspections himself; the third, after thorough warnings, brought in 168 guilders in fines, since the *keudé*-holders had naturally waited to see whether the *toean* would really punish. Then the painting was done and the rickety roofs of petroleum tins were set in red lead — "en dat was geen façade maar noodzaak."

In [[1926]] the great [[Teukoe Keumangan]] of [[Seunagan]] acknowledged to du Croo that [[T. Radja Tampo']] had indeed been present on the *pasar* at [[Djeuram]], and that this had come to his ears, but that it could not be required of him, the *[[oelèëbalang]]*, that he betray so holy and at that time harmless a figure — "the oelèëbalang who did so could not remain a territorial chief in Seunagan."

Also in [[1926]] he watched a genuine *toean* of the Swart school travel through his old Acehnese district after ten years, and saw how the Acehnese "streamed together from far and near" to see him once more. In the evening an old *[[keutjhi']]* told him with quiet, respectful inward amusement how *his* *toean* ("master, sir") had once forgiven and forgotten a debt of fifty guilders in embezzled tax money, reading the man his *toeroenan* (genealogy) aloud and then giving him fifty guilders with the words "village head, you are a good chief and you are also a good friend of mine — go home! I have forgotten this." To du Croo's question whether the money had ever been repaid or withheld from his income, the old chief could only stare in astonishment: "his master was a truly generous master!"

## Method and sources

Du Croo built the biography on interviews with Swart himself, then seventy-nine years old, whose spoken recollections are quoted at length throughout; on the collaboration and memories of [[H. J. Schmidt]]; on official documents including the *[[Koloniale Verslagen]]*, the *[[Monthly Reports of the Government of Atjeh and Dependencies|Maandverslagen]]*, *[[Patrouilleverslagen en Rapporten]]* and the [[Extract from the service record of H. N. A. Swart|extract from Swart's service record]]; and on a published literature listed in the appendix *[[List of works consulted (General Swart)|Geraadpleegde boeken, brochures, artikelen, enz.]]*.

He emphasised that Swart had preserved no letters and no notes, possessed no archive and no diaries, so that recovering even the private correspondence with Schmidt required effort. He borrowed extended narrative passages from [[H. C. Zentgraaff]]'s *[[Atjeh (book by H. C. Zentgraaff)|Atjeh]]* — notably the deaths of [[Pang Nanggròë]], [[Tjoet Meutia]] and [[Tgk. di Barat]] — and from *[[Military Constabulary in Atjeh (1943)|Marechaussee in Atjeh]]*, and from [[J. C. Lamster]]'s Atjeh article in the *[[Haagsch Maandblad]]* for the survey of the pacifying administrative work.

## Judgements

Du Croo was frankly partisan for Swart and against the detractors, whom he grouped as *katjauers* (gossip-mongers) and *vergissers* (those who erred). His most pointed passage concerns [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], whose condemnation of Swart in [[1908]] he quoted at length and then answered: "the facts and the years from 1918 to 1939 have proved that Swart could indeed do it, that he broke the resistance and, where necessary, broke it hard, and that besides this, as regards the pacification, he was precisely a pacifier of the first order." The professor, who had spared many *vergissers* in and around Atjeh none of his sharp criticism, but forgot that he was himself no administrator and probably no great judge of men, "demolished Swart before he could possibly be competent to judge of Swart."

At the same time du Croo did not conceal the cost of the war to the Acehnese, quoting Zentgraaff's judgement as the epigraph of his chapter on the patrols: "the Acehnese, men and women alike, have in general fought splendidly for what they saw as their national or religious ideal."

## Significance (du Croo, 1943)

Du Croo's book is the fullest single account of the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]] years [[1908]]–[[1918]] from the Dutch administrative side, and preserves — in Swart's own spoken words, in the *maandverslagen* it quotes, and in the private letters it prints — a mass of detail on the last phase of the [[Atjeh-oorlog]] that survives nowhere else in so connected a form. Written and published in [[1943]], during the Japanese occupation of the Indies, it is also a late monument of Dutch colonial self-understanding.
