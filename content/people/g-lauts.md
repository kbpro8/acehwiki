**G. Lauts** — *hoogleeraar* (professor) Lauts — was a Dutch historian of the East Indian archipelago, the author of a multi-volume *Geschiedenis van Nederlandsch Indië* (History of the Netherlands Indies) whose sixth volume (1866) is cited by [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] as one of the chief transmitters of a long-standing misreading of Article 6 of the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|Raffles–Coombs convention]] of April 1819.

## The Misreading of Article 6

Lauts's *Geschiedenis*, volume VI (1866), p. 149 note 1, contains the question — rhetorical, in the original — "Had not Raffles and Coombs, in the treaty of their own authority concluded with the sultan of Acheh of the 22nd of Grass-month 1819, by Article 6 excluded the trade of that kingdom to all other European powers and the Americans?" Van der Kemp introduces Lauts as the first in a chain of Dutch historians — followed by [[Smulders|Smulders]] and [[J. J. Meinsma|Meinsma]] — who repeated, without examination of the treaty text, the proposition that Article 6 of the Acheh convention established a British commercial monopoly over the sultanate.

Van der Kemp's answer to Lauts's rhetorical question is emphatic: "Absolutely not, and [the treaty] was not even of its own authority concluded." Both halves of Lauts's statement are, in Van der Kemp's reading, false. The treaty of April 1819 had been concluded by a commission duly authorised by the Governor-General of Bengal, not by Raffles and Coombs on their own authority; and Article 6, properly read, contained no exclusion of European or American trade. What Article 6 did was to prohibit the *permanent settlement* of Europeans in Acheh — a prohibition on territorial establishment, not on commerce.

## Lauts in the Chain of Error

Van der Kemp treats Lauts's reading as the first link in a historiographical chain that ran from the official instructions of Minister [[Elout|Elout]] of 31 August 1824 — themselves derived from Falck's advice of 6 August 1824 — through the official *Nota* of 1873, and into the standard Dutch histories of the second half of the nineteenth century. The same reading was repeated by [[Smulders|Smulders]] in his dissertation on the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 1824]] (p. 105), by [[J. J. Meinsma|Meinsma]] in his *Geschiedenis* (II, p. 180), and by the *Tijdschrift voor Ned.-Indië*. Lauts's volume of 1866 thus stands at the head of a thirty-year-long misrepresentation that Van der Kemp, writing in 1900, sets himself to correct.

## Significance

The importance of Lauts's error lies not merely in bibliographical pedantry. As Van der Kemp demonstrates, the misreading of Article 6 formed the documentary basis upon which the British plenipotentiaries at London in February 1824 had argued that the Acheh convention was incompatible with Article 3 of the proposed Anglo-Dutch treaty, and upon which the Dutch plenipotentiaries had agreed to a separate declaration acknowledging Achehnese "independence." The declaration, in turn, became the foundation of the long Anglo-Dutch [[Paper war|paper war]] over Acheh that lasted until the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-tractaat]] of [[1871]]. Lauts's repetition of the misreading, by lending it the authority of the professorial chair, helped to perpetuate the diplomatic error long after its original causes had passed.
