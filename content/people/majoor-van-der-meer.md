**Major van der Meer** was a Dutch *majoor* of the [[Indische Leger|Indische leger]] who commanded the 10th battalion of infantry and the left column of [[Generaal Pel|Pel]]'s two-*colonne* operation against [[Longbatta]] on [[31 December]] [[1874]], and whose *misinterpretation* of Pel's orders and subsequent loss of cohesion on the open *sawah* turned the [[Battle of Longbatta (31 December 1874)|battle of Longbatta]] into the worst Dutch reverse since the [[Battle of Lampoe Oek (16 April 1874)|échec at Lampoe Oek]]. In [[G. F. W. Borel|Borel]]'s account he is the principal culprit of the disaster.

## The Longbatta Operation

Pel's plan for [[31 December]] [[1874]] was a converging movement: the left column (10th battalion under van der Meer, with a four-gun battery and a *mineur* detachment) to march from [[Pakan Atjeh]] along the left bank directly on Longbatta; the right column (5th battalion under [[Majoor Romswinckel|Romswinckel]], under overall command of [[Kolonel Wiegand|overste Wiegand]]) to make a feint attack east of [[Kota Radja]]. Romswinckel's morning attack on the *bosch-rand* succeeded so well that the enemy streamed east to face him; the stelling at [[Pendetti]] opposite [[Lemboe]] was abandoned, and van der Meer's column reached [[Blang Tjoet]], left the river, and marched on Longbatta. The fortified [[Longbatta-Mesigit|Mesigit]] (stone mosque) was taken without a shot. Longbatta was a very important kampong, long the residence of [[Panglima Polim]], the [[Imam of Longbatta]] and other hostile chiefs.

## The Disastrous Retreat

While van der Meer held a *benteng* by the Mesigit, Pel sent him an order to *investigate* the nearest kampong-rand to the south-east. Van der Meer *misinterpreted*: he abandoned the captured Mesigit *benteng* without leaving a garrison, and marched his whole force southward over the open, marshy *sawah*. The Atjehneezen, who had streamed back in, occupied the abandoned *benteng* and cut off the column's retreat; van der Meer, marching calmly across the *sawah*, did not notice. Heavy firing broke out on the far side. Pel's staff tried in vain to recall van der Meer by signals. By sunset the firing grew heavier and the column did not appear. The men, exhausted by the march over the sticky *sawah*, were attacked from all sides in the thick brush. At first the soldiers held out and even advanced, but as more enemy arrived van der Meer ordered retreat. The retreat across the marshy *sawah*, under furious pursuit and cold-steel attacks, became a rout; the battalion lost cohesion and orders could not be given. The rear guard, pursued on foot, wandered into very marshy ground, lost all cohesion, and was cut to pieces; some men threw away their weapons. The wounded and exhausted who fell behind were mercilessly killed by the Atjehneezen.

## Borel's Judgement

Borel treats van der Meer as the principal culprit of the day. The *colonne-van der Meer* was saved from annihilation only by [[Majoor van Zijll de Jong|van Zijll de Jong]]'s heroic counter-attack with some sixty infantry and a four-gun battery that retook the lost *benteng*. The day after the battle, more than fifty severely wounded — many with dreadful *klewang* cuts — were taken into the Kota Radja ambulances. One administration officer, one *mineur* sergeant and some twenty men were missing — probably killed on the spot by the furious Atjehneezen; some forty [[Beaumont-geweer|Beaumont rifles]], much *munitie* and ambulance materials were captured by the enemy. Borel's broader lesson is that marching in dispersed order over a *doorweekte sawah* along a wooded edge loses all order and exposes troops to ambush — a lesson he would invoke again at the [[Atoeh catastrophe (13 February 1876)|Atoeh catastrophe]] of [[13 February]] [[1876]].
