## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Raja Ibrahim

**Raja Ibrahim** was an Achehnese chief who appears in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* as the guardian of [[Raja Suloyman]] (Suleman) and the opposing party in a civil war that disturbed [[Acheh]] in the middle of the nineteenth century. He is mentioned by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in the course of his discussion of the slave population of Acheh, and in particular of the importance of [[Nias|Niasese]] slaves in Achehnese warfare.

The civil war between [[Raja Suloyman]] and his guardian Raja Ibrahim, which Snouck Hurgronje dates to [[1854]]–[[1858]], was, by his account, "of small importance and little blood was spilt." A correct estimate of the weight to be attached to such civil wars in Acheh, he adds, may be found in J. A. Kruyt's *Ajeh en de Atjehers* (p. 144). Van Langen's *Atjehsch Staatsbestuur* (p. 397) is cited as the principal published reference for the conflict. Snouck Hurgronje's broader point is that, in the "endless little wars that divided the country against itself," the Achehnese commonly employed their Niasese slaves "to carry out all operations against the enemy"; and "the supporters of [Raja Ibrahim] in particular usually employed Niasese to carry out all operations against the enemy."

This notice places Raja Ibrahim in a recognizable Achehnese pattern: a chief who, whether as regent or as self-seeking rival, controlled the person and claims of a royal prince and used him as the nominal head of a faction. The relationship of guardian to ward, exploited to dominate the succession, was a recurring feature of Achehnese dynastic politics — especially during the long periods when the sultanate had lost effective control over the great chiefs of the interior. The pattern repeats itself later in the elevation of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|puppet princes]] and in the conflicts among the uleebalangs that fill the history of the country from the seventeenth century onwards.

Beyond the single notice in Vol. I, Raja Ibrahim is not further discussed in *The Achehnese*. He belongs, like [[Raja Suloyman]], to that numerous class of mid-nineteenth-century Achehnese chiefs who appear in the European record only as names attached to a local disturbance — a class whose individual biographies have otherwise left little trace in either native or European sources.
