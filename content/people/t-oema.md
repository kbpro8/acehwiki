**T. Oema**—also known as [[Teungku Oemar]], [[Toekoe Oemar]], and after his defection to the Dutch side as [[Toekoe Djohan]] or [[Panglima Prang Besar]]—was an [[Atjeh]] warlord whose shift in loyalty marked one of the most dramatic episodes of the [[Aceh War]]. His defection to the Dutch in [[1893]] and subsequent re-defection in [[1896]] became the immediate catalyst for the strategic reversal that brought [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]] to power.

## Early role and alliance with the Dutch

[[Toekoe Oemar]] first emerged as a leader alongside [[Teungkoe di Tiro]] during the revival of resistance in the [[XXII Moekims]] in the early [[1880]]s. By the time [[Generaal Deijkerhoff]] assumed governorship in [[January 1892]], [[Oemar]] had become the central figure in the Dutch strategy of using [[Atjeh]] allies to suppress [[Atjeh]] resistance. [[Deykerhoff]] placed unlimited trust in [[Oemar]], despite the absence of any record of service justifying such confidence.

Under the new policy, [[Oemar]] was officially granted clemency, permitted to maintain a legion of 250 men at Dutch expense, and later received an additional 1,000 rifles, much ammunition, and substantial sums of money. This arming and subsidising was undertaken explicitly against the warnings of [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]], whose [[1892]] report had cautioned against relying on a single powerful ally of uncertain loyalty.

## The 1896 defection

In [[1896]], [[Oemar]] "threw off the mask" (*wierp het masker af*). The co-operative posture he had maintained concealed an underlying resistance loyalty. Using the modern Dutch rifles with which he had been equipped, he turned against his former patrons. The immediate consequence was that Dutch soldiers found themselves fighting Atjeh opponents armed with modern weapons—supplied by the Dutch themselves.

The defection produced the [[Van der Wijck]] reversal. [[Governor-General Van der Wijck]] ([[1893]]–[[1899]]) recognised that [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s warnings must be followed. In [[1896]] [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] was appointed to lead military operations in [[Groot-Atjeh]], and the active subjugation policy—rather than the alliance policy—was adopted.

After the defection, [[Generaal Vetter]] was sent to [[Atjeh]] as government commissioner with three battalions of infantry. [[Toekoe Djohan]] (as [[Oemar]] was now called) and his bands were defeated and driven out of [[Groot-Atjeh]]. Through continued military action the fallen-away [[XXVI Moekims]] were cleansed of enemies, and part of the [[XXII Moekims]] was also treated—though these could not be entirely subdued.

## The failure in the XXII Moekims (1893-1894)

Even before his [[1896]] defection, [[Toekoe Djohan]] had demonstrated the limitations of the alliance strategy. In [[1893]] and [[1894]] he encountered strong resistance in the [[XXII Moekims]]—the ancestral territory of [[Panglima Polim]]—though he managed to penetrate as far as [[Senelop]]. It was precisely during this campaign that his loyalty began to shift. A treacherous plan he had devised—to strike a blow that could have cost a large part of Dutch troops their lives—failed, and shortly thereafter he defected to the hostile side.

The peculiar circumstance that the success and loyalty of [[Toekoe Djohan]] should find their end precisely in the land of the [[Polims]]—where Dutch authority had always met the toughest resistance—underscored the fundamental weakness of the alliance approach. The Dutch could arm and subsidise an ally, but they could not guarantee his loyalty, especially when operating in the very territory where resistance was deepest rooted.

## Significance

The [[1896]] defection of [[T. Oema]] is recognised as the immediate political-military hinge of the [[Aceh War]]. Together with the concurrent appointment of [[Van Heutsz]], it marks the [[1896]] turning point. The episode demonstrated conclusively that the strategy of using [[Atjeh]] proxies—however well-armed—could not achieve pacification, and that only direct military action guided by accurate ethnographic intelligence could succeed.
