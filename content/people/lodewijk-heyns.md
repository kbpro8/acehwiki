**Lodewijk Heyns** was a Dutch renegade (*renegate*) who, together with Gerard Leroy, [[Jan Banninck Coeckebakker|Jan Banninck Coeckebakcker]], Jan Jansz. Moll and presumably [[Jacob Le Maire|Lemaire]], outfitted the French Saint-Malo expedition's two ships *St. Michel* and *St. Louis* — the ships whose Atjeh pepper cargo was eventually confiscated by [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s forces at Jakatra in [[1618]] (see [[St. Michel affair at Atjeh (1620-1621)]]). Heyns himself remained in Atjeh while his brother Michiel Heyns died in the Strait of Malakka on the *St. Louis*.

## The conspiracy

The two ships of the Saint-Malo expedition — the *St. Michel* and the *St. Louis* — were "van Hollanders gekocht" (bought from Hollanders) and "uitgerust door renegaten" (outfitted by renegades): [[Lodewijk Heyns|Lodewijk Heyns]], Gerard Leroy, [[Jan Banninck Coeckebakker|Jan Banninck Coeckebakcker]], Jan Jansz. Moll and presumably [[Jacob Le Maire|Lemaire]] — "het stel samenzweerders tegen de Nederlandsche Oost-Indische Compagnie" (the gang of conspirators against the Dutch East India Company). The choice of French colours was itself part of the intrigue: the conspirators intended to trade under French flags in waters the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] claimed as its own.

## Atjeh and the Strait of Malakka, 1617-1618

The two ships were signalled already when they lay at Atjeh — where the *St. Michel* took on a full cargo of pepper. They then sailed through the Strait of Malakka, called at [[Malakka]], and were scattered by storm and current. [[Lodewijk Heyns|Lodewijk Heyns]]'s brother — Michiel Heyns — died in the Strait of Malakka of the red flux (*roode loop*), with many others whose names do not concern us here. Michiel Heyns may, Godee Molsbergen notes, be remembered by the French as perhaps the very first who for France traded at [[Pondichéry|Pondichéry]] in July [[1617]] in indigo, diamonds and cloths.

## Lodewijk remains at Atjeh

While the *St. Louis* — with Michiel Heyns aboard — sailed on through the Strait, [[Lodewijk Heyns|Lodewijk Heyns]] himself "was in Atjeh gebleven" — remained at Atjeh. His remaining at Atjeh while his brother sailed on is one of the small human details of the Saint-Malo expedition's Atjeh call: it suggests that Heyns had business or interests at the sultanate that kept him there beyond the simple loading of the pepper cargo. He thus survived his brother and was not aboard the *St. Louis* when Michiel died of the red flux.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, Lodewijk Heyns is significant as the Dutch renegade whose Atjeh sojourn and whose outfitting of the *St. Michel* produced one of the principal French intrusions into the Atjeh pepper trade of the early seventeenth century. His choice to remain at Atjeh while his brother sailed on also testifies to the sultanate's role as a place where European renegades could find refuge or commercial opportunity outside the structures of the chartered companies. The Atjeh court's willingness to host such figures — and to sell them pepper — was one of the factors that made the sultanate a persistent rival to the VOC's commercial ambitions in northern Sumatra.
