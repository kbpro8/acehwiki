**Kali Malikul-adil** — Achehnese form of the Arabic *Qāḍlu Malikul ʿĀdil*, "Judge of the Righteous King" — was the title bestowed by a reforming Sultan of [[Acheh|Acheh]] on the hereditary court judge whom he chose from the tribe of **[[Ja Sandang|Ja Sandang]] to preside over the royal court of justice. The original holder of the title was thus the first of a long line of hereditary judges and court dignitaries whose descendants, in Snouck Hurgronje's day, still bore the secularised dignity of [[Teuku Kali|Teuku Kali]] (or **Teuku Kali Malikon Ade**) at the [[Dalam|Dalam]].

The institution of the office belongs to the period — perhaps the reign of [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]] or an earlier sovereign — when the centralising port-kings of Acheh were seeking to organise the administration of justice on a footing more consonant with Islamic law. The Sultan "chose his court judge from the tribe of Ja Sandang, which had meantime greatly increased in dignity. This official received the title of Kali Malikul-adil (Malikon Ade) and the judicial office or at least the title appertaining to it remained hereditary in his family". The choice fell upon Ja Sandang, according to the legendary account preserved in the chronicles, because an ancestor of that tribe — a man of humble rank from [[Lam Panaih|Lam Panaih]] in the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]] — had once assuaged the Sultan's thirst with milk in a bamboo vessel carried *sandang*-wise on his shoulder, and had been promised in return the favour of the court.

The insignia of the office preserved the memory of its popular origin. The bearers of the title "gradually acquired the positions of masters of ceremonies at the Court. Those who have seen them in the exercise of their functions at the installation of a new Sultan, can testify that on such occasions the Teuku Kali wore a white cocoanut spathe under his head-cloth, and a blunderbuss attached to a leathern belt passing over his shoulder. The reason given for adhering to this adat is that the titular 'judge' appeared also in the character of the representative of his tribe the Ja Sandang. As, however, customs change with lapse of time, the later Sultans had permitted the holders of this office to exchange the bamboo vessel for a blunderbuss and to hide the cocoanut spathe to some extent beneath their head-cloth".

Snouck elsewhere traces the slow deterioration of the office: from a learned president of the royal court of justice, the Kali Malikon Ade degenerated into an "uleebalang of our lord", a secular chief of twelve gampongs near the Dalam who could neither read nor write, and whose pretensions to supremacy over the *Kali Rabon Jale* of the three sagis were the fruit of the misleading information given by a later holder of the title to [[Mr. T. H. Der Kinderen|Mr. T. H. Der Kinderen]] in [[1881]]. The founder of the line — whoever he was — thus became, in the course of two and a half centuries, the ancestor of an hereditary dignity at once more worldly and less learned than the office of his first institution.
