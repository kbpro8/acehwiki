**Sultan Iskandar Moeda** — also written *Iskandar Muda*, and called the Atjèhse "*Roi Soleil*" (Sun King) — was the Sultan of Atjèh from [[1607]] to [[1636]], under whose reign the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Atjèh] reached the highest power and regard. Driven by an untempered ambition, he cast his eyes far beyond the borders of his realm; from [[1612]] to [[1625]] we see him almost continuously at war; and in the course of the long reign of that "Roi Soleil" treasures could be piled up that excited the admiration of foreign visitors. The splendour of his court — with a throne estimated at forty *bharen* of gold (one *bhār* being 3 *pikols* or 375 Amsterdam pounds) and decorated with the rarest diamonds and other stones, worth, according to the Sultan's own saying, ninety times a hundred thousand guilders — was the outward sign of the greatness and wealth of Atjèh at its apogee.

## The Apogee of the Sultanate

The successors of [[Sultan Ali Moeghajat Sjah|Sultan Ali Moeghajat Sjah]] continued the work of the energetic founder, until the Atjèhse Realm reached the highest power and regard under the reign of Sultan Iskandar Moeda — the Atjèhse "*Roi Soleil*" (1607–1636). Driven by an untempered ambition, he cast his eyes far beyond the borders of his realm. From [[1612]] to [[1625]] we see him almost continuously at war. No wonder that in the course of the long reign of that "Roi Soleil" treasures could be piled up that excited the admiration of foreign visitors.

## The Splendour of the Court

The throne of Sultan Iskandar Moeda was estimated at forty *bharen* of gold (one *bhār* being 3 *pikols* or 375 Amsterdam pounds) in weight, and was decorated with the rarest diamonds and other stones, worth, according to the Sultan's own saying, ninety times a hundred thousand guilders. In the first fourteen days that the ambassador spent at Atjèh, he seldom came to the Sultan, or this one let him always see many rarities, "*die al bij malkanderen een onwaardeerlijcken schat souw bedragen*" (which all together would amount to a priceless treasure). At another place the ambassador says:

> "*Op dit instant liet de Sultan mij soo uytermaeten waerdige en menichte van allerhande dat schoone stucken sien juweelen en diamanten men schier beschaemt zoude wezen 't zelve te seggen: ende beroemde zich, dat soo ick machtich ware twee etmalen achter den anderen te sitten, mij gestadich veranderingen van costelijcke stucken zoude thoonen.*"

And so everything was "*naar advenant*" (in proportion).

## The Snouck Hurgronje Critique

But, as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. Snouck Hurgronje] wrote:

> "*Beziet men dien luister op den keper, dan neemt men wel uitbreiding naar buiten waar, vermeerdering van heerschappij over havens (de zetels van relatieve beschaving en rijkdom in alle Maleische landen) en daardoor vermeerdering van rijkdom, die tot een glansrijke organisatie van het Hof aanleiding geven, maar geen ernstig streven naar den opbouw van solide inzettingen, die dynastieke omwentelingen overleven.*"

(If one examines that splendour closely, one perceives indeed extension outward, increase of dominion over harbours (the seats of relative civilisation and wealth in all Malay lands) and thereby increase of wealth, which give occasion to a glorious organisation of the Court, but no serious striving for the building of solid institutions that survive dynastic revolutions.)

## The Decline

It is also known how the Atjèhse Sultanate — bearer of that wealth — gradually declined in significance, as the popular chiefs developed ever greater power and various dependencies tore themselves loose. But first the Sultan had to miss important trade advantages, since the tin trade of [[Perak|Perak]] came in [[1645]] principally into the hands of the Dutch.

## Significance

Sultan Iskandar Moeda is the principal Atjèhse figure of the 17th century and the Sultan under whom the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Atjèh] reached its apogee. The almost continuous wars from [[1612]] to [[1625]], the treasures piled up in the course of the long reign, and the splendour of the court — with the throne of forty *bharen* of gold and the rarest diamonds and other stones — were the outward signs of the greatness and wealth of Atjèh at its apogee. The Snouck Hurgronje critique — that the splendour was outward extension and increase of wealth, but no serious striving for solid institutions — is the principal scholarly judgement on the Iskandar Moeda reign, and frames the subsequent decline of the sultanate.
