**Teungku Kutakarang** (died [[1895|November 1895]]) was an Achehnese ulama, a leader in the [[Holy War (Jihad) against the Dutch|holy war]] against the Dutch, and the most persistent rival of [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] in the direction of the religious party. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] describes him as "an active, clever but peculiar man," whose perversity of opinion was as much a personal idiosyncrasy as a settled conviction. He lived in the neighbourhood of the tomb of the "pepper saint" [[Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun]] in the [[IX Mukims]], by whose miraculous power the pepper-plant was said to have originated in Acheh, and he delighted to call himself the servant of that tomb, drawing thereby the numerous gifts dedicated to it.

## Earlier career

Long before the coming of the Dutch to Acheh, Teungku Kutakarang had made himself conspicuous by his eagerness "to pose as the teacher of doctrines different from those of the majority of Achehnese ulamas." He was always trying to entice away the disciples of others, and his decisions on points of law were of so strange a character that he was once banished from [[Banda Acheh|the capital]] in the sultan's name. He figures in Vol. I as the author of one of the more remarkable Achehnese ethnological conjectures: the view that the Achehnese are composed of elements derived from three peoples — the Arabs, the Persians and the Turks. Though "he has absolutely no grounds for this absurd idea," Snouck notes that those who looked up to him as a great scholar accepted his theory without hesitation.

## Peculiar tenets

The war with the Dutch gave Teungku Kutakarang the opportunity, especially after the [[Dutch policy of 'concentration'|Dutch "concentration" policy]], to try his fortune afresh. At first he worked under, or alongside, Teungku Tiro, to whom he found himself constrained now and then to pay public homage. In the circle of his intimates, however, he spoke contemptuously of the great man as *Leube Saman*, criticising his rules and decisions, and "certainly felt but little sorrow at the death of that honoured and dreaded ulama." He differed from Teungku Tiro on a series of points, most of them trivial in themselves but magnified by mutual jealousy. Where Teungku Tiro taught that the prohibition of the Moslim law against the wearing of gold or silk applied also to combatants in the holy war, Teungku Kutakarang decreed the contrary — and carried his opposition so far as to insist that the warriors of Allah should wear gold and silk, so that the Dutch, finding these costly objects on the bodies of the slain, "might be dismayed by the wealth of Acheh which defied all reverses." He taught, again in opposition to Teungku Tiro, that relations with those within the Dutch *linie* were not sinful but should be encouraged, since intercourse with the infidel gave opportunity to incite disaffection, to plunder and slay, and to retreat in safety.

Perhaps his most singular tenet was that, under existing circumstances, the [[Friday service (Jumu'ah)|Friday service]] — universally performed in Arabic — should in Acheh be preferably celebrated in the Achehnese tongue. He also taught great forbearance for the faults of the combatants in the holy war, proving by examples from sacred tradition and history that many sins were forgiven them in consideration of their noble work. The fighting men who lodged with him were treated as distinguished guests: contrary to Achehnese custom they sat at table upon chairs, and water was set before them in glasses in place of brass drinking-vessels.

## The Tadkirat-ar-rakinut and the sabil treasury

After Teungku Tiro's death (December [[1891]]), Teungku Kutakarang roused himself to greater activity and circulated, under the Arabic title *Tadkirat-ar-rakinut* ("[[Tadkirat-ar-rakinut|exhortation to the inactive]]"), an enlarged and amended edition of a number of politico-religious pamphlets already known in a narrower circle. In the common Achehnese metre he proclaimed the principles sketched above, sought to rouse the people to better organization, and represented the Dutch as outcasts of humanity whose aim was the destruction of the countries over which they held sway. The sultan he hardly mentioned, employing only the similitude of the marriage of the sultans with the State of Acheh to remark that a bridegroom who will not lift a finger to save his bride from the stranger is unworthy of her. The collection of the [[Sabil contributions (lui sabi aqq sabil)|sabil monies]] should, he insisted, be made by concerted arrangement, and a central treasury established — "of course with Teungku Kutakarang as its administrator" — in the neighbourhood of the theatre of war; provision should also be made for attacking the enemy by sea. The whole programme was rounded off with his favourite metaphor: the Achehnese were wasting themselves on "louse-questions," while the "elephant" — the infidel invasion — went unheeded.

## Withdrawal and death

After the [[Submission of Teuku Uma to the Dutch Government|submission of Teuku Uma]] to the Dutch Government, Teungku Kutakarang withdrew to a great extent from public life. He never came into contact with the Government, though in the last years of his life his friends succeeded in making the Dutch civil officers believe that this fanatic ulama had been transformed into an ardent advocate of acknowledgment of the supremacy of the Gompeuni. He died in November [[1895]].
