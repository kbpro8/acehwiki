**T. N. Nieuwenhuyzen** was the vice-president of the [[Raad van Nederlandsch Indie|Council of the Netherlands Indies]] who, in [[1873]], was dispatched as *gouvernements-commissaris* (government commissioner) to [[Acheh|Atjeh]] with four battalions of troops to demand Dutch sovereignty from the Sultan. His ultimatum of [[22 March]] [[1873]] and the subsequent bombardment precipitated the [[First Atjeh expedition (March-April 1873)|first Atjeh expedition]] — a campaign that ended in failure and retreat, provoking the greatest parliamentary controversy of the decade.

## Selection as gouvernements-commissaris

Nieuwenhuyzen had previously served as *commissaris* at [[Banjermasin|Banjermasin]] and as resident of [[Riouw|Riouw]], where he had concluded the [[Siak-tractaat (1858)|Siak treaty]] that extended Dutch sovereignty over the entire east coast of Sumatra — without a single soldier being involved. He was also known as a follower of [[I.D. Fransen van de Putte|Van de Putte]] and his opinions; the [[Raad van Indie|Raad van Indie]]'s unanimous advice of the end of [[1870]] — warning against all the consequences of a war with Atjeh — had been signed by Nieuwenhuyzen himself.

[[I.D. Fransen van de Putte|Minister Fransen van de Putte]] pointed to Nieuwenhuyzen when the Governor-General telegraphed that the original commissioners [[Schiff|Schiff]] and [[Van de Wall|Van de Wall]] were unavailable (one sick, the other unable to leave his post). The Minister's telegram of [[18 February]] [[1873]] stated: "Misschien is vice-president Raad van Indie in de tegenwoordige omstandigheden meest geschikt, daar welligt gouverneur Sumatra's Westkust ook niet weg kan." Loudon, however, had already asked Nieuwenhuyzen before receiving this telegram.

## The Sultan's objection

The Sultan of Atjeh had declared that he did not wish to have dealings with Nieuwenhuyzen, because Nieuwenhuyzen had concluded the [[Siak-tractaat (1858)|Siak treaty]] — which had altered Atjeh's borders in ways the Sultan had never recognised. The [[Insinger|Insinger]] in the [[Tweede Kamer debate on Atjeh war origins (April 1874)|Tweede Kamer debate]] called Nieuwenhuyzen "the most unwelcome person to come into contact with the Sultan of Atjeh," and argued that selecting this man was what one would do if one wished war, whereas the governor of Sumatra's Westkust was the man designated by the [[Siak-tractaat (1858)|treaty of 1858]] for all relations with Atjeh.

## The ultimatum of 22 March 1873

Nieuwenhuyzen arrived at Atjeh and, on [[22 March]] [[1873]], delivered an ultimatum to the Sultan. The letter demanded recognition of Dutch sovereignty as the primary condition, with only a short period for compliance. A manifest was simultaneously sent ashore stating: "This manifest will be delivered to the Sultan today, and as soon as its receipt is confirmed, it will be further confirmed by the firing of some projectiles into or near the Sultan's residence, from which we are only 3,600 metres distant." The ultimatum changed the originally stipulated 14-day period to 24 hours — a modification that [[I.D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] later took "entirely upon myself," arguing that once blood had been shed, article 6 of the original instruction (which envisaged only sovereignty on paper) was no longer applicable and the right of establishment must be reserved.

The [[Haffmans|Haffmans]] in the [[Tweede Kamer|Tweede Kamer]] characterized the entire proceeding as one in which "the commissaris fell, as they say, with the door in the house; he came equipped for war and brought the war. The asking of clarification and accountability that preceded was merely for form, an idle display."

## The failed first expedition

Nieuwenhuyzen's mission ended in the disastrous [[First Atjeh expedition (March-April 1873)|first Atjeh expedition]]. [[Nierstrasz|Nierstrasz]] in the [[Tweede Kamer|Tweede Kamer]] called Nieuwenhuyzen "the truly guilty one" while noting that he lived in Arnhem on a pension of 12,000 guilders, whereas "the poor Van Daalen" — referring to the colonel blamed for the first expedition's failure — could not even receive his pension.
