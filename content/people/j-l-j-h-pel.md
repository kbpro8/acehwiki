**J. L. J. H. Pel** — usually rendered ***Pel*** in the school-textbook tradition and ***Pell*** in the staff history, and styled **General Pel** (*Generaal Pel*) after his promotion to major-general — was a Dutch colonel of the infantry, promoted major-general by extraordinary Royal Decree of [[18 February]] [[1875]], who served as the first Military and Civil Commander (*Mil. en Civ. bevelhebber*) of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] from the departure of [[Jan van Swieten|Lieutenant-general Van Swieten]] in [[April 1874]] until his sudden death from an arterial rupture at the kampong Tonga on [[24 February]] [[1876]], in the middle of the campaign that broke the [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI Moekims]]. His appointment in [[1874]] — the year after the first Dutch military expedition against [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]] — marks the formal inauguration of the *Gouvernement* as an administrative unit of the [[Netherlands Indies|Netherlands Indies]], succeeding the unstable arrangements of the early [[Aceh War|Atjeh-krijg]] years. His period of command was marked by the abandonment of Van Swieten's defensive policy in favour of systematic offensive operations, and by the formulation in his note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] of the program that guided Dutch strategy in Atjeh until [[1877]].

## Succession to Van Swieten

The [[De Kleine Oosterling|Wijma school reader]] records Pel's succession to Van Swieten as part of the aftermath of the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the kraton of 24 January 1874]]: General Van Swieten considered his task accomplished and departed once more for the mother country. Kolonel Pel — later appointed general — became supreme commander in his place. Pel's succession to the supreme command of Atjeh thus followed Van Swieten's return to the Netherlands, and his appointment to the rank of general was made in connection with this succession.

When [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] and the bulk of the expeditionary force departed on [[26 April]] [[1874]], Pel succeeded him as civil and military supreme commander with a garrison of 128 officers, 1,788 Europeans, 176 Africans, 489 Ambonese and 698 natives. Van Swieten's note of [[22 April]] [[1874]] had laid down a defensive policy: active defence of [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] itself, but elsewhere passive defence, on the ground that "the enemy's offensive strength is not great and will gradually diminish," and that his defensive strength was considerable owing to the nature of the terrain.

The same source — *Het Land van Jan Pieterszoon Coen* — records that the command over the 3,000 troops left behind after the second expedition, and the political leadership, was entrusted to Kolonel Pel: "*Aan Kolonel Pel werd het bevel over de achtergelaten 3000 man opgedragen, benevens de politieke leiding*."

## The Échec of 16 April 1874

Already on [[16 April]] [[1874]] Dutch troops had suffered an *échec* (reverse). A patrol had come upon an enemy fortification near Kota Radja; a column sent by Colonel Pel, then commandant of Kota Radja, failed to take it. "The position was so well covered that our fire had no effect, and a storming, twice undertaken, failed, so that one was forced to return with a loss of 9 dead and 86 wounded, among whom 10 officers." Van Swieten, already on the point of departure, did not think it necessary to wipe out this *échec*.

## Pel's Note of 30 April 1875

That Van Swieten's defensive policy would not bring the war to an end was soon evident. Even before Pel's departure on leave in [[June 1875]] the enemy had resumed the struggle "with the greatest possible boldness and perseverance," and the [[Westerlinie|Westerlinie]] had to be occupied in May–June to connect Kota Radja with [[Marassa|Marassa]] and [[Lampassei|Lampassei]]. The principal post on this line, [[Oleh-leh|Oleh-leh]] on the coast west of the Atjeh-rivier mouth, became the principal landing-place; a temporary bamboo sea-head was built, replaced on [[11 August]] by a wooden auxiliary head built by the engineers. The [[Noorderlinie|Noorderlinie]] was also strengthened with posts at [[Lamara|Lamara]] and Kota Radja Bedil.

During his leave on Java, Pel succeeded in winning the government for his plans. His note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] to the Governor-General set out his analysis: the task assigned by Van Swieten had been fulfilled — the *hoofd-stelling* (main defensive position) was secured, communication with the sea assured — but "all those victories have as yet given few political results." The war-party still had great influence; it consisted largely of persons who lived far enough from the theatre to be safe from immediate danger, including men from the [[XXII Moekims|Sagi XXII]], Kerkoen, Silang in the [[XXVI Moekims|Sagi XXVI]], and "persons from all regions, also from the dependent states, who take part in the war from religious zeal, or from a love of war, or because they have reason to consider themselves less safe in their own hearths." It was led by influential chiefs such as [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]], [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]], [[Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang|Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang]], and the family members of the former sultans, [[Toewankoe Haschim|Toewankoe's Haschim]], [[Toewankoe Hoesin|Hoesin]], and [[Toewankoe Abdoel Madjid|Abdoel Madjid]], "who rightly saw that they had nothing more to expect from a foreign overrule."

His program was: consolidation of the actual position; continuation of hostilities as soon as the fighting forces permitted, with the object of separating the enemy entirely from the sea and clearing the terrain near the Dutch establishments of enemies; introduction of direct administration in the occupied territory, with respect for the domestic rights and institutions of the native population, but avoidance of bringing under direct administration those regions that might prove willing to submit. Pel further urged the construction of a harbour with land-heads, docks and coal-depots, and good judicial administration. "It is the crown on the work; it is the only way to make our settlement useful and productive."

## The Campaigns of December 1875 – February 1876

The government authorised Pel by his instructions of [[10 November]] [[1875]] to act in this sense, and to subject successively the VI, IV and IX Moekims, and then the eastern territory as far as [[Kroëng Raja|Kroëng Raja]]. On [[26 December]] [[1875]] at five in the morning three columns left the *hoofd-stelling* for the conquest of the [[VI Moekims|VI Moekims]] south-west of Kota Radja. The enemy's fortifications at [[Mibouw|Mibouw]], [[Ketapan Doewa|Ketapan Doewa]] and [[Djempit|Djempit]] were taken and [[Pakan Badak|Pakan Badak]] occupied. "In six days the strong enemy line opposite our posts south of Kota Radja had been broken, turned and abandoned by the enemy. We were wholly master of the VI Moekims and, by the occupation of Pakan Badak, in possession of an important point that was to serve as a base for operations against the IV Moekims."

On [[15 January]] [[1876]] the advance into the [[IV Moekims|IV Moekims]] began over the *pas van Blang Kala* (pass of Blang Kala). The path was so narrow that four mules with ammunition cases fell into the ravine; the guns had to be taken apart and carried by coolies. By the afternoon of [[17 January]] the troops descended into the plain of the IV Moekims; on [[18 January]] [[Kroëng Raba|Kroëng Raba]] was reached; on [[25 January]] the troops returned to Pakan Badak. In ten days, from [[28 January]] to [[7 February]], the [[IX Moekims|IX Moekims]] was also reduced. Boekit Daroe, Boekit Teroen, the *missigit* of Oleh Soesoe, Toeram, Atoöh, the *missigit* of Biloel, Lamrong, Pagger Ajer were all taken in succession.

## Marine Operations, January – February 1876

As senior Dutch commander (*opperhevelhebber*) of the colonial forces in Atjeh during the [[1875]]–[[1876]] expeditionary campaign, Pel also directed operations involving the [[Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers|Korps Mariniers]] from the main camp at Kota Radja. He coordinated closely with a range of Dutch officers — [[F. T. Engel|Lieutenant-Colonel Engel]], [[J. R. J. P. Cambier|Lieutenant Cambier]], and [[Cramer von Baumgarten|Captain Cramer von Baumgarten]], who commanded infantry, marine, and artillery units respectively — and with the marine officers [[J. B. Verhey|Lieutenant Verhey]], [[Mirandolle|Lieutenant Mirandolle]], and [[Diepenheim|Major Diepenheim]].

Pel received explicit instructions from the Dutch colonial government to subdue the IX Moekims. On [[27 January]] [[1876]] he wrote to [[J. B. Verhey|Lieutenant Verhey]] that the marine detachment should not be withdrawn from the coast, fearing a negative impression for the Dutch presence. He ordered the construction of a fortified *benteng* at [[Bockit Daroe|Bockit Daroe]] on [[29 January]] [[1876]] and directed the subsequent march towards the Atjeh-rivier. The same month, on [[3 January]] [[1876]], he had commanded the burning of houses in [[Pakan Badak|Pakan Badak]] after the local population raised a white flag, describing the action as necessary to suppress "traitorous" behaviour. He inspected the troops before their departure from Aceh on [[5 February]] [[1876]], praising their conduct despite difficult terrain and disease-ridden conditions.

His orders also shaped the march towards the Atjeh-rivier and the retreat from the [[Beradin|Beradin]] pass in favour of the [[Blang Kala|Blang Kala]] route.

## Death at Tonga, 24 February 1876

The campaign then turned to the [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI Moekims]]. Tjapoetoe, Oleh Karang, Lamjong Tonga were the first stages. "It was not given to General Pel, however, to bring it to an end. In the kampong Tonga, where he had his headquarters, he died suddenly of an arterial rupture in the evening of [[24 February]] [[1876]]: a great loss for the Netherlands-Indian army and the Dutch interests in Atjeh." The command passed temporarily to Lieutenant-colonel [[F. T. Engel|F. T. Engel]], and the operations were continued according to Pel's plan. Over [[Silang|Silang]] and [[Tjadé|Tjadé]] [[Kwala Gighen|Kwala Gighen]] was reached, and fortifications were laid out there and at Kota Pohama. But for the moment the troops could not advance to [[Kroëng Raja|Kroëng Raja]]: 1,200 men were in hospital, 1,893 had been evacuated between [[1 January]] and [[10 March]], and 503 had died in the hospital of Kota Radja alone.

Pel's death in [[1876]] — almost two years after his [[1874]] succession — was thus unexpected, and is the staff history's principal instance of the loss of a capable Dutch commander in Atjeh by natural causes rather than by combat. The death of Pel left the supreme command of Atjeh vacant, and was the immediate occasion of the appointment of [[K. van der Heyden|Generaal Van der Heyden]] as the next supreme commander.

## A Discrepancy on Pel's Fate in the Sources

The death of Pel at kampong Tonga on [[24 February]] [[1876]] is the most comprehensively documented event in his career and is recorded in detail by J. A. de Bruyne ([[1912]]) on the authority of the contemporary staff history. Two discrepancies in the broader source base are nonetheless worth noting:

1. **The school-textbook date of 1875.** The [[De Kleine Oosterling|Wijma school reader]] ([[1901]]) records Pel's death as occurring "a year later" after his [[1874]] succession — i.e. in [[1875]]. The same school reader also describes Pel as "this capable senior officer" who "died a year later unexpectedly." The discrepancy is a school-textbook simplification; the staff-history date of [[24 February]] [[1876]] is followed in this article.

2. **The marine-corps silence on Pel's death.** The *Geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers* (Dorren, [[1948]]), pp. 135–141 — which records Pel's orders to the marine detachment during the [[1875]]–[[1876]] campaign — does not mention his death at Tonga. The result is a wiki-source article on "General Pel" that closes by speculating that "after the conclusion of the 1875–1876 campaign, General Pel returned to [[Batavia|Batavia]], where he continued to serve within the Dutch colonial administration, though specific subsequent postings are not detailed in the available sources." This account is inconsistent with De Bruyne's record of Pel's death at kampong Tonga on [[24 February]] [[1876]] in the very middle of the campaign, and is here corrected. The marine-corps history's silence on Pel's death is a function of its focus on the marine detachments rather than on the supreme command; it does not support an inference of Pel's survival.

## Administrative Office and Successors

Pel's brief tenure established the dual military-civil character of the governorship that would persist, with periodic adjustments, for the next thirty years. He was succeeded in [[1876]] by [[G. B. T. Wiggers van Kerchem|G. B. Th. Wiggers V. Kerchem]] (in the same *Mil. en Civ. bevelhebber* capacity), then by [[A. J. E. Diemont|A. J. E. Diemont]] ([[1876]]–[[1877]]). The structure was modified under [[K. van der Heyden|K. Heyden v. D.]], who served as *Wd. Mil. en Civ. bevelhebber* from [[1877]] to [[1881]] and was redesignated *Civ. en Mil. Gouverneur* in [[1878]] — the title that, with one interruption, would be borne by his successors down to [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]].

## Significance

Pel's appointment formalised the transition from ad hoc military occupation to the institutional structure of a *Gouvernement*. The very existence of the Bijlage I list — presenting an unbroken chain of commanders and governors from [[1874]] to the handbook's publication — was itself a political statement: a demonstration that, after thirty years of war, the territory had become a permanent part of the colonial administrative order. In the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]] narrative, Pel is the principal Dutch commander of the interregnum phase between the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the kraton]] and the [[Governorship of General Van der Heyden|Van der Heyden governorship]], and the staff history's principal instance of the loss of a capable Dutch commander by natural causes. The school reader's emphasis on Pel's capable character — and on his unexpected death — frames the interregnum phase as a Dutch loss that would be compensated only by the subsequent appointment of Van der Heyden, "the hero of Samalangan."

Pel's leadership of the marine-ground operations during the [[1875]]–[[1876]] campaign — the burning of villages such as Pakan Badak, the construction of fortified *bentengs* (forts) at Bockit Daroe and other positions, the coordinated use of the [[Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers|Korps Mariniers]] alongside infantry and artillery — also exemplifies the Dutch high-command's reliance on decisive, often harsh measures to subdue Achehnese resistance.
