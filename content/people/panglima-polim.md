**Panglima Polim** (also spelled *Panglima Polem*) was the hereditary chief (*panglima sagi*) of the [[XXII Mukims]], the foremost of the three *sagis* surrounding the Achehnese capital, and de facto ruler of [[Acheh]] in the years around [[1819]]. The title, literally “Lord Elder Brother”, had been borne “for many generations” by the chief of the XXII Mukims, who ranked first among the sagi chiefs. In the protracted [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] between [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Sultan Saif ul-Alum (Syf)]], Polim held the balance of power, and the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Commission]] of [[1819]] identified him as the actual master of Acheh. Later, during the later phase of the [[Aceh War]] (1875‑1879), he re‑emerged as a principal military leader of Acehnese resistance, noted in Dutch military historiography (see *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers*, entry on [[116]]).

## The Three Sagis and the hereditary chief

By the end of the seventeenth century the Achehnese sultanate had effectively fallen under the tutelage of the chiefs of the three *sagis* surrounding the capital—the [[XXII Mukims]], the [[XXV Mukims]] and the [[XXVI Mukims]]. A Calcutta‑government report of [[7 October]] [[1820]] to London observed that authority was exercised “in name by the council of the Sagis, but in reality by [[Panglima Polim]] assisted by his adherents”. Polim’s hereditary title “Lord Elder Brother” designated the chief of the XXII Mukims and made him first among equals in the federation of the [[Three Sagis]].

## The 1819 Raffles–Coombs Commission

When the joint [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|commission of Raffles and Captain Coombs]] arrived off Acheh on [[14 March]] [[1819]], no sultan was present in the capital. The commission summoned the chiefs to an assembly; Polim replied from his XXII Mukims that the assembly would be held in eight days and that the commission should not come ashore until then, directing any matters to the [[Shahbandar of Acheh|Shahbandar]]. The Bengal report described Polim’s conduct as a display of “his entire disregard for both the competitors and of his own undisguised supremacy at the capital”. Polim subsequently took residence in the sultan’s palace, leading the Bengal government to conclude that he alone was the real ruler, that [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] was not recognised, and that the pretender Syf was “merely a tool” in the hands of the parties. Polim showed “no inclination to acknowledge in him any real sovereignty”.

## The warning of treason and Raffles’s withdrawal

On the appointed day a chief of the Jauhar party warned Raffles of a possible treason. Holding the casting vote, Raffles judged it wiser not to venture ashore and claimed illness, thereby dissolving the assembly. The Calcutta government later disapproved, noting that “courtesy and decorum” required the commissioners to attend a meeting they themselves had requested. With the assembly dissolved, Polim retained mastery of Acheh; after seven fruitless weeks on the roadstead the commission sailed for [[Pedir]] to treat with Jauhar Alum.

## Continued supremacy and the 1822 letter

Polim’s supremacy survived the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of Pedir]]. In a letter of [[15 April]] [[1822]] [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] wrote to [[W. E. Phillips|Governor Phillips]] of Penang that he hoped “in two or three months” to meet “Pangleema Polim” and to re‑establish “the laws of Government in some degree”. On [[4 December]] [[1822]] the [[Three Sagis]] sent a “brutal” letter to Governor Phillips refusing further recognition of Jauhar Alum and demanding the return of Syf; the letter reflected Polim’s policy. A Bengal review of [[28 January]] [[1825]] still reported that “the whole power at Acheen was in the hands of Panglima Polim”.

## The Queen‑Mother and Polim’s later role

After [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]]’s death in November [[1823]], the [[Queen-Mother of Acheh|Queen‑Mother]] together with the three Sagis exercised power in practice with Polim. Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]’s minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] noted that the present occupier of the throne was not the legitimate heir and that a disputed succession would likely ensue when the legitimate son (then in the custody of the Queen‑Mother) came of age—an indication that Polim’s faction had installed a nominee. Throughout this period the British at [[Penang]] recognised that the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|treaty of 1819]] remained a [[dead letter]] chiefly because Polim would not permit either sultan to rule.

## Role in the Aceh War (1875‑1879)

### Office and position in the 1870s

The political constitution of [[Acheh]] divided the realm into three sagis—[[XXV Moekims|XXV]], [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI]] and [[XXII Moekims|XXII]]—and the coastal [[Onderhoorigheden|Onderhoorigheden]]. The sultan’s authority was nominal; real power lay with the hereditary chiefs (*hoeloebalangs*), the most eminent being the panglimas of the three sagis, “among whom the panglima polim, the head of the XXII moekims, took the first place.” Thus the name functioned both as a title and a personal designation.

### The war‑party of [[1875]]

In a note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] to the Governor‑General, [[J. L. J. H. Pel|General Pel]] described the war‑party as consisting largely of men from the Sagi XXII, Kerkoen, Silang in the Sagi XXVI, and other regions, motivated by religious zeal, love of war, or insecurity. He listed the chief leaders as “Panglima Polim, Imam Longbatta, Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang and others”.

### Campaigns of [[1879]]

After the [[Submission of Habib Abdurrahman (13 October 1878)|submission of Habib Abdurrahman on [[13 October]] [[1878]]]] and the start of the [[Campaign against the XXII Moekims (1879)|campaign against the XXII Moekims]] on [[23 March]] [[1879]], the capture of [[Indrapoeri|Indrapuri]] on [[24 March]] [[1879]] failed to secure submission. Heavy fighting followed at [[Djerreek|Djerreek]], [[Redep|Redep]] and [[Panteh Karang|Panteh Karang]]; on [[6 May]] and [[7 May]] [[1879]] the Dutch suffered great losses at Panteh Karang, and the fortress fell only on [[7 May]] with reinforcements. On [[31 May]] [[1879]] troops advanced on [[Gleiêng|Gléiëng]], Polim’s fortified chief place, which was finally taken on [[9 July]] [[1879]] after a tiring march and repeated fights. The campaign in the XXII Moekims was then considered complete.

### Flight to the Pedir upper lands

Following the [[Campaign against the XXVI Moekims (July 1879)|campaign against the XXVI Moekims]] of early July [[1879]] and the [[Selimoen Expedition (August 1879)|Selimoen expedition]] of August [[1879]], Polim, his son [[Radja Koelala|Radja Koelala]], Imam Longbatta, Toewankoe Haschim and others retreated to the interior south of Pedir, “namely at Kemala”, beyond Dutch reach.

## Historical note in Dutch marine corps historiography

An entry for “Panglima Polim” appears on page [[116]] of *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers van 1665‑1945* (pages [[485]]‑[[488]]). Its inclusion among Dutch marine corps entries underscores his prominence in the resistance against Dutch incursions into [[Aceh]] and reflects the impact of Acehnese leaders on Dutch military planning.

## Legacy

Panglima Polim’s resistance, alongside that of [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]] and [[Habib Abdoerrahman|Habib Abdurrahman]], belongs to the first phase of the *prang Gompeuni*—the war of the sultan, the *uleebalangs*, and the irregular chiefs. He epitomises the hereditary military aristocracy of Acheh, contrasting with later religious leaders (*teungkus* and ulamas) who, after the [[Dutch Concentration Scheme|concentration scheme]], became the principal organisers of resistance. His name persisted in the lineage of later panglimas of the XXII Moekims, and his career illustrates the complexity of Acehnese resistance, navigating between open opposition and strategic accommodation to colonial powers.
