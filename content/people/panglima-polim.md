**Panglima Polim** (also spelled *Panglima Polem*) was the hereditary chief (*panglima sagi*) of the [[XXII Mukims]], one of the three *sagis* (federations) surrounding the Achehnese capital, and the de facto ruler of [[Acheh]] in the years around [[1819]]. The title — literally "Lord Elder Brother" — was borne, as Van der Kemp records, "for many generations" by the chief of the XXII Mukims, who was accounted the foremost among the sagi chiefs. In the long-drawn [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] between [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Sultan Saif ul-Alum (Syf)]], Panglima Polim held the balance; his "undisguised supremacy at the capital" was acknowledged by all parties, and the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Commission]] of [[1819]] found him to be the actual master of Acheh.

## The Three Sagis and the hereditary chief

By the end of the seventeenth century the Achehnese sultanate had fallen, in effect, under the tutelage of the chiefs of the three *sagis* surrounding the capital — those of the [[XXII Mukims]], the [[XXV Mukims]] and the [[XXVI Mukims]]. The sultan's authority was exercised, as the Calcutta government wrote to London in its report of [[7 October]] [[1820]], "in name by the council of the Sagis, but in reality by Panglima Polim assisted by his adherents". Polim's title, "Lord Elder Brother", was the hereditary designation of the chief of the XXII Mukims; he was, by long tradition, the first among equals in the federation of the [[Three Sagis]].

## The 1819 Raffles–Coombs Commission

When the joint [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|commission of Raffles and Captain Coombs]] arrived off Acheh on [[14 March]] [[1819]], no sultan was present at the capital. The Commission sent a circular to the chiefs summoning them to an assembly; Polim replied from his XXII Mukims that the assembly would take place in eight days, and that until then the Commission was not to come ashore — "if it had anything to say, one would address oneself to the [[Shahbandar of Acheh|Shahbandar]]". The Bengal report describes Polim's conduct as a display of "his entire disregard for both the competitors and of his own undisguised supremacy at the capital". Polim eventually took up residence in the sultan's palace, leading the Bengal government to conclude that he alone was the real ruler, that [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] was not recognised at all, and that the pretender Syf was "merely a tool" in the hands of the parties — professed allegiance to him notwithstanding, Polim had "no inclination to acknowledge in him any real sovereignty".

## The warning of treason and Raffles's withdrawal

The day appointed for the assembly arrived; but a chief of the Jauhar party warned Raffles of treason. Raffles, who held the casting vote in the Commission, judged it wiser not to venture ashore and wrote that he was ill, so that the assembly could not proceed. The Calcutta government later disapproved of this conduct: although it did not believe the meeting would have borne fruit, "courtesy and decorum" required the Commissioners to attend a meeting they themselves had requested. Polim, with the assembly dissolved by Raffles's withdrawal, retained his mastery at Acheh; after seven fruitless weeks on the roadstead the Commission sailed for [[Pedir]] to treat with Jauhar Alum.

## Continued supremacy and the 1822 letter

Panglima Polim's supremacy at Acheh survived the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of Pedir]]. In a letter of [[15 April]] [[1822]] [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] told [[W. E. Phillips|Governor Phillips]] of Penang that he hoped "in two or three months" to have "a meeting with Pangleema Polim" and to re-establish "the laws of Government in some degree" — a striking acknowledgment by the treaty-sultan of the chief's continued local power. When, on [[4 December]] [[1822]], the [[Three Sagis]] wrote their celebrated and "brutal" letter to Governor Phillips refusing any longer to recognise Jauhar Alum and demanding the return of Syf, the letter was in effect an expression of Polim's policy. As late as the Bengal government's review of [[28 January]] [[1825]], the situation was the same: "the whole power at Acheen was in the hands of Panglima Polim".

## The Queen-Mother and Polim's later role

After [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]]'s death in November [[1823]], the [[Queen-Mother of Acheh|Queen-Mother]] with the three Sagis exercised power at Acheh — that is, in practice, with Polim. Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] noted that the present occupier of the throne was not the legitimate heir, and that a disputed succession would probably ensue on the legitimate son (then in the custody of the Queen-Mother) coming of age — an indication that Polim's faction had installed a nominee of their own. Throughout this period the British at [[Penang]] recognised that the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|treaty of 1819]] had remained a [[dead letter]] chiefly because Polim would not permit either sultan to rule.

## Account from *Boon, Van Lansberge* (1943) — Panglima Polim

**Panglima Polim** was an Acehnese chief who played a significant role in the resistance against Dutch rule during the [[Aceh War]]. He was the chief of the Sagi (region) of the [[XXII Mukims]] and a prominent figure in the conflict.

## Role in the Aceh War (1875-1879)

In the later phase of the [[Aceh War]], Panglima Polim emerged as one of the principal military leaders of Acehnese resistance. Named by [[J. L. J. H. Pel|General Pel]] in [[1875]] as one of the chiefs who led the war-party, he was still resisting the Dutch in [[August 1879]], when he fled to the upper lands of [[Pedir|Pedir]] rather than submit; his fortified chief place of [[Gleiêng|Gleiêng]] had been carried by the Dutch on [[9 July]] [[1879]] after a long and exhausting campaign.

### Office and Position (1870s)

In the political constitution of [[Acheh]] as described in the source work, the realm of Atjeh consisted of Atjeh proper — the northernmost triangle of Sumatra, divided into the three [[sagi|sagis]] of the [[XXV Moekims|XXV]], the [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI]] and the [[XXII Moekims|XXII]] moekims — and the [[Onderhoorigheden|Onderhoorigheden]] on the north, east and west coasts. The authority of the sultan was little more than nominal; the real powers were the [[Uleebalang|hoeloebalangs]] or hereditary chiefs, the most eminent being the panglimas of the three sagis, "among whom the panglima polim, the head of the XXII moekims, took the first place." The name is therefore a title as well as a personal designation.

### The War-Party, 1875

In his note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] to the Governor-General, [[J. L. J. H. Pel|General Pel]] described the war-party in terms that fixed the political sociology of the Acehnese resistance for the Dutch:

> "The war-party consists for the greater part of persons who live far enough from the theatre of war to be for the present not afraid that their wives, children and possessions will be attacked — men from the Sagi XXII, Kerkoen, Silang in the Sagi XXVI, and further persons from all regions, also from the dependent states, who take part in the war from religious zeal, or from a love of war, or because they have reason to consider themselves less safe in their own hearths. In general, therefore, from the most fanatical, bravest, worst, but most energetic part of the nation. That party forms, like an army, a fairly coherent whole, which is led by influential chiefs, as Panglima Polim, Imam Longbatta, Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang and others, who have the same motives for their actions as those above assigned to their subordinates, and to which, of course, the relatives of the former sultans, the Toewankoe's Haschim, Hoesin, Abdoel Madjid and others, have attached themselves, who rightly see that they have nothing more to expect from a foreign overrule."

### Resistance in 1879

After the [[Submission of Habib Abdurrahman (13 October 1878)|submission of Habib Abdurrahman on 13 October 1878]] and the beginning of the [[Campaign against the XXII Moekims (1879)|campaign against the XXII Moekims]] on [[23 March]] [[1879]], the capture of [[Indrapoeri|Indrapuri]] on [[24 March]] [[1879]] did not bring about the desired submission. "On the contrary, it was as if the struggle, under the leadership of our embittered enemies — Panglima Polim, Imam Longbatta and others — flared up with new strength." Heavy fighting followed at [[Djerreek|Djerreek]], [[Redep|Redep]], [[Panteh Karang|Panteh Karang]]; on [[6 May]] and [[7 May]] the Dutch suffered great losses at Panteh Karang, where all the officers engaged in the storming were put out of action one after another, and only on [[7 May]] was the fortress taken with the help of reinforcements.

On [[31 May]] the troops again advanced on [[Gleiêng|Gléiëng]], the fortified and well-armed chief place of Panglima Polim. "After a tiring march and repeated fights, this was at last reached and taken on [[9 July]] [1879]. With this the campaign in the XXII Moekims was over and the objective fully attained. The enemy's resistance was broken, and those who wished to continue the war had fled to the XXVI Moekims."

### Flight to the Pedir Upper Lands

After the [[Campaign against the XXVI Moekims (July 1879)|campaign against the XXVI Moekims]] of early July [[1879]] and the [[Selimoen Expedition (August 1879)|Selimoen expedition]] of August [[1879]], the principal hostile chiefs — [[Toewankoe Haschim|Toewankoe Haschim]], who still kept watch over the young pretender [[Toewankoe Daoed|Toewankoe Daoed]] (destined for the sultan's rank since [[1874]]), the [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]], Panglima Polim, his son [[Radja Koelala|Radja Koelala]], and others — persisted in their hostility, but their means were scattered and they had placed themselves beyond the reach of the Dutch troops, "namely at Kemala, in the interior south of Pedir."

## Legacy

Panglima Polim's resistance, like that of the [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]] and [[Habib Abdoerrahman|Habib Abdurrahman]], belongs to the first phase of the *prang Gompeuni* — the war of the sultan, the uleebalangs, and the irregular chiefs. His role in the Aceh War illustrates the complexity of the conflict, with local chiefs navigating between resistance and accommodation to Dutch rule.
