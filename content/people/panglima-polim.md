**Panglima Polim** — properly *Seri Madoo Perkasa Teukoe Panglima Polem*, also rendered *Panglima Palim* — was the very influential [[panglima]] and [[Uleebalang|oeléëbalang]] of the XXII Moekims, the great federation of the [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] interior. He was a brother-in-law of Toeankoe Radja Keumala, who was likewise of the Sultans-family; the two, with Toeankoe Machmoed (also of the former Sultans-family), were the three most prominent submitted chiefs of Atjeh in the years [[1907]]–[[1910]]. By his side Colijn — then a young lieutenant — appears in the famous photograph of the submission at [[Lho Seumawé]].

In Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* Polim is further identified as a senior [[Achehnese]] resistance war-leader of the [[XXII Moekims]] whose relations with [[Teuku Umar]] before and after the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March]] [[1896]] form one of the central strands of the narrative. As the dominant independent military chief in the XXII Moekims — outside the Dutch *geconcentreerde linie* and never previously reconciled with the colonial government — Polim was the natural ally whom Umar sought out once he had broken with the [[Dutch]], but their cooperation was conditional and transactional, reflecting the deep distrust that the XXII Moekims population had accumulated against Umar for the destructions he had inflicted on them while in Dutch service.

## The 1907 letter to the Tiro-oelama's

Before governor [[H.N.A. Swart|Swart]] set the destructive apparatus of lieutenant [[Schmidt]] in motion against the [[Tiro-oelama|Tiro-oelama's]], he wished to make one last attempt to bring the Tiro-tengkoe's to submission. He had the three most prominent chiefs of Atjeh write a letter advising the tengkoe's to give up further resistance. Those chiefs were: Toeankoe Machmoed, member of the former Sultans-family; Panglima Polim, the very influential head of the XXII Moekims; and his brother-in-law Toeankoe Radja Keumala, also of the Sultans-family. The [[Brief van 18 Radjab 1325|brief]], properly sealed, was brought by a prominent Atjeher who also had the confidence of the Tiro-oelama's, to their schuilplaats in the forest. It opens:

> *"Assalmoé 'alaikoem warahmatoellahi Ta'ala wabarakatoeh! With a thousand greetings from us, Toeankoe Machmoed, Toeankoe Radja Keumala and Seri Madoo Perkasa Teukoe Panglima Polem, now residing at Koetaradja, Groot-Atjeh, to our Penghoeloe (Lord) [[Habib Teupin Wan|Habib Abdoerrachman Teupin Wan]], and our Teachers Teungkoe Mahjoeddin and [[Tengkoe di Boekit|Teungkoe di Boekit]], son of Moekarram Maulana Almoedabbar Almalik Teungkoe di Tiro, Teungkoe Hasjim, Teungkoe Oeleë Toetoeë, Tengkoe Ibrahim, as well as to their followers who still live as survivors of those who took part in the holy war (perang-sabil) in the great forest-regions."*

The three chiefs recount that they had been able to perform the *hadj* four times, had seen the prevailing poverty among the Islamic race settled there, and that the world now nears its end. They had fought against the Kompeuni Belanda and, despite all efforts, had had to acknowledge defeat — and so they had to submit to the Dutch authority. They cite the precedent of other Islamic powers — *Negeri Hindi* (British India) to the English Kompeuni, *Magribi* (Morocco) to the French Kompeuni, *Mesri* (Egypt) allied with the English — and ask the Tiro-mannen to follow their example, surrendering no weapon. The brief was dated at Koetaradja, Kampoeng Keudah, on the 18th of Radjab 1325. It actually reached [[Tengkoe Majet di Tiro|Tgk. Tjhi Majet]], and it is known that he discussed this advice of the three submitted chiefs with the other Tiro-mannen. None was willing to give up the fight; they would fight to the end, and with them would be done according to Allah's will.

## Identification of Tgk. Tjot Plieëng

When [[Tengkoe Tjot Plieëng|Tgk. Tjot Plieëng]] — the holiest and highest of the strijdbare geestelijke hoofden — was killed accidentally by a patrol of lieutenant Terwogt, the body was brought to a bivak for identification. After a few days it still showed no sign of decomposition; even Panglima Polim was called in for the identification, and he bent over the corpse that did not stink, in the midst of a group of Atjehers dumb with awe at this proof of the grace of Allah. *"This was God's secret,"* said the old Polem at Zentgraff's last meeting with him.

## Background and Position in the XXII Moekims (per *ATJEH '96*)

The [[XXII Moekims]] formed the western territorial division of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and had been a bastion of resistance to the Dutch since the [[1873]] conquest of the [[Kraton of Koeta Radja|kraton]]. The region had produced religious-war leaders of the stature of [[Teungkoe di Tiro]] and had only intermittently come under effective Dutch influence; [[Ana' Galong]] — described by Kruisheer as the post "considered by the Acehnese as the heart of Great Aceh" — had been the former main residence of Teungkoe di Tiro before its conquest by Umar's legion on [[30 November]] [[1893]]. Polim inherited this tradition of resistance and, in the years before Umar's defection, operated as an autonomous *panglima* in the XXII Moekims while Umar was still serving the Dutch. According to intelligence reaching the colonial administration, Polim set as an explicit condition for any future cooperation with Umar that "all damage Oemar caused while serving the infidel/Dutch be compensated to the XXII Moekims population" — a demand which both reflected the depth of grievance against Umar and signalled Polim's determination to drive a hard bargain before committing his forces.

## Role in the Spring of 1896 (per *ATJEH '96*)

Once Umar defected on [[29 March]] [[1896]], Polim was the obvious counterpart for a coalition. Kruisheer records that Umar "now sought cooperation with Panglima Polim" in the immediate aftermath of the defection, and that Polim sent a letter to [[Teungkoe Sjech Tjot Poetoe]] attempting to persuade him to defect as well, reporting that he himself was on his way to [[Lampisang]]. The most striking evidence of Polim's military independence comes from the aftermath of the [[Battle of Senelop (17 April 1896)|battle of Senelop]] on [[17 April]] [[1896]], in which a Dutch column under [[J. B. van Heutsz|Lt.-Col. Van Heutsz]] lost a 7 cm bronze field gun and a *mitrailleur* (machine gun) in the confusion of the retreat from [[Lam Bada]]. Polim "reportedly brought the Dutch gun lost on 17 April into Senelop and had set up that post for himself", turning what had been a Dutch *buitenpost* into his personal fortified base. This was an exceptional propaganda and tactical victory: the recapture of a Dutch artillery piece, displayed in a former Dutch post, was a powerful symbol that the colonial *buitenlinie* had truly collapsed.

## Significance

The submission of Panglima Polim at Lho Seumawé, with Colijn at his side, is one of the emblematic images of the close of the great phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. After his submission he became the most prominent of the great chiefs through whom the Dutch bestuur sought to consolidate its authority; the letter of [[1907]] shows the use the bestuur made of his prestige. His identification of the body of Tgk. Tjot Plieëng is one of the small but revealing episodes by which the great Panglima steps out of the official record into the human one.

Panglima Polim's significance in *ATJEH '96* lies in his embodiment of the autonomous, un-reconciled Achehnese resistance — the class of chiefs who had never accepted Dutch authority and who therefore welcomed Umar's defection but did not subordinate themselves to him. His demand for compensation for Umar's earlier devastations is a unique documented case of an Achehnese leader setting financial conditions on cooperation with the renegade Dutch *Panglima Prang Besar*, and it foreshadows the continuing fragmentation of the Achehnese side even as the Dutch counter-offensive of May–June [[1896]] pressed Umar back toward [[Lepong]]. Polim's capture of the Senelop gun, meanwhile, ranks among the most damaging Achehnese tactical successes of the first semester of [[1896]] and one of the most politically resonant images of the entire campaign.

## Account from *De Atjeh-Oorlog* by J.A. Kruijt (1896)

Kruijt's *De Atjeh-Oorlog* presents Panglima Polim as one of the two principal leaders of the Atjehese resistance in the middle phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] — the principal military *panglima*, as opposed to the religious leader [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]].

In [[April 1878|April]] [[1878]], when the Dutch posts in [[Simpang Olim|Simpang Olim]] were withdrawn, the garrison at [[Edi|Edi]] reduced, and the *benting* of the beach moved further inland, a period of unrest broke out on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] after years of calm. Kruijt identifies Panglima Polim as the instigator: enemies from the [[Noordkust|North Coast]] attacked Edi. They were driven off in [[May 1878|May]] [[1878]], and as most of them came from [[Gedong|Gedong]], a statelet in [[Pasei|Pasei]], an expedition thither took place in [[June 1878|June]] [[1878]].

Under the governorship of [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]] ([[1886]]–[[1891]]), Panglima Polim — together with [[Toeankoe Hasschim|Toeankoe Hasschim]] — who were little pleased with the ever-increasing influence of [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] and therewith of the religious party, made himself heard from the side of the Kemala-party. Given the existing tension, Tengkoe di Tirou — fearful of eventually being caught behind the net — in [[1889]] offered to bring the Sultan to the Dutch. The Dutch did not accept the offer.

When in [[August 1888|August]] [[1888]] the Sultan suddenly appeared in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and met with Panglima Polim, [[Imam Longbattah|Imam Longbattah]], and Tengkoe di Tirou, Van Teijn cherished a justified hope that it would now come to a conclusion; but after three weeks the Sultan departed again for [[Kemala|Kemala]], the chiefs brought him gifts, but the Dutch heard nothing from him.

In [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] Tengkoe di Tirou and Panglima Polim died suddenly. This too was an occasion for the Kemala-party to await the consequences of the impending change of administration. In [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]] Van Teijn, disappointed in his expectations, departed owing to increasing indisposition. Their simultaneous deaths removed the two principal addressees of the Dutch conciliation policy and marked the transition to the more fragmented resistance of the [[1890]]s.

## Account from *Onze vestiging in Atjeh* (Borel, 1878)

**Panglima Polim** was one of the principal hostile Atjehneesche leaders of the [[Atjeh (Borel account)|Atjeh-oorlog]], residing at [[Longbatta]] — the kampong that was the meeting point of the [[XXII Moekims]] and [[XXVI Moekims]] and the key to the enemy's line of investment around [[Kota Radja]]. He shared the residence with the [[Imam of Longbatta]] and other hostile chiefs, and his name is, in [[G. F. W. Borel|Borel]]'s narrative, attached to the most stubborn centre of Atjehneesche resistance in the *stelling-Pel* period.

## Longbatta as a Centre of Resistance

Longbatta was a *zeer belangrijke kampong*, long the residence of Panglima Polim, the Imam of Longbatta, and other hostile chiefs. It had been briefly in Dutch hands on [[24 January]] [[1874]] — when [[Generaal Verspijck|Verspijck]]'s *omtrekkende beweging* had taken the Kraton — and was then immediately abandoned. By December [[1874]] it had become the key to the enemy's line of investment around Kota Radja. Pel's plan for [[31 December]] [[1874]] was a converging movement against Longbatta: the left column under [[Majoor van der Meer|van der Meer]] (10th battalion) to march from [[Pakan Atjeh]] along the left bank directly on Longbatta; the right column under [[Majoor Romswinckel|Romswinckel]]'s 5th battalion (under [[Kolonel Wiegand|overste Wiegand]]'s overall command) to make a feint attack east of Kota Radja. The fortified [[Longbatta-Mesigit|Mesigit]] (stone mosque) was taken without a shot on the morning of [[31 December]] [[1874]], and Longbatta was entered.

## The Longbatta Disaster and Its Aftermath

The same afternoon, however, the [[Battle of Longbatta (31 December 1874)|disastrous retreat of the colonne-van der Meer]] turned the victory into a reverse. Van der Meer misinterpreted Pel's orders, abandoned the captured Mesigit *benteng* without leaving a garrison, and marched his whole force over the open, marshy *sawah*. The Atjehneezen, who had streamed back in, occupied the abandoned *benteng* and cut off the column's retreat. Only [[Majoor van Zijll de Jong|van Zijll de Jong]]'s heroic counter-attack with some sixty infantry and a four-gun battery saved the *colonne-van der Meer* from annihilation. The Longbatta-Mesigit remained in Dutch hands, and a post was *opgericht*. From the Mesigit, Pel's *linie* was gradually extended: [[Blang Tjoet]] was conquered on [[18 January]] [[1875]], the *surprise of Lamara Olejlo* ([[14 February|14]]–[[15 February]] [[1875]]) was carried out by [[Kapitein von Ende|kapitein von Ende]] from nearby [[Mandarsa Poeti]], and the [[Night attack on Longbatta-Mesigit (22 November 1875)|night attack on the Mesigit]] of [[22 November]] [[1875]] was repulsed. The post became the *tophoek* (apex) of the *stelling-Pel*, six thousand metres into enemy territory.

## Significance

Panglima Polim's residence at Longbatta fixed it as the apex of the *stelling-Pel* and the eastern pivot of the Dutch line. The repeated Dutch efforts to take and hold Longbatta — the [[Battle of Longbatta (31 December 1874)|disaster of 31 December 1874]], the [[Night attack on Longbatta-Mesigit (22 November 1875)|night attack of 22 November 1875]], the [[Pel's offensive against the VI, IV and IX Moekims (1875–1876)|great offensive of December 1875 – February 1876]] — were, in Borel's reading, the consequence of van Swieten's original abandonment of the kampong in January [[1874]]. Polim and the Imam of Longbatta are, in Borel's narrative, the symbols of the resistance that the *afwachtende houding* had allowed to harden.
