The **Shahbandar of Acheh** (also written *Sjahbandar*) was the harbour master of [[Acheh]] — the chief port official of the Achehnese capital, and the customary channel of communication between foreign merchants and the Achehnese chiefs. In the confused politics of the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] he played a double role: as the official intermediary of the [[Three Sagis]] he was the person to whom the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Commission]] of [[1819]] was told to address itself, and he was also the assassin of [[Tunku Pakih]], the powerful chief of [[Pedir]] and protector of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]].

## The Commission's intermediary (March 1819)

When, on [[14 March]] [[1819]], the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|joint Commission of Raffles and Coombs]] anchored before the Achehnese capital, no sultan was present. The Commission sent a circular to the chiefs summoning them to an assembly. [[Panglima Polim]] replied from his [[XXII Mukims]] that the assembly would take place in eight days, but that until then the Commission was not to come ashore; "if it had anything to say, one would address oneself to the Shahbandar". The Shahbandar thus appears in the British records as the standing official through whom the Achehnese chiefs chose to transact with foreigners; his role was that of the customary *shahbandar* of Malay port-polities, with the difference that, at Acheh, the central authority was so fragmented that the office had become an instrument of the [[Three Sagis]] rather than of a sultan.

## The mission to Pedir and the murder of Tunku Pakih

The Shahbandar's most consequential action belongs to the period immediately after the Commission had concluded its seven-week sojourn at Acheh and was preparing to sail for Pedir. The British government's report of [[7 October]] [[1820]] recorded that, before any negotiation could be opened with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]], an apology had to be received from him for past insults against the British flag — one of which, as recounted on page 161 of the article, was the [[Capture of British brig by Tunku Pakih|capture of a British brig by Tunku Pakih]] in 1817–18. To carry this apology and to offer the submission of the three Sagis, the Shahbandar of Acheh proceeded to Pedir at the head of a commission.

The mission was a feint. As Van der Kemp narrates:

> "The shahbandar of Acheh had proceeded with a commission to Pedir, as if to ask Sultan Djohor's forgiveness and to offer him his submission on behalf of the three Sagis, but in truth with the aim of deceiving the prince. When he found himself hindered in this design by Packie, he stabbed him to death."

The [[Death of Tunku Pakih|death of Tunku Pakih]] removed at a stroke the principal support of Jauhar Alum's party at Pedir and the chief obstacle to the Shahbandar's intrigue. Van der Kemp cautions that "the truth of this representation may perhaps be doubted; in any case Packie was no more". The Asiatic Journal, which Van der Kemp cites, gives the same account. With Tunku Pakih's removal, the path was smoothed for the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] between Raffles and Jauhar Alum.

## Place in the wider narrative

The Shahbandar's double role — official intermediary and factional assassin — captures the character of Achehnese politics in 1819: the formal port-officials of the sultanate had become instruments of the [[Three Sagis]] and of [[Panglima Polim]], who together treated both rival sultans as mere tools. The Shahbandar's mission to Pedir was one of the immediate preliminaries to the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]], and his murder of [[Tunku Pakih]] one of the decisive local events that made the treaty possible.
