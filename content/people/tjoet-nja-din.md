**Tjoet Nja Din** — modern *Cut Nyak Dhien*, also written *Tjoet Nyak Dhien* — was the third wife of [[Teukoe Oemar]] and, in Zentgraff's phrase, *een der grootste vrouwenfiguren* (one of the greatest female figures) of the time of action in [[1896]] and the years that followed. Her brother was T. Rajeu Nanta. After the fall of her husband near [[Meulaboh]] in [[February]] [[1899]] she chose the life of the forests over submission, and held on even as her following gradually melted away. In Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* she is further identified as an [[Achehnese]] noblewoman, the daughter of [[Toekoe Nanta Tocha]] of the [[VI Moekims]] and one of the wives of [[Teuku Umar]], who figures as the most important female agitator behind Umar's defection from the [[Dutch]] colonial government on [[29 March]] [[1896]]. Kruisheer identifies her unambiguously as "one of the Dutch government's greatest opponents" who "continuously urged Oemar to resist the government, *just as zij zelve dat later nog lang volhield*" (as she herself continued to do for a long time afterward).

## Six years in the forests

She was old and blind, but nothing could break her spirit. She suffered hunger in the forests, and the patrols hunted her from one schuilplaats to another. There were weeks in which she did not see a mouthful of rice and had to feed herself on the infusion (*afkooksel*) of the stem of the wild banana. About six years this woman held out, she who in the days of her glory had stood beside the highest women of the land; and one may guess at the size of the sacrifice she made for her national idea.

## The betrayal and capture

The misery of this wandering blind old woman became at last too much even for her own countrymen, and one of the chiefs came to [[Veltman]] with the offer to point out her schuilplaats on condition that he guaranteed her personal safety, with the proper treatment due to a woman of her standing. Veltman accepted this, and found her schuilplaats. Tjoet Nja Din, powerless through her blindness, emaciated by misery and privation, was so desperate at falling into the hands of the *kaphé*'s that, groping in the right direction, she stabbed with her *rentjong* at the Atjeher who had betrayed her hiding-place. His good intentions she did not understand; it was only clear to her that she was in the hands of the unbelievers.

## Family Background and Marriage to Teuku Umar (per *ATJEH '96*)

Tjoet Nja Din belonged to the *uleëbalang* aristocracy of the [[VI Moekims]], the western *landschap* of [[Groot-Atjeh]] which contained [[Lampoe's Oe]] and the historic post of [[Ana' Galong]]. Her father, [[Toekoe Nanta Tocha]], is named by Kruisheer only in passing, but the family's standing was sufficiently high that her marriage to [[Teuku Umar]] — who was himself, by [[1896]], the *hoeloebalang* of [[Lepong]] and [[Panglima Prang Besar]] of the Government — consolidated his influence in the VI Moekims and tied his personal fortunes to the western districts which would become the principal theatre of the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|Dutch counter-offensive of May 1896]]. Her aristocratic status gave weight to the prophecies which circulated around her in early [[1896]]; at the time of the defection she appeared pregnant, though this was "later proved not to be" the case, and her advisors foretold that she would bear a son who would one day hold a high position in the Acehnese kingdom — provided her husband broke his ties with the Dutch-Indian government as soon as possible.

## Role in the Defection of 29 March 1896 (per *ATJEH '96*)

Kruisheer's account places Tjoet Nja Din at the very centre of the defection. The [[Ketjihik Oemar]], a long-standing Dutch informant "*sedert jaren op onze hand*" (on our side for years) who had served as guide to [[Generaal Pel]] during his [[1876]] tours of the IV and VI Moekims, "belonged to the immediate circle of Toekoe Oemar and could know of his intentions" and reported that Umar and his wife Tjoet Nja Din "were holding busy meetings with the heads of the clergy" — that is, with the *oelama* (religious scholars) network of [[Teungkoe Koeta Karang]] and, after his death on [[8 December]] [[1895]], [[Teungkoe Tanah Abée]]. Her activity in the councils leading up to the defection is thus presented as a co-equal catalyst with Umar's own grievances (the alleged insults by the [[Controleur te Oleh-leh]] and the *hoofddjaksa* [[Mohamad Arief]], and his disappointment at being denied a Royal decoration), and her sustained pressure on her husband is what the text credits with converting what might have been a wavering into a definitive break. Her continued resistance "for a long time afterward" is mentioned without further detail in Kruisheer's narrative, which closes at [[28 June]] [[1896]] — but the phrase clearly foreshadows the long post-1896 resistance for which she would become famous.

## Significance

Her hatred of the *kaphé* she carried to the end. Zentgraff pairs her with the wife of [[Tengkoe di Barat]] as the two supreme instances of Atjehsche feminine heroism: the widow who would not accept quarter from an unbeliever's hand, and the wife who put herself before her husband to receive the bullet meant for him. The figure of Tjoet Nja Din, blind, emaciated, striking out in the dark with her rentjong at the man who had bought her life, closes the great age of the Westkust resistance — and opens the long later history of her legend.

Though Kruisheer devotes only a few sentences to Tjoet Nja Din, her role is presented as decisive: she is the figure who kept Umar committed to the path of defection when the political and military odds still favoured continued alliance with the Dutch, and she is the only woman in *ATJEH '96* given direct political agency. Her combination of aristocratic standing, religious credibility through the *oelama* network, and family ties to the VI Moekims made her a unique force within the resistance, and her survival beyond Umar's own death in [[1899]] would carry her resistance forward into the next decade. The brief portrait in *ATJEH '96* thus captures her at the moment of her historical emergence.

## Account in Hooijer's *De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh* ([[1897]])

**Tjoet Nja Din** — written by [[G. B. Hooijer]] as *Potjoet Nja Din* — was the Achehnese noblewoman of the [[IV Moekims]] and [[VI Moekims]] whose marriage to [[Teukoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] was named by Hooijer as one of the chief foundations of his rise from the head of a crossroads settlement to an influential and feared figure in Achehnese society.

## Marriage and influence

Hooijer wrote that Toekoe Oemar had climbed *van pertigganeershoofd van deftige familie* to become an influential and feared person in Achehnese society by virtue of his marriages with the daughters of several great families on the [[Westkust|West Coast]] and in [[Atjeh]] — *onder anderen met de, in de IV en VI Moekims zoo machtige Potjoet Nja Din*, "among others with Potjoet Nja Din, so powerful in the IV and VI Moekims" — together with his energetic character, his cunning and cultivation, his enterprise and his liberality.

## Significance (Hooijer's account)

Hooijer's brief notice locates the source of Oemar's authority in affinal alliance with the established nobility of the western *moekims* rather than in inherited rank, and thus explains why Dutch policy in [[Groot-Atjeh]] came to depend on a single individual whose standing rested on kinship networks the colonial administration neither controlled nor fully understood.
