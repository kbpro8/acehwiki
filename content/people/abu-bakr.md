**Abu Bakr** (also written, by an obvious clerical error, **Abu Bahr**) was the first of the four Rashidūn caliphs of Islam, the close companion of the Prophet [[Muhammad]], and the inaugural Caliph of the nascent Muslim community. He is named alongside [[Omar]], [[Uthman]] and [[Ali]] in the preamble of the Achehnese sultans’ letters patent (*sarakata*), and his name also appears in the Malay translation of the *Burda* where a passage recounts his refuge with the Prophet in a cave, symbolising divine protection.

## Early Islamic Role
Abu Bakr supported the Prophet during the early struggles of the Muslim community, notably the battles of Badr and Uhud, and was revered for his piety, steadfastness, and leadership. After the Prophet’s death he was elected the first Caliph, a decision that cemented the principle of *shūrā* (consultation) in the early Islamic polity.

## Veneration in the Aceh Sultanate
In the formal diplomatic language of the Achehnese sultanate, the sultans invoked the blessing of Allah, the Prophet, and the four companions in their official documents. The invocation appears in the [[Letters patent with the ninefold seal to Panglima Meuseugit Raya|letters patent granted by Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan]] to [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] on [[4 Rabi' al-Awwal]] [[1307]] H ([[1889]]). The text reads:

> “with the help of Allah the omniscient King, and with the blessing of the Prophet, the Lord of Men, and of his four companions, which be Abu Bahr, Omar, Uthman and Ali (may they be pleasing unto Allah).”

The same document also invokes the saint [[Abdul-Qadir Jilani]] and “all the devout *walis* of God from East to West”. The placement of the four companions immediately after the Prophet marks their elevated status in the Achehnese hierarchy of religious intercession. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes, the spelling “Abu Bahr” is a scribal error arising from the mixture of Malay and imperfect Arabic used by late‑nineteenth‑century officials.

The veneration of Abu Bakr and the other three caliphs is universal among Sunni Muslims; the Achehnese, who adhered strictly to the Shafiʿī school, displayed this universal reverence in their official edicts to signal alignment with the broader Muslim ummah and the historic succession of the caliphate.

## Appearance in the Malay *Burda*
The Malay translation of the *Burda* (the celebrated poem of praise for the Prophet) contains a passage that recounts Abu Bakr’s refuge with the Prophet in a cave. The episode highlights Abu Bakr’s steadfastness and the Prophet’s role as a shelter for believers. The translation, preserved in a nineteenth‑century manuscript (see [[20260707_232246_DLP-54-Een_16de_eeuwse_Maleise_vertaling_van_de_Burda_van_al_Bu_s_i_ri_OCR.txt]], pp. 38‑39), uses the name “Abu Bakr” without the “Bahr” variant, confirming the correct identification of the companion in Malay literary tradition.

## Significance
The dual presence of Abu Bakr’s name in both the religious literature of the Malay world (the *Burda*) and the formal diplomatic formulae of the Achehnese sultanate illustrates the intertwining of devotional piety and political legitimacy in Southeast Asian Islam. By invoking the Rashidūn caliphs, Achehnese sultans anchored their authority in the universal Sunni tradition while simultaneously adapting Arabic‑derived formulas to local linguistic capacities.
