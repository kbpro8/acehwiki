**Abdurra'uf of Singkel** (Arabic: Abd al-Raʾūf al-Sinkīlī; Dutch: Abdoerraoef van Singkel; in Atjèhnese religious tradition: **Teungkoe di Koeala**) was the most celebrated Atjèhnese saint and scholar of the seventeenth century, the introducer of the [[Sjattarijjah|Sjattarijjah]] (Shattariyya) *tarikat* (mystical order) into Atjèh and the Malay world, and author of the first full Malay translation of the Qur'an. His grave at the mouth of the [[Atjèh River|Atjèh river]] (Koela Atjèh, or "mouth of the Atjèh river") is the oldest pilgrimage site in [[Aceh|Atjèh]] — later superseded in popular devotion only by the grave of [[Teungku Anjong|Teungkoe Andjöng]].

## Account from *Atjèh* by J. Kreemer (Deel II)

[[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]] records Abdoerraoef van Singkel as a central figure in the popular religious tradition of Atjèh. The folk tradition (*volksgezegde*) describes him as the source of all *hoekom* (religious law) in Atjèh — "al het hoekom dat men thans in Atjèh heeft, is afkomstig van Abdoerraoef van Singkel" ("all the *hoekom* (religious law) that one now has in Atjèh, originated from Abdoerraoef van Singkel").

His grave lies at the mouth of the Atjèh river — at **Koela** (Koeala), a landschapje then attached to the XXV Moekim's, later to the XII Moekim's. This location gave him the honorific **Teungkoe di Koeala** ("Teungkoe at the river-mouth"). Kreemer notes that this was formerly *the* foremost pilgrimage destination (*bedevaartsoord*) in Atjèh — before it was eclipsed by [[Teungku Anjong|Teungkoe Andjöng]]'s grave at Gampöng Djawa.

**The giant ray legend**: Kreemer records a widespread popular legend about Abdoerraoef: that when he first arrived in Atjèh, he and his companion **Sanoedi** sailed across the sea on the back of a giant ray (*pari*). This legend is told of several other saints in the Malay world and illustrates the popular hagiographic tradition in which Abdoerraoef was embedded.

## The Sjattarijjah tarikat

Abdoerraoef introduced the **[[Sjattarijjah|Sjattarijjah]] *tarikat*** into Atjèh, and through Atjèh into the broader Malay world. The Sjattarijjah was and remains the dominant *tarikat* in Atjèh, but Kreemer records that other *tarikat*'s also became established:
- **Naqsjibandija** (*Naqshbandiyya*)
- **Kadriah** (*Qadiriyya*)
- **Sammanïja** (*Sammaniyya*)

The Sammanïja is particularly noted by Kreemer as having spread through Atjèh in the nineteenth century, introduced by Arab or Hadhrami teachers.

## The spiritual genealogy

In the spiritual genealogy (*silsila*) of the Sjattarijjah, Abdurra'uf is described as being "of the tribe of Hamzah Pansuri" (*van de stam van Hamzah Pansoerï*) — a formulaic expression of his place in the chain of mystical transmission — despite Abdurra'uf's own Shafi'ite orthodoxy and his opposition to the pantheistic mysticism of [[Hamzah Pansuri|Hamzah Pansoerï]] and [[Sjamsoeddin van Pasè|Sjamsoeddin van Pasè]].

## Significance

Abdurra'uf of Singkel stands at the junction of two great transformations in Atjèhnese religious history: the end of the heterodox mysticism promoted by [[Sultan Iskandar Moeda|Iskandar Moeda]] and the establishment of the orthodox Shafi'ite-Sjattarijjah tradition that would characterise Atjèhnese Islam to the present day. Kreemer describes him as the religious founder of the tradition in which [[Teungkoe Tjheh Ibrahim|Teungkoe Tjhèh Ibrahim]] — Kreemer's own principal informant — was still working in [[1922]].
