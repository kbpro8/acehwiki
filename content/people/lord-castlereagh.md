**Lord Castlereagh** (1769–1822), born Robert Stewart, styled Viscount Castlereagh from 1796 and later 2nd Marquess of Londonderry, was a British statesman who served as **Foreign Secretary** from 1812 until his death in 1822. As the cabinet minister chiefly responsible for the conduct of British foreign policy in the years immediately following the Napoleonic Wars, he played a direct part in the negotiations that culminated in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] and in the diplomatic exchanges which preceded them.

## Role in the Anglo-Dutch Colonial Negotiations

In the correspondence and memoranda reviewed by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] (1900), Castlereagh appears as the British minister with whom the Dutch plenipotentiaries dealt during the preliminary exchanges of [[1820]]. Notes were exchanged between Castlereagh and [[George Canning|Canning]] on the British side, and the Dutch envoys [[Fagel-Elout negotiations (August 1820)|Fagel and Elout]] on the other, on 4 August [[1820]], in which the declaration was already set forth that Britain would not oppose Dutch arrangements in the archipelago provided Dutch policy did not clash with British commercial interests. As Van der Kemp noted, this declaration was "not new" in [[1824]], since it had already been put on paper in the Castlereagh–Canning/Fagel–Elout exchanges of [[1820]], though at that time the parties had failed to reach a formal treaty and the documents had been left in suspension.

Castlereagh's name recurs in connection with the Dutch envoy Fagel's reports to the Netherlands foreign minister [[Van Nagell]]. In January [[1820]] an exchange took place in which Castlereagh was said to have communicated to Fagel that the Netherlands ought to be prepared to make certain sacrifices in order to satisfy opposition sentiment in England. The Dutch minister Van Nagell was deeply indignant at this communication; Castlereagh afterwards denied having made the statement, but Fagel maintained the truth of his report. The episode, preserved among the documents printed by Van der Kemp, illustrates the mistrust that ran beneath the surface of Anglo-Dutch relations during the negotiations that eventually produced the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]].

## Historical Significance

Castlereagh's conduct of British foreign policy was marked by the congress system of the post-1815 settlement and by a general preference for stability and balance among the European powers. In colonial affairs his approach was pragmatic: he was prepared to acknowledge the Dutch position in the East Indies while safeguarding the freedom of British commerce, an attitude that set the parameters within which the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] was finally negotiated. Although Castlereagh died by suicide in August [[1822]], before the treaty was signed, the diplomatic groundwork laid under his direction continued to shape the negotiations carried through by his successor [[George Canning|Canning]] and by the Dutch plenipotentiaries Fagel and Elout.

For the [[Acheh]] question specifically, Castlereagh's significance lies in the fact that the diplomatic exchanges of [[1820]] — which produced no formal treaty but established the language of mutual non-interference later invoked in [[1824]] — took place under his authority. The eventual abandonment of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] concluded by [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] was thus prepared during Castlereagh's tenure at the Foreign Office.
