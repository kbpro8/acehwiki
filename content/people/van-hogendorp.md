**Van Hogendorp** was a nineteenth-century Dutch author whose collected essays, *Bijdragen* ("Contributions"), preserved — in its second edition, volume V, page 236 — one of the principal printed texts of the exchanged notes accompanying the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]]. Van der Kemp refers to the work as *Van Hogendorp's Bijdragen*.

## Cited by Van der Kemp

Van der Kemp cites Van Hogendorp's *Bijdragen* in a footnote on page 45 of his article on [[Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819|Raffles's Acheh agreement of 1819]]. The note lists the printed sources in which the diplomatic correspondence exchanged between the British and Dutch plenipotentiaries at the time of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 treaty]] is to be found, "partly with, partly without the treaty of 1824". Van Hogendorp's *Bijdragen* (2nd ed., vol. V, p. 236) stands at the head of a series that includes Elout's *Bijdragen* of 1863 (p. 228), E. de Waal's *Nederlandsch-Indië in de Staten-Generaal* (vol. I, p. 82), [[J. E. de Sturler|De Sturler]]'s *Grondgebied van Nederlandsch-Indië* (p. 344) and J. J. Meinsma's *Geschiedenis van de Nederlandsch Oost-Indische Bezittingen*.

The point of the enumeration is to give the reader a key to the documents that, alongside the treaty itself, regulated Anglo-Dutch relations in the archipelago for the half-century that followed. Van Hogendorp's compilation holds the leading place in the list, suggesting that Van der Kemp regarded it as the earliest and most authoritative printed source of the exchanged notes.

## Significance for Acheh studies

The exchanged notes that Van Hogendorp helped to preserve are of central importance for the Acheh question, because they contain the famous Acheh reservation. Under Article 2 of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 treaty]] Britain undertook not to establish any office on Sumatra or to conclude any treaty with the native princes of that island; the Dutch, in the exchanged notes, promised in turn that this stipulation would not be construed so as to affect the independence of [[Acheh]]. It was this reservation that became the legal basis for the long British veto on Dutch expansion over the sultanate — a veto that the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra Treaty of 1871]] finally lifted and that [[Lord Palmerston]] had famously exploited, "adhering simply to the letter of the treaty of 1824".

Van Hogendorp's role in the present article is therefore that of a documentary source. As one of the editors whose compilations Van der Kemp cites, he shares the stage with [[J. E. de Sturler|De Sturler]], [[H. D. Levyssohn Norman|Levyssohn Norman]], [[Hoek]], [[L. W. G. N. Netscher|Netscher]] and [[L. J. P. J. Jeekel|Jeekel]] — the network of Dutch colonial historians on whose editorial labours Van der Kemp's own researches were built.
