**Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri** was an Acehnese *Mohammedaans wetgeleerde* (Islamic jurist) and the patriarch of the family of religious resisters known to the [[K.N.I.L.]] as the *Tiriō Teungkoes* — the four sons and grandson whose band, operating out of the bergland ten Oosten of [[Tangsé]] against the colonne of luitenant [[Schmidt]] in [[1910]]-[[1911]], was the last organised armed resistance in [[Atjeh|Aceh]]. He was the father of four sons — the eldest (and his successor) fell at the [[Battle of Aneu Galong (1896)|defence of Aneu Galong]] in [[1896]] as commandant; *[[Tgk. Lambada]]* was killed in a gevecht with a patrouille under *[[Luitenant Darlang]]*; *[[Tgk. di Boekét]]* and *[[Tgk. Tjhi Majét]]* lived on as the two voornaamste Teungkoes of the resistance band. Saman di Tiri held the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Aceh War]] to be a true *[[prang sabi]]* — a genuine holy war — and was followed in that conviction by his volgelingen and most of the bevolking.

## The four sons

"Van de vier zoons van Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri was de oudste, tevens de opvolger, in [[1896]] gesneuveld als commandant bij de verdediging [[Battle of Aneu Galong (1896)|Aneu Galong]]." The second, *[[Tgk. Lambada]]*, was killed in a gevecht with a patrouille under the luitenant [[Luitenant Darlang|Darlang]]. The third and fourth, *[[Tgk. di Boekét]]* and *[[Tgk. Tjhi Majét]]*, lived on as the two voornaamste Teungkoes of the bende. The fifth generation — *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]*, son of the *[[Tgk. Mat Amin]]* who had fallen at Aneu Galong — was an infant when his father fell; in [[1911]] he was fifteen, and would lead the last stage of the resistance.

## The doctrine of prang sabi

"Tgk. Sjech Saman di Tiri, was wel van die mening geweest en al zijn volgelingen, benevens het grootste deel van de bevolking, deelden die overtuiging ten volle." The opinion — that the Aceh War was a true holy war — is one the book treats with respect even while noting the doctrinal difficulty: "Het is wel volgens de Koran aan te tonen, dat er in de hele Atjeh-oorlog geen sprake is geweest van een werkelijke heilige oorlog." The distinction the book draws is between the doctrinal question of whether the war as a whole qualified as *prang sabi* and the lived conviction of the Tiriō family and their followers that it did. For Saman di Tiri, the colonial war in Aceh was a religious war in which a Muslim who fell became *[[sjahid]]* — a geloofsmartelaar — and went to the paradijs.

This conviction is what made the Tiriō bende so hard to break. When, at [[Generaal Swart]]'s request, the three voornaamste Atjehse hoofden — two *[[Toeankoe Machmoed Radja Keumala|Sultans-family members]]* and *[[Panglima Prang Polim]]* — wrote urging submission, the Teungkoes "bespraken de brief, doch besloten tot het einde te vechten; hen zou geschieden naar Allah's wil." And when the widow of *[[Teungkoe Asan Oeleë Toetooë]]* went into the bergen to urge the young *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* to submit, the boy refused: "Zijn Vader was als geloofsgetuige gevallen, hij zou in diens voetstappen treden. Allah's wil zou geschieden."

## Significance

Saman di Tiri is the patriarch of the last Acehnese resistance. The doctrine of *prang sabi* he held — and the family structure of religious authority he bequeathed to his sons and grandson — is what gave the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]] campaign of [[1910]]-[[1911]] its particular difficulty: not a military difficulty (the bende was tiny) but a doctrinal and familial one, in which a fifteen-year-old boy could not surrender without denying the *sjahid* status of his father, uncles and great-uncles. His placement in the book is also Dames's clearest statement of the religious dimension of the Aceh War — the dimension the [[K.N.I.L.]] could not, in the end, defeat by arms alone.
