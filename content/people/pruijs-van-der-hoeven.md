**Pruijs van der Hoeven** — also written *Pruys van der Hoeven* — was a Dutch colonial civil servant, formerly *Resident van Palembang* (Resident of Palembang), who served as the first *Civiele Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* (Civil Governor of Atjeh and Dependencies) from [[April 1881|April]] [[1881]] to his resignation in [[December 1882|December]] [[1882]], and whose governorship — characterised by *struisvogelpolitiek* (ostrich policy) toward the Atjehese resistance and by the dismantling of the defensive measures of his predecessor [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] — inaugurated the disastrous [[Civil Government Period (1881-1884)|civil government period]] of [[1881]]–[[1884]] that would culminate in the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[1884]].

## Appointment (April 1881)

Pruijs van der Hoeven was the former *Resident van Palembang* when, in [[1880]], the *Gouverneur-Generaal* [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]] — who judged the war ended and the time come for civil government — sent him to Atjeh to arrange with [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]],[[General van der Heijden|]] a proposed organisation of the administration. Against Van der Heijden's opinion, the government decided that the Governor of Atjeh should be a civil official, and in [[April 1881|April]] [[1881]] Pruijs van der Hoeven was so appointed. [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]] would later write that the displacement of Van der Heijden was "tot eeuwige schande en groote schade voor Nederland" (to the eternal disgrace and great damage of the Netherlands), and that "de ontijdige invoering van civiel bestuur was de grootste fout, die wij hebben begaan" (the premature introduction of civil government was the greatest mistake we have made).

## The Policy of Conciliation (1881–1882)

On taking over the administration, Pruijs van der Hoeven himself described the situation in Atjeh as "zeer bevredigend" (very satisfactory). Full of confidence in his personal insights and policy, considering the Atjehers accessible to benevolence, generosity and justice, too little reckoning with the great religious and racial hatred, missing or underrating the experience of his predecessors, he believed that he could now chiefly govern Atjeh with civil officials, Atjehese chiefs, and police. This view caused the endless patrols in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] — still deemed necessary by Van der Heijden — to be virtually abolished, against the advice of the military commander, [[Kolonel Haus|Colonel Haus]]; the military force to be confined to the care of the posts' security, otherwise acting only on request of the civil administration; some posts to be dismantled to spare transports, no longer to serve as targets for marauders, and so to increase the general security; and the path of friendly negotiation to be tried. Moreover, Van der Heijden's [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] was withdrawn in [[September 1881|September]] [[1881]], while another regulation that Pruijs van der Hoeven considered useful and proposed to the government was not implemented.

## The Collapse of Security (1881–1882)

The consequence of disregarding Van der Heijden's advice was that in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] — where in [[September 1879|September]] [[1879]] the military commander [[Generaal Boumeester|General Boumeester]] had still visited the outermost posts without hearing a shot or seeing an enemy — the small band of malefactors that had occurred even under Van der Heijden, and against which precisely those endless patrols were to serve, soon began to form themselves into enemy bands, as they noticed that they could do so unpunished; that the war party began to reorganise itself and to appear under its known chiefs; that fanaticism was skilfully aroused and fanned by the religious leaders, who had already begun to preach the holy war in [[March 1881|March]] [[1881]]; that the population, no longer feeling protected, lost confidence in the Dutch; and that on the coasts trade was carried on more lustily than ever.

In [[June 1881|June]] [[1881]] the Dutch posts were fired on again for the first time; thereafter patrols and transports were ambushed without cease, Chinese settlements and enterprises massacred, and so on. Vainly Colonel Haus urged the taking of military measures in [[August 1881|August]] [[1881]]. Ever bolder, strong enemy bands pressed into the [[22 Moekims|22]] and [[26 Moekims|26 Moekims]] and onto Dutch territory, even into the immediate vicinity of [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] and [[Oleh-leh|Oleh-leh]], where the passes were ambushed and had to be moved. The Atjehese chiefs declared that without troops they could no longer answer for the peace in their territory. They finally dared no longer come to [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]], and, caught between two fires, were forced to side with the enemy. The idleness and disparagement of the military, who had to let themselves be shot at and murdered with impunity, worked killing on the spirit of the troops and produced discontent and desertion to the enemy.

## The Scheepvaartregeling of May 1882

Inflexibly, the first civil governor remained blind to the facts, and supported by the government, which naturally went by his reports, he continued in good faith to demolish the laborious and costly work of years. Only in [[August 1882|August]] [[1882]], when the need grew ever more urgent, was he compelled to take another path, and the military commander was given more freedom of action. But by then it was too late. The evil was done. To be sure, during this time much work was done on the improvement of communications by the construction of roads and bridges, on the consolidation and strengthening of [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] and surroundings, and in [[August 1882|August]] [[1882]] on a part of the [[Noordkust|North Coast]] a [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] was successfully introduced — but the good effects of all this were nullified by the ostrich policy toward the enemy.

## The Telegrafie of "Pacificate van Atjeh verzekerd"

The numerous engagements and ambushes, in which the Dutch sometimes even had to leave their dead with their rifles in enemy hands, continued until Pruijs van der Hoeven's departure. Yet he claimed that in [[September 1882|September]] [[1882]] he had the fire underfoot, that the peace was not further disturbed until his departure, and that the situation remained favourable — he telegraphed the government shortly before his departure: "*Pacificatie van Atjeh verzekerd*" (Pacification of Atjeh secured) — and declared at the handover of the administration that everything pointed to a peaceful solution. Kruijt observes: "Terecht vraagt men zich hierbij af waarom, wanneer naar zijn inzien de toestand zoo gunstig was, hij, met terzijdestelling zijner grieven, toen niet gebleven is om te bewijzen dat zijn inzicht juist was en om zijne grootsche taak te voltooien?" (One rightly asks why, if in his view the situation was so favourable, he did not then, setting aside his grievances, stay to prove that his view was correct and to complete his great task?).

## Resignation (December 1882) and the 1893 Speeches

Pruijs van der Hoeven requested his discharge in [[December 1882|December]] [[1882]] on account of differences of opinion with the government. The government had begun to perceive that the reports did not represent the situation as it really was; that it was not peace but full war; that not small bands of marauders but numerous enemies stood against the Dutch; and pressed the Governor to take more account of them.

In [[March 1893|March]] [[1893]], at the handover of the administration in Atjeh, he declared himself in speeches on [[13 March|13]] and [[18 March]] [[1893]] about the Atjeh question — speeches that Kruijt cites at the opening of his brochure as evidence of the Dutch public's unfamiliarity with "doel, handelingen en beteekenis van voorvallen in Atjeh" (the purpose, actions and significance of events in Atjeh). Kruijt cites the speeches as evidence of the persistence, even after a decade, of the civil-government illusion that the war could be ended by conciliation.

## Significance

Pruijs van der Hoeven's governorship is, in Kruijt's analysis, the principal disaster of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. His dismantling of Van der Heijden's defensive system, his abolition of the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]], his ostrich policy toward the resistance, and his optimistic but false reports to the government together constitute the chain of decisions that turned the promising situation of [[April 1881|April]] [[1881]] into the catastrophic situation that required the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[1884]]. His telegraphed claim of "*Pacificatie van Atjeh verzekerd*" became, in Kruijt's brochure, the byword for the Dutch civil government's self-deception in Atjeh.
