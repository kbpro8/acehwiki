**Tjoet Meuthia** — modern *Cut Meutia*, "the Pearl" (*meuthia* means *pearl*) — was a daughter of T. Ben Dawot of [[Pira]], and indeed a pearl among women. She was first married to [[T. Tjhi Bentara]], the weak and unpopular [[Uleebalang|oeléëbalang]] of [[Keureutoe]] appointed by the Dutch, then to his half-brother [[T. Tjhi Toenong|T. Tjhi Toenong]] (the *balang tjoet baroh*), and after Tjhi Toenong's execution in [[March]] [[1905]] to [[Pang Nanggroë]]. She was the mother of [[T. Radja Sabi]], the *adelaarsjong* of Keureutoe.

## Of remarkable beauty

She was a daughter of T. Ben Dawot, of [[Pira]], one of the unyielding chiefs who would hear nothing of submission and who, after the Kompeuni's victory, withdrew to the mountains and forests just outside the range of the still-not-very-mobile Dutch garrisons. There they sat, a little beyond musket-shot of the territory covered by the outermost Dutch posts, and acknowledged only the authority of the Sultan. Tjoet Meuthia was not only of remarkable beauty, but a proud and stately figure. In the picturesque festive dress of women of her rank — the black silk pantalon (*siloeuë Atjeh*) and the *badjoe*, the breast closed by interlocking golden ornaments, the dark hair crowned with the golden *oeléë tjeumara*, and the foot-rings on her fine ankles — she was a presence of rare allure and enchantment. No wonder the pretendent-sultan, a *vrouwenjager* by excellence, had an eye on her; but he had to remain an admirer at a distance. This Pearl might perhaps have been the wife of a Sultan and the flower of his house in [[Keumala]], but never his mistress. The soft ringing of her golden ankle-rings was no lure for him.

Such a woman did not fit with the limp [[T. Tjhi Bentara]] or with his dependence on the Kompeuni. She was a true daughter of her people, and her heart drew to the party of the unyielding ones in the mountains, the men who walked the way of God and among whom her father and brother were to be found. Therefore [[T. Tjhi Bentara|Tjhi Bentara]] repudiated her — a gesture by an unwanted man trying to save his face. Very shortly afterwards she married his half-brother [[T. Tjhi Toenong]], who also disliked the Kompeuni, and they withdrew to the free mountains where he became one of the most feared commanders.

## Marriage to Pang Nanggroë, March 1905

In the days before his execution on [[5 March]] [[1905]], [[T. Tjhi Toenong]] was permitted to receive his wife, and in the bitterness of those last days — he had not in a dozen fights been granted the crown of the *sjahid* — his hatred flared up again. He made the pregnant Tjoet Meuthia swear that she would bring up their son, then about five years old, in hatred of the *kaphé*, and that after his execution she would marry [[Pang Nanggroë]]. This she swore to him, and he knew that a woman of her character would keep that oath. After forty-four days — the *puth ploh peuët* of Atjehsche custom — Tjoet Meuthia was clean in body and spirit and let Pang Nanggroë know she was ready.

## Death at Kroeëng Peutoeë, 25 October 1910

On [[25 October]] [[1910]] she was killed in the overval by [[Mosselman]]'s brigade at the Kroeëng Peutoeë. The overval, in the course of a pursuit of the bende of [[Tengkoe Sjech di Paja Bakong|Seupot Mata]] and of [[T. Radja Sabi|her son]], was made near a small river-bed. She was identified by Mosselman afterwards as the woman with loosened hair who charged the brigade with the klewang. Her son, then about ten years old, was standing unarmed in the beek fishing when the warning cry *"Kaphé datang"* sounded; one of his *kawans* seized him in his arms and bore him into the wood. *"Saja tida lihat lagi lboe"* — "I have not seen my mother again." Her grave he never found.

## Significance

Of Tjoet Meuthia, Zentgraff writes that her second husband was fusilladed, her third (Pang Nanggroë) was shot; the unceasing hunt, year after year, naturally drove her to seek death in the certainty of reunion with those who had gone before her. She and Pang Nanggroë were persons of whom one would later say that they had carried themselves to the last as the great figures of the resistance.
