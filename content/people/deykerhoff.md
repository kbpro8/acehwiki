**Deykerhoff** (also spelled [[Deijkerhoff]]) served as [[Gouverneur]] of [[Atjeh]] from [[January 1892]] until the strategic reversal of [[1896]]. His administration is defined by the alliance-policy centred on [[T. Oema]]—a policy that relied on arming [[Atjeh]] proxies to suppress resistance, and which ended catastrophically with [[Oema]]'s defection.

## The alliance policy and T. Oema

Upon his appointment in [[January 1892]], [[Deykerhoff]] rejected the conclusions of [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]]'s [[1892]] report, *Verslag omtrent den religieus-politieken toestand in Atjeh*, in favour of his own approach. The government—fearing the costs of direct military aggression and mindful of recent failed expeditions to [[Flores]]—preferred [[Deykerhoff]]'s alliance strategy to direct military action with Dutch troops.

Under this policy, [[Deykerhoff]] placed unlimited trust in [[T. Oema]] (also known as [[Toekoe Oemar]]), who was granted official clemency, permitted to maintain a legion of 250 men at Dutch expense, and later received 1,000 rifles, much ammunition, and substantial sums of money. This arming and subsidising was undertaken against the explicit warnings of [[Snouck Hurgronje]].

## The rejection of Snouck Hurgronje

[[Deykerhoff]]'s confidence in [[Oema]] extended to active obstruction of [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s work. When [[Snouck Hurgronje]]—after completing his [[Mecca]] journey—requested permission from [[Minister Keuchenius]] to investigate [[Atjeh]] affairs in the interior (either under an Arabic name or as an unofficial European), he received a favourable response. However, upon arriving in [[Penang]] ([[Pinang]]), he received notice from the [[Indische Regeering]] that the [[Gouverneur]] of [[Atjeh]]—[[Van Teyn]]—had raised objections to the arrival of a new adviser, arguing that this could damage the expected submission of the so-called [[Keumala]] party.

Although it was actually [[Deykerhoff]] who had replaced an assistant resident with the more prominent [[Ass. Resident Ruys-Senaers]]—who apparently saw no competitor in [[Snouck Hurgronje]]—the effect was the same: [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s recommendations were ignored, and his services were not utilised during [[Deykerhoff]]'s administration.

## The 1896 catastrophe

The history of events that followed from [[Deykerhoff]]'s programme demonstrates clearly that too much honour and too great trust had been given to the [[lunglima-peran]] (the chief of [[Leupung]]?). In [[1896]], [[T. Oema]] threw off his co-operative mask and turned his modern Dutch weapons against the government. The immediate result was that Dutch soldiers found themselves fighting Atjeh opponents armed with rifles supplied by the Dutch themselves.

Only after this catastrophe did it become clear that [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s ideas could not be dismissed without consequence. The [[Indisch Besluit]] of [[14 September]] [[1896]] No. 2—issued after [[Deykerhoff]]'s policy had collapsed—adopted many of [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s recommendations. The strategic reversal that followed—culminating in the appointment of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in [[March 1898]]—was a direct repudiation of [[Deykerhoff]]'s alliance-based approach.

## Assessment

In the analysis of [[Dr. J. M. Somer]], [[Deykerhoff]]'s administration exemplifies the dangers of political interference in military affairs. By arming an unreliable ally and ignoring ethnographic intelligence, the government achieved only temporary and deceptive calm—followed by a more serious crisis. The episode confirmed [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s central argument: that no treaty with a sultan or alliance with a powerful chief could pacify [[Atjeh]], and that only sustained direct action based on accurate knowledge of the country and its people could succeed.
