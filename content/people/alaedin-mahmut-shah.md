Sultan of Acheh, reigned approximately [[1752]]-[[1766]], ruling in the period following the succession struggles recorded in the Hikayat Pochut Muhamat.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Alaedin Mahmut

**Alaedin Mahmut**, also written **Alaeddin Mahmud** or **Alauddin Mahmud**, and bearing the formal regnal name **Sultan Mahmut Shah** or **Alaedin Mahmut Shah**, was a [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan]] of the latest (Bugis-origin) dynasty of [[Acheh]], generally assigned a reign from [[1781]] to [[1795]]. He is best attested in the documentary record by a *sarakata* (royal edict) bearing the date [[1766]].

## The edict of 1766

Snouck Hurgronje, during his residence in [[Acheh]], collected copies of a number of *sarakata* not included among those published by [[K. F. H. Van Langen|Van Langen]]. Among them he lists "one of Alaedin Mahmut dated [[1766]]", in close association with an edict of Jamalul-Alam dated [[1689]] revived by [[Alaedin Juhan]] in [[1752]], and with the undated edicts of Sapiatodin, [[Amat Shah Juhan]] and [[Badrudin Asem]] (Hashim). All of these dealt with "commercial and port regulations as affecting different nationalities and different kinds of merchandise, and the collection and distribution of taxes", and included detailed customs-tariffs. They are described as "identical in spirit and intention with those published by Van Langen", but richer in detail regarding court and festival ceremonial and the disposal of import taxes, and occasionally at variance with the latter on points of detail.

The document is one of the principal surviving testimonies of Alaedin Mahmut's legislative activity. The discrepancy between the edict's date of [[1766]] and the later conventionally-accepted regnal dates of [[1781]]–[[1795]] is one of those chronological puzzles that Snouck Hurgronje notes are common in Achehnese royal chronology.

## Place on the ninefold seal

Alaedin Mahmut's name appears on the ninefold seal of the pretender [[Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan|Sultan Muhamat Dawot]], drawn up in [[1879]]. There he is inscribed as "Sultan Malmut Shah 1781–1795" — one of the seven sultans whose names surround the pretender's own in the central circle. The first four names on such seals — those of [[Sultan Sayyidi al-Mukamnal|Sayyidi al-Mukamnal]], [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]], [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Tajul-alam]] and [[Sultan Ahmat Shah|Sultan Ahmat Shah]] — recur on most Achehnese ninefold seals and represent the rulers to whom the former prosperity of Acheh and her *adat*-institutions are generally ascribed. The remaining names, including that of Alaedin Mahmut, were chosen by each sultan to suit his own taste.

## Invocation in letters patent

In the formal letters patent granted by the pretender sultans to their chiefs — such as the document bestowed on [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] on 4 Rabi' al-Awwal 1307 H ([[1889]]) — the "blessing of the mediation" of a fixed series of former monarchs is invoked. Alaedin Mahmut appears in this list as "His late Majesty Sultan Alaedin Mahmut Shah", between [[Sultan Ahmat Shah|Alaedin Ahmat Shah]] and [[Sultan Alaedin Muhamat Shah|Alaedin Muhamat Shah]]. The formula is purely conventional, but the place of Alaedin Mahmut in the sequence testifies to his standing in the dynastic memory.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sultan Mahmut Shah

**Sultan Mahmut Shah** — known in full on his royal seals as **Sultan Alaedin Mahmut Shah** — was one of the last recognised sultans of [[Acheh]], reigning in the period immediately before and during the first Dutch attack on the sultanate's capital. He is named in the formal invocation of deceased sultans recited in the [[sarakata]] (royal edicts) of his successor [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]], where his mediation is invoked alongside that of [[Alaedin Ahmat Shah]], [[Alaedin Muhamat Shah]], [[Sultan Jauhar Alain Shah|Sultan Alaedin Jauhar al-Alam Shah]] and [[Sultan Mansur Shah|Alaedin Manso Shah]]. His inclusion in this chain of "blessed" predecessors confirms his position within the late royal house that traced itself to [[Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah|Sultan Ibrahim Manso Shah]] ([[1858]]–[[1870]]).

Snouck Hurgronje's account of the late sultanate is unflattering. He describes the late [[Dalam]] (the Sultan's court) as a sunk and paralysed institution in which the sultan's actual power was "confined to the limits of the Dalam", though forms of address continued to suggest a wider authority. The Tuankus, scions of royal blood, were "convinced that they stood superior to the *adat pôteen meureuhom* and all other adats", but were generally men of ill repute, "such for instance as had fled to the Dalam to escape a righteous blood-vengeance which threatened their lives". It was this enfeebled court that Sultan Mahmut Shah headed when the Dutch expedition of [[1873]] struck at [[Banda Acheh]].

The most consequential event of Mahmut Shah's reign was the [[Loss of the Dalam|fall of the Dalam]] to the Dutch forces during the [[First Dutch attack on Acheh|first Dutch attack]] in [[1873]]. The capture of the court — called the *Kraton* by the Dutch — deprived the sultanate of its symbolic and administrative centre, and Snouck notes that "before the Dutch gained possession of the Dalam, the sultan had disappeared from the scene". Sultan Mahmut Shah died shortly afterwards, leaving no successor capable of uniting the sundered country. "When Sultan Mahmut Shah died shortly after the loss of the Dalam," Snouck writes, "it was not thought necessary even in so perilous a crisis to select from among the candidates for the succession to the throne a man before all things. They contented themselves with a child, Tuanku Muhamat Dawot".

The choice of a child-sultan proved disastrous for organised resistance. The [[Achehnese War]] thereafter "was always separate and independent bodies of troops, led either by adat-chiefs or by some newly-arisen commanders of energetic disposition", without any common royal direction. The sole figure who succeeded in organising the forces to "a comparatively high degree" during the early part of the war was the foreigner [[Sayyid Abdurrahman Zahir]], not the fugitive court. Mahmut Shah's death thus marks the practical end of the Sultanate of Acheh as a political reality, even though [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot|Sultan Alaedin Muhamat Dawot Shah Juhan]] and his successors continued to issue sarakata from their exile at [[Keumala]].
