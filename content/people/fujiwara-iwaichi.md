**Fujiwara Iwaichi** (藤原岩市, [[1909]]–[[1986]]) — Dutch sources' **Major Fujiwara**, also **Fujiwara of the F-organisatie** — was a Major of the Imperial Japanese Army who founded and led the [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] — the Japanese intelligence organisation, also known as the [[F-organisatie|F-organisatie]] (F-organisation) or the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] — that supported anti-Dutch elements in [[Atjeh|Atjèh]] prior to the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]] and that gave the fifth-column movement in Atjèh its distinctive character and its dangerous quality. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Fujiwara as the architect of the Japanese political preparation of the Atjèhnese terrain — the intelligence officer who, building on the lively trade relations between [[Penang|Penang]] and the [[Overwal|Overwal]] that had been maintained since time immemorial, constructed the network of Atjèhnese collaborators whose activation would accompany the invasion. The Dutch staff history *[[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Nederlands-Indië contra Japan]]* identifies Fujiwara through the interrogation of a Menadonese soldier at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]] in late February [[1942]], and frames him as the principal Japanese intelligence officer of the pre-invasion Atjeh rising, and the founder of the wider *Fujiwara-kikan* that would later re-emerge in [[1945]] as the [[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi-kikan]] under [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]].

## Identification

The *[[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Nederlands-Indië contra Japan]]* staff history identifies Fujiwara through the interrogation of a Menadonese soldier at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]] in late February [[1942]]. The Menadonese had boasted of his admiration for the Japanese; under sharp interrogation he confessed "dat hij door een aantal Atjehers te zijn overgehaald toe te treden tot een wijdvertakte z.g. F. beweging. … Later werd bekend, dat dit een afkorting was van Fujiwara, een Japanner, die in Malakka een georganiseerde colonne had uit aldaar aanwezige Indonesiërs. Reeds na de val van Penang (19 December 1941) maakte Fujiwara met de opbouw van deze organisatie een aanvang."

The staff history's identification of Fujiwara as the "Japanner" who founded the F-beweging in Malakka after the [[Fall of Penang|fall of Penang]] is the principal Dutch printed identification of the F-beweging's organiser. The identification is consistent with the post-war recognition of **Major Iwaichi Fujiwara** (藤原岩市, [[1909]]–[[1986]]) as the founder of the *Fujiwara-kikan* — the Japanese intelligence network that, under Fujiwara and later [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]], would govern the [[Jappentijd (Japanese occupation, 1942-1945)|Jappentijd]] occupation of Atjèh.

## The Founding of the Fujiwara-kikan

The [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] — literally "the Fujiwara organisation," deriving its name from its founder — was formed by the Japanese during their advance in Malaya from the Indonesians residing there. After the [[Fall of Penang|fall of Penang]] on [[19 December]] [[1941]], Piekaar records, "the construction of this organization could be begun. For on Penang, the lively trade relations, which had been maintained with the [[Overwal|Overwal]] since time immemorial, were concentrated." The free port of Penang — the same entrepôt whose commercial dominance the Dutch had long tried to challenge through the development of [[Sabang|Sabang]] — provided the Japanese intelligence apparatus with the commercial and personal networks through which the F-organisatie's Atjèhnese agents could be recruited and supplied. The free port hosted a large Atjèhnese colony of merchants and seamen, "besides many merchants and seamen, also numerous people who, for political or other reasons, voluntarily or not, preferred an existence abroad" — a reservoir of dissatisfied Atjèhnese from which the F-organisatie drew its propagandists.

## The F-propagandists

The agents of the Fujiwara-kikan — the F-propagandists — were Atjèhnese political dissidents, religious figures, and descendants of the Prophet who had been recruited in Penang and sent back to Atjèh as "refugees" for the Japanese oppressor. Piekaar records that "as most of them were not politically conspicuous, they were, even though they were intercepted and arrested by the coast guard, after having obtained advice from the local Dutch and Indonesian administrative officials, in most cases released after a brief interrogation." The Dutch failure to recognise the danger of the F-organisatie's apparatus — a failure Piekaar attributes to the inconspicuous character of the agents — allowed the network to be activated when the invasion came.

## The F-beweging's Membership Insignia

The staff history records that "alle tot deze beweging toegetredenen zouden door de Japanners als hun vrienden en medewerkers worden beschouwd en het insigne (witte band met rode letter F.) zou bij hun komst op zichtbare wijze worden gedragen." The white armband with the red letter F was thus the visible insignia by which F-beweging members were to identify themselves to the Japanese on their arrival — an insignium whose meaning was unknown to the Dutch command at Kotaradja until the Menadonese's interrogation in late February [[1942]]. The Dutch command's initial incomprehension of the letter "F." is, in the staff history's framing, the operational measure of the concealed character of Fujiwara's pre-invasion apparatus.

## The 1942 Activation

When the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]] came, the F-organisatie's network was activated. The F-propagandists [[Said Aboe Bakar|Said Aboe Bakar]] and [[Said Doellah|Said Doellah]] entered Atjèh with the first Japanese troops at [[Meulabóh|Meulabóh]] and thereupon assembled the governing commissions that displaced the loyal Dutch *oelèëbalang*'s. The intercepted letters found on Said Doellah — addressed to "the same group of persons" who would form the membership of the new commissions — revealed the extent of the F-organisatie's pre-invasion network. The activation of the network was, in this sense, the realisation of Fujiwara's pre-investment in the Atjèhnese terrain. The invasion ended 39 years of Dutch rule in Atjèh and installed the [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]] military administration; the pre-invasion Fujiwara-kikan network now stepped forward openly to displace the loyal *oelèëbalang*s.

## The Transition to Masubuchi

After the invasion, the Fujiwara-kikan was succeeded by the [[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi-kikan]] — the M-organisatie, under Major (later Lieutenant-General) [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]]. The transition from F- to M-organisation marked the shift from the intelligence-and-fifth-column phase to the occupation-administration phase. Piekaar notes that "in the Japanese Masubuchi, whom we shall get to know better in the second part of this work, this organization found for Aceh an extremely capable leader" — implying that the F-organisatie's apparatus, in Masubuchi's hands, became the institutional foundation of the occupation administration. The Fujiwara–[[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]] succession is the staff history's principal instance of the institutional continuity of the Japanese intelligence network in Atjèh from [[1941]] to [[1945]].

## Significance

Fujiwara is, in the *[[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Nederlands-Indië contra Japan]]* staff history's Atjèh chapter, the principal Japanese intelligence officer of the pre-invasion Atjèh rising, and the founder of the wider *Fujiwara-kikan* that would govern the Japanese occupation of Atjèh through the [[Jappentijd (Japanese occupation, 1942-1945)|Jappentijd]]. The staff history's emphasis on Fujiwara's role in the *opbouw* of the F-beweging after the [[Fall of Penang|fall of Penang]] — and on the Dutch command's initial incomprehension of the letter "F." — frames Fujiwara as the principal Japanese intelligence officer whose concealed operation determined the operational shape of the Atjèh campaign. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* gives Fujiwara the corresponding role on the political side: the architect of the Japanese political preparation of the Atjèhnese terrain, whose F-propagandists — recruited from the Atjèhnese colony on Penang and slipped back into Atjèh as "refugees" in the weeks before the invasion — laid the network that would be activated on the night of [[11 March|11]]–[[12 March]] [[1942]] in coordination with [[Poesa|Poesa]] and the sultan's party.
