The **Tommegong of Singapore** (in Van der Kemp's Dutch spelling, *Tommegong of Sincapour*; modern spelling: the **Temenggong of Singapore**) was the Malay chief of [[Singapore]] whose naval dispositions in March [[1819]] attracted the anxious attention of the Dutch authorities in the region. He is mentioned in a private letter from Governor [[Timmerman Thyssen]] of Malacca to [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek]] at Batavia, dated 16 March [[1819]], preserved as *Bijlage* IV to Van der Kemp's article. The Temenggong is reported to have had at sea "nine well-armed *pengojabja*" — large Malay war-boats — which Thyssen believed were not so much cruising for plunder as watching for dispatches expected with a certain **Santa Maria**, otherwise known as [[Teunes]].

## Historical identity

The Tommegong of Singapore at this date is generally identified with **Temenggong Abdul Rahman**, the Malay chief who, together with Sultan Hussein Shah of Johor, had only weeks before — in early February [[1819]] — granted [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] permission to establish a British trading settlement on Singapore. Raffles's [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]] in February [[1819]] thus forms the immediate backdrop to Thyssen's report of March. The Temenggong's "nine well-armed *pengojabja*" should be read in this context: the new British settlement was barely a month old, relations between the Malay chiefs and the incoming British were still fluid, and Dutch intelligence was naturally alive to any movement of armed Malay craft in the Straits.

## The Wolterbeek–Thyssen correspondence

Thyssen's letter to Wolterbeek was written at a moment of acute Dutch anxiety about Raffles's movements in the region. The governor had been told by the harbour-master [[Carnegy]] — a figure whose loyalty Thyssen himself distrusted — that two ships with three hundred troops had sailed from [[Penang]] bound for the [[Karimon Islands|Carrimons]], with the intention of taking possession of those islands, and that Raffles was preparing to follow with three more ships and four hundred men, ostensibly for [[Acheh]]. Thyssen did not believe the Acheh report; he suspected Raffles would instead join his friends at the Carrimons and attempt an attack on [[Lingga]]. It is in this atmosphere of rumour and counter-rumour that the Tommegong's war-boats enter the report: their presence at sea was taken as a sign that the Singapore chief was himself mobilising, perhaps in concert with the British, perhaps only to intercept the dispatches carried by [[Teunes]].

## The dispatch question

The most curious element in Thyssen's report is the suggestion that the Tommegong's boats were watching not for plunder but for "the dispatches which with Santa Maria (alias Teunes) were expected." Van der Kemp does not explain whose dispatches these were, nor why a Malay chief should be intercepting them. The likeliest reading is that Teunes — a pirate-cum-courier whose alias "Santa Maria" suggests a Christian (Indo-Portuguese) origin — was carrying correspondence between Dutch posts, perhaps between [[Malacca]] and Batavia or between Malacca and Riau, and that the Tommegong, now aligned with the newly arrived British, was seeking to seize or inspect those dispatches. Whether he succeeded is not recorded in Van der Kemp's text.

## Significance

The Tommegong of Singapore is a minor figure in Van der Kemp's narrative, but his appearance in the Thyssen–Wolterbeek correspondence illustrates the dense interconnection of British, Dutch, and Malay actors in the Straits during the spring of [[1819]]. Within weeks of Raffles's landing, the Malay chief of Singapore was already being drawn into the broader Anglo-Dutch rivalry — his war-boats a small but visible piece on the chessboard that would, five years later, be settled (temporarily) by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London of 1824]].
