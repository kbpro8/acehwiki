**I. D. Fransen van de Putte** was a Dutch politician who served as Minister of Colonies in the ministry De Vries-Geertsema, appointed by Royal Decrees of [[6 July]] [[6 July]] [[1872]]. His tenure as Minister of Colonies covered the immediate prelude to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] — the [[Aceh Envoys' Visit to Singapore (January 1873)|Acehnese envoys' visit to Singapore]] of [[January 1873|January 1873]] and the Dutch decision to send an expedition — and the first months of the war itself.

## The Ministry De Vries-Geertsema, 6 July 1872

By Royal Decrees of [[6 July]] [[6 July]] [[1872]] honourable discharge was granted to the ministers [[Van Bosse|Van Bosse]], [[Blussé|Blussé]] and [[Delprat|Delprat]], and with that came an end to the third ministry [[Thorbecke|Thorbecke]]. On the same date were appointed: as Minister of Justice, Mr. A. de Vries Gz.; as Minister of the Interior, Mr. G. H. Geertsema; as Minister of Finance, Mr. A. van Delden; as Minister of War, M. D. graaf van Limburg Stirum; and as Minister of Colonies, I. D. Fransen van de Putte. The Ministers of Foreign Affairs and of Marine, Mr. J. L. H. A. Gericke van Herwynen and [[Brocx|L. G. Brocx]], retained their portfolios.

## The Decision to Send an Expedition, 18 February 1873

The telegram of [[16 February]] [[16 February]] [[1873]] from the Consul-General [[W. H. Read|W. H. Read]] at Singapore, reporting the Acehnese envoys' approaches to the American and Italian consuls for help against the Dutch, was transmitted by the Governor-General [[J. Loudon|Loudon]] to the Minister of Colonies. Fransen van de Putte, with the knowledge of the King and the council of ministers, sent a telegram on [[18 February]] [[18 February]] [[1873]]: "If you do not doubt the correctness of the report of Consul Singapore, further delay is unlawful. Will send powerful sea-power to Atjeh, to ask clarification and account for ambiguous and treacherous conduct and the regulation of the relation of the Netherlands to Atjeh in the proposed sense. If not sufficiently complied with, the fighting forces shall be brought together, suitable to give sufficient emphasis to the demand with the least delay."


## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**I. D. Fransen van de Putte** — Isaac Dignus Fransen van de Putte ([[1822]]-[[1908]]) — was a Dutch liberal politician who served three brief terms as minister of Colonies ([[1866]]; [[1872]]-[[1874]]; [[1876]]-[[1877]]) and thereafter, as a member of the [[Tweede Kamer]] for the *Onafhankelijken* (u.l., independents), became one of the most persistent parliamentary critics of the [[Atjeh-oorlog ([[1873]]-[[1904]])|Atjeh-oorlog]]. His 1896 speech — "Laat ons wat leren van onze voorouders" — let us learn something from our ancestors — is one of the most quoted liberal statements against the conquest of Atjeh.

### The 1896 speech

In the [[Tweede Kamer]] debate following [[Toekoe Oemar's defection ([[1896]])|the defection of Toekoe Oemar]], [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] (u.l.) delivered the speech that would become the canonical liberal indictment of the Atjehnese war:

> "Het woord moet eruit ... naar mijn oordeel zijn wij er niet op aangekleed zulk een land te veroveren ... Laat ons wat leren van onze voorouders. Wij hebben 150 jaar in Makassar gezeten zonder buiten de limiet van de stad te kunnen komen ... En op dit ogenblik hebben wij op Celebes een gouverneur en een assistent-resident, die het volk kennen en bijna alle rijken van Celebes doortrekken vergezeld van slechts enige politiedienaren".

— The word must come out ... in my judgement we are not equipped to conquer such a land ... Let us learn something from our ancestors. We sat in Makassar for 150 years without being able to go beyond the city limits ... And at this moment we have on Celebes a governor and an assistant-resident who know the people and traverse nearly all the kingdoms of Celebes accompanied by only a few policemen.

[[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] "haalde nog andere voorbeelden aan van de lange duur van onze bestuursvestiging" — adduced still other examples of the long duration of our administrative establishments — and concluded with the exhortation: "Geduld, Mijnheer de Minister, en weet dat ook aan anderen in te prenten" — Patience, Mr Minister, and know that you must also impress it upon others.

### The call for withdrawal

[[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]]'s parliamentary ally [[H. B. van Kol|Van Kol]] (s.d.) went further and "stelde voor, dat Nederland zich geheel uit Atjeh zou terugtrekken" — proposed that the Netherlands withdraw entirely from Atjeh. With the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|Sumatra-tractaat]] in hand the [[Netherlands]] had nothing to fear from [[Great Britain|England]]; other powers would readily recognise Dutch claims on Atjeh. The only danger was that, by continuing "onze wrede wijze van oorlogvoeren" — our cruel way of waging war — the [[Netherlands]] would give a great power a pretext for intervention.

### The later influence

The 1896 speech of [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] echoes through the subsequent Atjeh debates. When, in [[1904]], [[J. K. J. de Stuers|De Stuers]] (catholic) pronounced his verdict on the [[Van Daalen's Gajo-Alas-Batak expedition (1904)|expedition of Van Daalen]] — "Laten wij beschaving brengen, maar niet door middel van bloedbaden! Wat is daarvoor dan nodig? Eén ding. Geduld. Dat nu ontbreekt" — his conclusion, in the explicit judgement of [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]], "week niet ver af van die van [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]], enkele jaren tevoren".

### Significance

[[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]]'s 1896 speech is the most forceful liberal parliamentary argument against the conquest of Atjeh. The Makassar precedent — 150 years of patient co-existence within the city limits — supplied the historical reference-point for every subsequent liberal argument that the [[Netherlands]] should either subjugate Atjeh outright or withdraw, but should not prolong the intermediate state of inconclusive warfare.

## Van Swieten’s account

[[Jan van Swieten]] wrote in [[1879]] that discussions among [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte]], [[James Loudon]], and himself had framed the [[Second Dutch Expedition to Aceh]] around restraint: neither unlimited subjugation nor immediate direct administration. This identifies Fransen van de Putte’s place in Van Swieten’s retrospective argument.
