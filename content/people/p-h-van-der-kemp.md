**P. H. van der Kemp** was a Dutch colonial historian and contributor to the *Bijdragen van het Koninklijk Instituut voor de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde van Nederlandsch Indië* ("Contributions of the Royal Institute for the Linguistic, Geographical and Ethnological Sciences of the Netherlands Indies"). He is cited by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in the Introduction to Vol. I of *The Achehnese* as the author of a recent essay on the treaty concluded with [[Acheh]] by [[Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] in [[1819]].

Van der Kemp's essay, published in volume LI of the *Bijdragen* (pp. 159–240, The Hague, [[1900]]), reproduces long extracts from the archives of the India Office in London. Through these quotations Snouck Hurgronje was able to demonstrate that the arrangements which the British East India Company made with Acheh at the close of the eighteenth and the beginning of the nineteenth century for the establishment of friendly relations were "overridden in the most insulting way" by the Achehnese side. The essay also documents the correspondence regarding "Johor Allum as king of Acheen," a figure about whom the Chairman of the Company wrote to Bengal on 4 August [[1824]] that "that chief, so far as we can collect from your correspondence, not having possessed an established authority in the country which he assumed to represent, has never been in a situation to maintain the relations into which he entered."

Snouck Hurgronje cautions his readers that "in the English documents quoted there are many writer's or printer's errors," a warning that applies to van der Kemp's transcriptions as much as to the originals on which they rest. Beyond this, van der Kemp's work is used by Snouck Hurgronje principally to establish the long historical record of Achehnese intractability towards European trading partners — a record stretching from the discourteous reply sent to [[Warren Hastings]] in [[1786]], through [[Captain Francis Light]]'s disparaging report on Acheh in the same year, to the early nineteenth-century negotiations with [[Raffles]] and beyond.

Van der Kemp thus belongs to that generation of Dutch colonial scholars — alongside [[K. F. H. Van Langen|Van Langen]], [[G. K. Niemann|Niemann]], and [[Prof. Veth|Veth]] — whose archival labours supplied the documentary foundations on which later writers, including Snouck Hurgronje himself, could construct a more analytical account of Achehnese state and society. His essay remains one of the principal European sources for the diplomatic history of Acheh in the period immediately preceding the Dutch intervention of [[1873]].

## A historian of the Dutch East Indies

Van der Kemp was one of the most industrious and pugnacious contributors to the *Bijdragen* in the late nineteenth century. He wrote at length on the relations of the Netherlands with the British in the Malay Archipelago, on the colonial administration of the East Indies, and on the political economy of Java and the Outer Possessions. His articles in the *Bijdragen* include, beside the present article on the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]], his *Papieroorlog* ("paper war") in volume XLIX (1898), his *Sumatra's Westkust* treatise in volumes XLVII (1897) and XLIX (1898), and his *Riouw-verhandeling* in volume LI (1900). In these he repeatedly crosses swords with the British records of the Roosegaarde Bisschop collection and with Dutch apologists such as [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] and [[J. C. Baud|Baud]], and he insists on the dangers of Dutch naivety in dealings with "our powerful neighbour and natural ally".

## *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* (1900)

The article under review, published in the *Bijdragen* of 1900 (6e Volgr., Deel VII, pp. 159–239), is a sustained and at times indignant reconstruction of the origins, character and afterlife of the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|treaty of 22 April 1819]] between [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Sir Stamford Raffles]] and [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Jauhar Alum of Acheh]]. Van der Kemp traces the treaty from the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] of 1802–1819 through the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Commission]] of 1819 and the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of Pedir]], to its afterlife as the "[[dead letter]]" of British diplomacy that, in the eleventh hour of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|London negotiations of March 1824]], was unexpectedly revived and incorporated — by indirection, and against the explicit instructions of the Dutch King — into the Notes exchanged alongside the treaty. The Acheh reservation thus created became, in Van der Kemp's view, the Trojan horse of Anglo-Dutch relations over [[Sumatra]] for half a century, until the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871]] finally removed it.

The article is notable for its extensive use of primary sources drawn from the Roosegaarde Bisschop collection (the "B-verzameling") of British India records and from the Dutch state archives, and it prints twelve appendices of primary documents, including the [[Sultan of Acheh's letter to Warren Hastings|sultan's letter of 7 November 1786 to Warren Hastings]], [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s letter of [[31 December]] [[1819]] to the Bengal secretary, Raffles's instructions to [[Lieutenant Criddle|Lt. Criddle]] of the *Minto*, the correspondence between [[Timmerman Thyssen|Thyssen]] and [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]], [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]], the [[Sultan of Deli]]'s letter of [[27 August]] [[1824]], the [[Three Sagis]]' letter of [[4 December]] [[1822]], [[R. Ibbetson|Ibbetson]]'s minute of [[25 February]] [[1824]], the [[London dispatch of 4 August 1824]], the Siak correspondence of [[1828]], and [[D. P. Faure|D. P. Faure]]'s letter to [[Joseph Chamberlain|Chamberlain]] on the [[Suzerainty question|Transvaal suzerainty question]] of [[1884]].

## Method and reputation

Van der Kemp writes with a sharp polemical edge. He is severe on the Dutch plenipotentiaries [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] and [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] — "we cannot comprehend them, they were really not up to their task, they thought too well of their counterparty" — and on the [[Elout's advice of 12 February 1824|advice of 12 February 1824]] of minister [[Elout]], which he judges to have been "all this very sensible advice" on the Anjer and Tapanuli clauses but inexplicably silent on the Acheh incident that Elout's own son had been dispatched to communicate. He is no less severe on the British: on [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s high-handed treatment of [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] in the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Acheh commission]], on the British plenipotentiaries' "indirect acknowledgement" of Dutch sole authority over Sumatra even while smuggling in an Acheh reservation, and on the "pecksniffian" tone of the English press in matters of colonial treaty-making. The article closes, dated The Hague, February 1900, with an extended comparison between the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|treaty of 1819]] and the [[London Convention on Transvaal (1884)|Transvaal convention of 1884]], arguing that the two episodes illustrate the same British diplomatic practice of introducing, as an innocuous reservation for the sake of "opposition", a clause that is later wielded as an instrument of policy.
