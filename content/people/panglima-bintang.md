**Panglima Bintang** — also written *T. Panglima Bintang* — was the hereditary territorial chief of the [[III Moekims Daroe]] whose hedging diplomacy in the spring and early summer of [[1896]] epitomises the dilemma of the Achehnese chief caught between the [[Dutch]] colonial government and the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]] of [[29 March]] [[1896]], as recorded in A. Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*. Bintang "sent word [in early May [[1896]]] he would join the strongest side", a stance of explicit opportunism which Kruisheer records as that of "*welk hooft op onze hand was*" (which head was on our side) — that is, despite his public posture of neutrality, Bintang had privately committed to the Dutch. He made his "first appearance before the Dutch administration" on [[20 June]] [[1896]], "fearing Umar's revenge", and was told by the [[Regeeringscommissaris]] [[J. A. Vetter|Vetter]] "to remain at [[Poenir]] to protect his subordinates". He "returned 12 of 16 *trompladers*" (muzzle-loaders) lent to him after the Regeeringscommissaris's mid-June disarmament order, a partial compliance which preserved four weapons while formally obeying the order.

## The III Moekims Daroe and the Position of Panglima Bintang

The [[III Moekims Daroe]] lay in the southern sector of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and contained the [[Grot of Daroe|grot of Daroe]] — the site of [[Teungkoe Koeta Karang]]'s *kaloet* meditations and, later, of [[Teungkoe Panté Gelimah]]'s imitation of the same practice. The *III Moekims Daroe* bordered the [[V Moekims Montassik]] to the east and the Dutch post line at [[Ketapan Doewa|Ketapan Doea]] to the west, and its *hoeloebalang* therefore commanded the approaches to both the western and the southern theatres of operations. The *Imam van Daroe* came in to the Dutch line in response to a summons from the *assistent-resident* in the spring of [[1896]] and "reported that Pa Bintang, the heads, and the population of the [[III Moekims Daroe|3 Moekims Daroe]] were against Oemar" — a report which suggests that Bintang's covert pro-Dutch stance reflected, or was at least consistent with, a broader anti-Umar sentiment in his *landschap*. The territory also formed part of the Dutch lines of communication with the south, and the [[29 May]] [[1896]] show of force under [[C. J. Laceulle|Lt.-Col. Laceulle]] was explicitly directed through the III Moekims Daroe to demonstrate Dutch authority and to confirm Bintang's reliability.

## The 20 June 1896 Submission and the Disarmament Order

The crisis of Bintang's position came on [[20 June]] [[1896]], when — almost three months after Umar's defection and following the heavy Dutch *tuchtigingen* of the IV/VI Moekims and Lam-Krak — he "made his first appearance [since Umar's defection] before the Dutch administration, fearing Umar's revenge". Bintang's fear was realistic: he was a *hoeloebalang* who had privately committed to the Dutch while his neighbouring chiefs ([[T. Rajoet]] of the [[V Moekims Montassik]], [[Toekoe Brahim]] and others) had joined Umar, and Umar's reprisals against perceived Dutch collaborators were well known. Bintang "requested permission to settle with his father-in-law, the [[imam of Lamara]]", within the concentrated line — a request for asylum which the Regeeringscommissaris [[J. A. Vetter|Vetter]] denied, ruling instead "that Bintang should remain in his mukim at [[Poenir]] to protect his subordinates against molest". The decision reflected the Dutch strategic preference for cooperative *hoeloebalang* in situ rather than as refugees within the line. The Regeeringscommissaris's mid-June disarmament order — that guns lent in loan to heads outside the line be returned — produced Bintang's partial compliance: he "returned 12 of 16 *trompladers*" lent to him, retaining four. The partial return was characteristic of Bintang's diplomacy throughout the spring of [[1896]].

## Significance

Panglima Bintang's significance in *ATJEH '96* lies in his embodiment of the *hoeloebalang* who chose the Dutch side privately while maintaining a posture of neutrality publicly, and whose survival depended on the Dutch military umbrella which the spring and summer [[1896]] operations were intended to provide. His [[20 June]] [[1896]] submission — and Vetter's refusal to grant him asylum — illustrates the Dutch strategic preference for cooperative Achehnese chiefs in their home territories rather than as refugees within the line, a preference which would shape the colonial *bindingspolitiek* for the remainder of the war.
