**G. B. Hooyer** was a Dutch military historian and author of *Krijgsgeschiedenis van Nederl.-Indië, 1811-1894* ("War History of Netherlands-India, 1811-1894"), published in three volumes in Batavia in [[1895]], with an atlas in quarto format. The work was offered in [[1902]] by the bookseller Martinus Nijhoff at 15.35 florins in commemoration of the third centenary of the Dutch East India Company.

## Work

Hooyer's comprehensive three-volume war history of the Netherlands Indies covered the entire period from the British interregnum of 1811 to the campaigns of the 1890s, including the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]]. The accompanying atlas provided cartographic documentation of the various military campaigns discussed in the text. The work was part of a tradition of Dutch colonial military historiography that included the more specialised studies of the Atjeh War by [[E. B. Kielstra]].

## Authorship of the Atjeh-Album (1896)

Hooyer was also the principal military correspondent of the illustrated weekly *Eigen Haard* and the author of the historical narrative of the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]] — the bound compilation of illustrated articles that the periodical devoted to the events of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] between [[April]] and [[June]] of that year. The album's preface (*Een Woord Vooraf*) credits him — identified as an *oud-luitenant-kolonel van het N.-I. leger* — with *zijn rijke ervaring van Indische zaken en zijn talent als schrijver* ("his rich experience of Indian affairs and his talent as a writer"). As author of the album, Hooyer is the principal Dutch popular historian of the [[1896]] crisis — the [[Toekoe Oemar's defection (1896)|defection of Toekoe Oemar]] and the relief of the besieged Dutch temporary posts at [[Anagaloeng|Anagaloeng]], [[Senelop|Senelop]], [[Biloel|Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit|Lamkoenjit]].

His narrative voice is that of a military intellectual of the late-nineteenth-century colonial army: critical of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentratie-stelsel]], sceptical of the civil administration of [[Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruys van der Hoeven]], and openly admiring of the *kloek, doortastend* (bold, decisive) generalship of [[K. van der Heyden|Karel van der Heyden]]. The album's biographical portrait of [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] acknowledges his *beschaafde manieren* (civilised manners) and *gaven … als aanvoerder* (gifts as a commander), even as it condemns his *trouweloosheid* (faithlessness). The album's biographical essays on [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Habib Abdoe'r Rachman]] and [[Teungku di Tiro|Teungkoe di Tiro]] — though derivative of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] — treat both figures as serious political actors.

## Account in Hooijer's *De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh* ([[1897]])

**G. B. Hooijer** was a Dutch officer of the [[Oost-Indisch Leger|Netherlands East Indies Army]], retired with the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel (*Gep. Luit.-Kolonel O. I. Leger*), and the author of *[[De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh (Hooijer, 1897)|De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh]]*, an illustrated account of the Dutch outposts established in [[Groot-Atjeh]] during the alliance with [[Teukoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] between [[1893]] and [[1896]]. His book was published with twenty-four plates by [[De Erven F. Bohn]] at Haarlem and [[G. Kolff & Co.]] at Batavia.

## Literary and polemical position

Hooijer prefaced his work with a [[Voorwoord of De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh|Voorwoord]] that was less a preface than a complaint against Dutch national reticence. He wrote that the Netherlands, symbolised by the *Nederlandsche maagd* ("Netherlands maiden"), a modest young woman of good family standing in her little garden with her lion, boasted of translated literature, of paintings known in every quarter of the world and of music honoured abroad — yet her voice was not heard in the *Europeesch Concert*, though she was called the second colonial power; her naval flag was rarely shown, though her merchantmen ploughed every sea.

He observed that the man- and money-devouring war waged for a quarter of a century in North [[Sumatra]] was treated by the nation *als een huishoudelijke onaangenaamheid* — "as a domestic unpleasantness" — about which it preferred to speak as little as possible in public. He noted that the soundest work possessed on the *Atjeh-krijg* covered only the first nine years of the war; that beside it stood only one ethnographic study of the Achehnese; that no literary work of art and no powerful poem had appeared to sing the great struggle; that the only painting relating to the war had been painted by a Frenchman; and that in music the Dutch remained limited to *eenige soldatendeuntjes* ("a few soldiers' ditties").

Hooijer then sketched, in musical terms, the symphony he thought the war deserved: the mysterious beginning full of fear of the European great powers; the martial music with which the first expedition departed, passing into violent battle-noise and suddenly dying away; the stately advance of the second expedition rolling broadly along Acheh's shores; the dissonances after [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]]'s departure with the shrill sounds of the *Allah il Allah*, first drowned out by the war- and victory-song under [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] and afterwards regaining the upper hand; the dull, muffled music representing concealment behind the *[[Geconcentreerde linie|geconcentreerde linie]]*; and finally the peculiar song in which the *Wien Neêrlands bloed* sought to harmonise with the war-music of [[Teukoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]], ending in a suddenly breaking, confused and violent noise from which the Dutch war-song developed ever clearer and higher, to pass at last into a prophetic *vredelied* ("peace-song"). The motif of that peace-song, he wrote, could in truth be the guiding thread through the whole, for the restoration of rest and peace had always been the Dutch aim.

## Method as a historian

Hooijer's narrative was written expressly as a commentary on photographic plates, and he repeatedly reminded the reader of that limitation, declining for example to describe the heavy fighting of April [[1896]] because it lay *buiten het kader van dit tot toelichting der platen geschreven verhaal*. Within that frame, however, he recorded technical, topographical and anecdotal detail with care: garrison strengths, armament, the construction of parapets and wire entanglements, the daily timetable of meals and the *oorlam* ration, the jocular signboards painted above gateways, the identity of officers in group photographs, and the presence of Chinese sutlers, Japanese women at [[Oleh-leh]], convict lantern-lighters and tame sheep accompanying patrols.

He was also willing to preserve uncertainty. On the central question of the book — why [[Teukoe Oemar]] broke his oath — he wrote that the matter *is nog altijd niet uitgemaakt* ("has still not been settled"), offered a hypothesis about the threatened loss of Oemar's indispensability, and closed it with *wie zal het zeggen?* On the death of [[Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro|Teungkoe di Tiro]] he reported the allegation that the *oelama* had been poisoned by his son [[Mat Amin]] only as hearsay, *naar men zeide* ("as it was said").

## Attitudes

Hooijer wrote from an explicitly imperial standpoint. He praised the Indies soldier at length — his courage, contempt of death, indifference to danger and misery, and cheerfulness in spite of *geestdoodende afzondering op *buitenposten* (outer posts)* ("mind-killing isolation on outposts") — observing that some thirty to forty thousand such men preserved order among the millions of subjects the young Queen [[Queen Wilhelmina|Wilhelmina]] counted in her Indies empire, and ending the passage *Eere aan hen!* ("Honour to them!"). He described the Achehnese population as bowed under *anarchie en priesterdwang* ("anarchy and priestly compulsion"), and closed the book with an appeal to the glory of the Indies army and the prestige of the Kingdom of the Netherlands as a great colonial power. His portrait of the Chinese sutler at [[Toengkoeb]] and of the Japanese women at [[Oleh-leh]] employs the racial caricature conventional in Dutch colonial writing of the period.
