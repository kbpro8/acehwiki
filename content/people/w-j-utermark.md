**W. J. Utermark** — published as Ir. W. J. Utermark (i.e., holding the Dutch *Ingenieur* engineering degree; the table of contents gives the initial as "I. W.J. UTERMARK", evidently an OCR artefact for "Ir.") — was a Dutch economist who, in [[1946]], contributed the closing chapter *Indië in de wereldhuishouding* ("The Indies in the World Economy") to the volume *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* edited by Dr. [[C. W. Wormser|Wormser]]. His chapter places the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Dutch East Indies]] in the global network of sea-lanes and ports, and gives [[Sabang]] — the northernmost point of [[Acheh|Sumatra]] — a strategic place in that network.

## Treatment of Sabang and the Atjeh position

Utermark's chapter opens the Indies' geography with the words: "The distance from Sabang, the northernmost point of Sumatra, to the eastern border of Dutch New Guinea, amounts as the crow flies to slightly more than 5000 km." The choice of [[Sabang]] — the port on the island of Weh off the Atjeh coast, terminus of the [[Atjehsche Staatspoorwegen|Atjeh railway]] and site of the [[N.V. Zeehaven en Kolenstation Sabang|Sabang sea-port and coaling station]] — as the western anchor of the Indies' east–west span is a measure of the port's significance in the late-colonial maritime imagination. The same chapter gives the total area of the Netherlands Indies as approximately fifty-five times that of the Netherlands, with a population almost eight times as large, and notes that the entire archipelago, laid out on the map of Europe, "reaches from Ireland to the Caucasus."

In the chapter's discussion of the Indies' principal ports — ordered by total incoming tonnage in [[1938]] — Sabang is listed alongside [[Tandjong Priok]] (Batavia), Semarang, Belawan (the port of [[Deli]]), [[Palembang]], Makassar, Menado, Balikpapan, Tarakan, Pontianak and Bandjarmasin. The list confirms the place of the Atjeh-adjacent Sabang–Belawan port complex in the Indies' late-colonial maritime economy.

## Significance

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Utermark's chief significance is the placement of [[Sabang]] at the head of his east–west geography of the Indies. The choice is not incidental: Sabang had been developed since the late nineteenth century as a coaling station and free port on the western entrance to the Strait of Malacca, and its strategic position — overlooking the sea-lane between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea — gave it an importance out of all proportion to the small population of the Weh island group. By naming Sabang (rather than, say, [[Kutaradja|Koeta Radja]] or [[Deli]]'s [[Medan]]) as the western anchor of the Indies, Utermark underlines the strategic conception that had informed Dutch colonial policy in northern Sumatra since the [[Aceh War]].
