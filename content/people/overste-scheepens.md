**Overste Scheepens** — *Toean Scheepens* in the Acehnese idiom of the old hunter [[Datoek]] — was a Dutch *overste* (colonel) of the [[K.N.I.L.]] and a Marechaussee commander of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen]] in [[Atjeh|Aceh]]. He appears in two distinct roles in the sources: as one of the great patrol commanders of the war, sixth-named in [[Musket]]'s list of the Marechaussee commanders in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]*; and as the *overste* wounded by an Acehnese during a *rechtzitting* (court session) in [[1913]] who died of his wounds twenty years later in [[1933]], as recorded in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]*.

## The List of Marechaussee Commanders

Scheepens is the sixth-named in [[Musket]]'s list: "Gilles Graafland, Van Daalen, Campioni, Christoffel, Van der Maaten, Scheepens, Watrin, Webb, Colijn, Geertsema Beckering, Darlang and the sergeants Nussy, Salowono, Pongoh … a colourful array." The names of these famous fighters and Marechaussee commanders rang through the Indies like carillon music, and their stories of brave deeds, adventures, and peculiarities went the rounds as if they were legends.

## Datoek's Close Comrades

"I was a 'heavy friend' with Mister Christoffel, and Mister General Van Heutsz, and Mister Scheepens, and Mister Darlang, and Mister Boreel, and Mister Governor Swart. Very many, and all of them truly brave." Datoek's list of his close comrades includes Toean Scheepens — the Acehnese idiom for Scheepens, the colonial memory of the Marechaussee commander who was a close comrade of the old hunter.

## The wounding (1913)

"Scheepens 1913 tijdens een rechtzitting dodelijk..." The *Overste* Scheepens was wounded in [[1913]] during a *rechtzitting* — a court session in the bivak — by an Acehnese wielding an Acehnese *[[reuntjong]]*. The wounding of the *overste* during a *rechtzitting* was an attack on the colonial army's judicial authority.

## The death (1933)

"Of this kind of wound Lieutenant-Colonel Scheepens died in 1933." The *Overste* Scheepens died of his wounds in [[1933]], twenty years after the wounding. The long delay is the book's marker of the lethality of the Acehnese *reuntjong* wound: a wound that could kill in [[1913]] or in [[1933]]. The *reuntjong* wound was therefore both an immediate and a long-term danger: the soldaat who was wounded by the *reuntjong* might die of the wound decades later. Scheepens is therefore the marker of the long tail of the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Aceh War]]'s wounds.

## Career under Swart

Swart placed Scheepens, after captain [[G. K. Dijkstra|Dijkstra]], at [[Sigli]], the most important troop command on the north coast. It is a mark, du Croo observed, of how difficult Swart's striving towards his civil end-goal was, that even a man like Scheepens found in [[1913]], in Swart's civil construction and in the constant increase of civil interference and civil officials, reason "to long for the end."

Scheepens's last letters to [[H. J. Schmidt]] before his death are quoted in the biography, including his account of the accidental killing of the unfindable [[Pang Bintang]] in July [[1913]] in the [[XXII Moekims]]: "His capture was such a stroke of luck as I have seldom seen … A few friends will be punished, but unfortunately the greatest thieves go free…" His last letter to Schmidt is dated [[10 August]] [[1913]].

He also reported to Swart in [[1911]] the death of [[Tgk. Ma'at]].

## The murder of 10 October 1913

The [[Murder of Lieutenant-Colonel Scheepens (10 October 1913)|murder of Scheepens]] arose from a trivial dispute over coconuts. In October [[1913]] [[Teukoe Béntara Titeuë]], son of the *oelèëbalang* of the [[II Moekims Titeuë]] in Pidië, walking through the *gampong* with a few followers, grew thirsty and ordered one of them to climb a coconut palm standing on an Acehnese's yard and pick a few coconuts. The owner, angered that the *oelèëbalang*'s son had not asked his permission, refused to allow it. The young Teukoe in his turn grew angry because in his view the picking was the *oelèëbalang*'s right, whereupon the owner threatened to bring his *parang* down on his skull. The Teukoe persisted in his order, and the owner did indeed strike him over the skull with the *parang*, causing a bleeding head wound.

The perpetrator was arrested and brought to Sigli to stand trial before the *[[Moesapat]]* (*adat* court). On the day of the trial [[T. Béntara Titeuë]] went to Sigli; at the entrance to the civil office the armed police servant on sentry duty took from him the visibly worn, gold-ornamented *[[rentjong]]* (dagger).

The *Moesapat* ("customary-law court") sat under the presidency of Overste Scheepens in his capacity as Civil Authority at Sigli. Scheepens asked the *adjunct-djaksa* (assistant native public prosecutor) what his demand was, and he demanded the maximum penalty for slight wounding known to Dutch criminal law, three months' imprisonment. One of the *oelèëbalangs* on the bench asked the Muhammadan adviser of the court what punishment he considered fitting. The Teungkoe answered according to Acehnese *[[adat]]*: *"Loeka oelón sipat, darah oelón soekat, lhò' oelón takat, diët lhëë ploh reunggèt"* — "the wound I have measured, the blood I have measured, the depth of the wound I have sounded; the blood-price is thirty dollars."

Hearing this, T. Béntara Titeuë sprang up in fury and abused the Teungkoe as *"Kaphé"* (unbeliever). "Jij weet heel goed," cried the *oelèëbalang*, "dat ik volgens onzen *adat* ("customary law") het recht heb niet alleen den man zelf, die het bloed van mijn zoon vergoten heeft, af te maken maar zelfs diens geheele familie." He told the President that he understood that Acehnese *adat* could no longer be applied to the perpetrator, but that if the man were not punished with at least banishment to [[Java]], he, T. Béntara, would be *[[maloe]]* (shamed) in the eyes of his people.

In vain the Civil Authority tried to explain to the *oelèëbalang* that his son, by improper conduct, was the principal culprit, and that Dutch law did not permit banishment for slight wounding. The *oelèëbalang* persisted in his demand, and when the Moesapat pronounced the penalty of three months, T. Béntara Titeuë suddenly drew a concealed knife from under his clothes and stabbed Overste Scheepens in the belly.

The Overste fell backwards to the ground; the *adjunct-djaksa* rushed forward, but had as his only weapon a wooden ruler. Then an armed police servant, an Acehnese, drew his *[[klewang]]* and struck T. Béntara Titeuë over the head. The *oelèëbalang* fell and died almost at once. Scheepens, having got to his feet, forbade any harm to be done to the *oelèëbalang*, but it was already too late.

With his hand pressed on the abdominal wound, Overste Scheepens walked on foot to the military sick-bay, saying: "that has gone well a couple of times, but this time it has gone wrong." The *officier van gezondheid* at Sigli gave first aid, and Scheepens was bandaged and put to bed in the sick-bay.

Swart was at once informed by telephone and travelled to Sigli by special tram with the well-known surgeon [[Dubinsky|dr. Dubinsky]]. But surgical intervention could no longer help. Scheepens died on [[17 October]] [[1913]].

At the burial in the heroes' cemetery [[Peutjoet]], Swart delivered the funeral oration — and then, for perhaps the first time in all those hard Atjeh years, Swart lost mastery of his nerves: "the address had to be broken off."

The monthly report closed: "Peace and order in the territory of [[Titeuë]] were not disturbed for a moment by what had occurred."

## Significance (du Croo, 1943)

The murder of Scheepens was, in du Croo's account, the deepest personal blow of Swart's governorship, and one of the "zware tegenslagen" he had to absorb alone, together with the flaring of resistance in the [[Daja]] districts and the sudden attack on [[G. F. P. Gosenson|Gosenson]]. Indirectly it also brought [[H. J. Schmidt]] back to Atjeh from leave before his time, since Swart could now demand a wholly suitable officer for the difficult Meulaboh post — "only by a chance — and that was, alas, the murder of Scheepens."

The case remained the classic Dutch illustration of how even an Acehnese *landschapshoofd* could be driven to such a murder by the collision of *adat* honour with colonial criminal law.
