**Habib Abdoerrachman el Zahir** — properly *Sejjid Abdurrachman Zahir*, and known to the Dutch and the Acehnese alike simply as *de Habib* (the Habib) — was an Arab *sayyid* of prophetic descent whose deep religious learning and political gifts made him, in the words of [[H.C. Zentgraff|Zentgraff]], one of the most dangerous and influential leaders of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh resistance]] in the last years of the 1870s. He was, with [[Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro]] after him, one of the two great figures who in that early phase succeeded in drawing the fragmented land towards anything resembling unified leadership, preaching the *prang sabil* (holy war) and drawing thousands of fighters to his standard.

The Dutch colonial government considered him such a dangerous adversary that, rather than continue the military contest, it resolved to remove him from [[Aceh|Atjeh]] by negotiation. His submission was celebrated in [[Batavia]] and [[Koetaradja]] as a political success: on [[13 October]] [[1878]] he formally submitted on the condition that he settle in [[Mekka]] with an annual pension, charged to the colonial budget, of no less than 10,000 dollars. On [[24 November]] [[1878]] he was embarked for Djeddah on board Hr. Ms. *Curaçao*. The Atjeh officers of the day sang of this "costly buy-off" of the Habib in the *Falderaldera-leed* that Major Macleod — husband of Mata Hari, then a lieutenant in Atjeh — later recalled:

> *"Daar ligt een ijzeren zeekasteel, / Dat heet de 'Curaçao', / Waarmee die brave Habib / Naar Mekka varen zou. / Nu zingt hij vroolijk 'falderaldera, / Voor dit Gouvernement. / Zoo'n duizend dollars in de maand: / Ben ik geen knappe vent?"*

## The return to Atjeh, March 1876 (Meijer, 1883)

The official field reports gathered by [[Kolonel Meijer|H. F. Meijer]] in *[[Atjeh van 26 December 1875 tot 4 September 1876]]* ([[1883]]) describe how the Habib re-entered the war. About mid-March [[1876]] the Dutch political agent at Penang, [[Lavino]], reported that the Arab Habib Abdoer Rachman ben Zahir — for a short time chief minister (*rijksbestierder*) of the late Sultan and afterwards a member of the notorious Council of Eight (*Raad van achten*) at [[Penang]] — had escaped the island despite all the precautions of the British police and of the Dutch agent, disguising himself as a sailor and shipping on a schooner trading to Atjeh's north coast. He landed near [[Edi]]; the [[Radja of Edi besar]] confirmed his arrival to the Dutch officials there.

His journey from the little coastal states to the focus of resistance in Groot-Atjeh was a triumph. His enemies soon circulated that a debt of about 5,000 dollars had driven him out of Penang; but his following in the dependencies grew daily. On three successive meetings in Groot-Atjeh he made the principal chiefs of the three [[Sagi]]s hand in written declarations of their wishes and confirm them by oaths, and he bound himself personally — at the chiefs' own request, by his later declarations — to take the conduct of the war. He organised payment and feeding of the fighters who rallied under his banners, levied a war contribution (*oorlogsschatting*) of 30,000 dollars, and sold gunpowder at 4 dollars per forty-odd catties to the population.

## The strategy and the unexecuted general attacks (Meijer, 1883)

Meijer presents the Habib's strategic thinking in the metaphor of the *laboe* plant with which, he noted, the Atjehers compared the E. I. Company: a little seed, carelessly thrown into a corner of a garden, becomes a creeping plant that strangles every smaller plant beneath its foliage; cutting here and there at the sprouts only strengthens it — the root itself must be torn out. Only a combined general attack by united forces, in mutual concert, on the Dutch troops could crush the Company's dominion in Atjeh.

Yet the Habib never succeeded in bringing about the general attack in his own spirit. On [[25 July]] [[1876]] came the first report that he had entered the [[XXII Moekims]] with 2,000 men; his force was later put at 15,000. Spy reports of August said he was at kampong Lamsi near [[Panglima Polim]], holding a great assembly and ordering the people to store rice; that he had prescribed his fighters a three-day fast before attacking; that at the mesigit of Montasih he gathered the chief leaders — Panglima Polim, [[Toewankoe Moeda Daoed]] and [[Toewankoe Hasjim]] — and that, after prayers at the Mesigit Raija which he claimed to have built himself near the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]], a divine revelation had pointed out to him the 27th of the month Redjeb ([[17 August|17]]–[[18 August]] [[1876]]) as the favourable day for a general attack, whose three-fold design the spies claimed to know. Dutch dispositions were made for that night — ambushes everywhere, two companies with artillery posted behind the [[Pedir-dijk]] — but the night passed everywhere undisturbed; nor did the second announced date, [[30 August]], when Lambaroe and Oleh Karang were to be overwhelmed by 3,000 men in a break into the IV Moekims, bring any attack. Meijer judged: all the partial actions of the guerilla had only served to show the Dutch minority in brilliant relief and to harden the Atjeher in his resistance; had the Habib succeeded in bringing the mass of the enemy before the Dutch troops and leading it himself, one serious field battle might have ended the war — in which sense it would almost have been desirable that he had succeeded.

His appearance nevertheless changed the war: the greater activity of the guerilla after March [[1876]], chiefs who nearly crossed over now rejoining the resistance, the letters to the chiefs of the IV Moekims sent by [[Toekoe Tjihiq Lamngga]] — son-in-law of the well-known [[Toekoe Nanta]] — were ascribed to him, as were the reported instructions to the principal chiefs of the enemy to fatigue the Dutch everywhere by partial actions until his great plan should be ripe.

## The 1884 memorandum

Even from Mekka, where he consumed his handsome annual stipend, the Habib could not detach himself from the Atjeh question. In [[1884]] he sent from Mekka a memorandum to the Netherlands-Indies Government outlining a scheme by which the submission of Atjeh might at last be achieved. Dated 3 Moharram 1302 — [[October]] [[1884]] — and signed *HABIB ABDOERRACHMAN EL ZAHIR* under a seal reading "Rijksbestierder van het Gouvernement ATJEH" (Chief Minister of the Government of Atjeh), the memorandum proposed that the Government appoint a Muslim distinguished by personal excellence, noble descent, and knowledge of Atjeh affairs as *regent* — under the title of *Radja* or similar — to govern the internal affairs of the country on behalf of the Netherlands-Indies Government, in cooperation with a college of chiefs, scholars, and notables, and within a precisely defined commission set out in his diploma. The Habib naturally saw himself as the coming *regent* of Atjeh; the seal he had had cut already named him so.

In Mekka he remained in regular contact with Atjeh pilgrims, who came to him for counsel as well as for the rites of the *hadj*. Zentgraff notes that this expectation, seen from the Dutch side, may look absurd and arrogant, but must be read in the light of an age when the colonial government itself judged the Habib's voluntary withdrawal from Atjeh so important that it paid him a princely annual stipend for Arabia.

## Significance

The Habib's removal in [[1878]] closed the first phase of unified religious-political leadership of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh resistance]], but it also opened the era of the [[Tiro-oelama|Tiro-oelama's]]. As Zentgraff puts it: after the Habib's period "our weak government received more slaps in the face; the Tiro-oelama's took care of that." His memorandum of [[1884]] is preserved as one of the curiosities of the early diplomatic record of the war — a sayyid's proposal that the Netherlands-Indies Government install him, with a sultan's title, as its Muslim chief minister over Atjeh.
