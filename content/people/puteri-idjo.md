**Puteri Idjo** — also known as **Putri Hijau**, "the Green Princess" — is the fairy-tale queen of [[Deli]] and [[Asahan]] whose legend, as preserved by P. de Roo de la Faille, is the central Acehnese legend of the east-coast polities: a sultan of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] pressed a marriage suit upon her; she refused, sickened, and died; her body was sent in a "glass coffin" (*glazen kist*) to Atjeh by sea, but the ship and its cargo were swallowed up en route. The legend is bound, in De Roo de la Faille's account, to the [[Kota Djawa|Javanese fortification]] at [[Deli]] and to the Buddhist goddess **Cyama-Tara**, the "Redster out of all woe" whom De Roo de la Faille identifies as the patroness of [[Sumatra]] itself.

## The legend

The story, as De Roo de la Faille records it, survives in both [[Deli]] and [[Asahan]] tradition. Puteri Idjo was a queen on whose name the building of the [[Kota Djawa]] is laid; its remains — recorded by [[Anderson]] in his *Mission to the East Coast of Sumatra* ([[1825]]) as "remains of a Javanese fortification" along the road from [[Medan]] to [[Belawan]], at Deli-Toewa — De Roo de la Faille would rather read as "of [[Djawaka|Djawaka]]", i.e. of the Sumatran-origin Javanese. She is "the fairy-tale queen who was troubled by the Sultan of Atjeh with a marriage proposal; but she refused, became ill, died, whereupon her corpse was conveyed in a 'glass coffin' to Atjeh; on the way ship and all were swallowed up".

## Decoding: *idjo* and *sijama*

For De Roo de la Faille the name *idjo* — "green" — is a *bedekte taal* form of the Kawi word *sijama*. Her realm is "the sijam-Awak of the Djawaka"; she herself is the earthly likeness of the Heavenly Queen, the Buddhist goddess **Cyama-Tara**, "Redster out of all Woe, patroness of the island of Sumatra". Her original seat was the islet in the bay of [[Aroe]] — [[Poelo Kampi]] — and in later times the negorij of the radja at the mouth of the river of [[Tandjoengpoera]], at [[Kwala Boeboen|Koewala Boeboen]]. The Atjehnese sultan's pursuit of her, on this reading, encodes the Atjehnese sultanate's design upon the east-coast polities — a design whose outcome, the legend insists, was frustrated not by war but by the queen's death and the loss of her body to the sea.

## The glass coffin

The detail of the "glass coffin" (*glazen kist*) in which the queen's body was sent to Atjeh, and its disappearance at sea, has no parallel in the standard chronicles but is, in De Roo de la Faille's reading, a *bedekte taal* marker: the "glass" coffin is the sea itself, the *tasik* of the *Taroema* allegory, and the loss of the ship is the swallowing-up of the old east-coast sacred complex by the Atjehnese maritime advance. The episode thus closes, in symbolic form, the same historical process — the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|absorption of the east-coast kingdoms by Atjeh]] — that the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)]] records in military form.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, Puteri Idjo is the most resonant single legend tying the [[Sultan of Atjeh]] to the [[Deli]]-[[Asahan]]-[[Aroe]] cultural zone, and the only one that frames the Atjeh-east-coast relationship in dynastic-marital rather than military terms. Her identification with the goddess Cyama-Tara also makes her the principal instance of De Roo de la Faille's broader claim that the Buddhist goddess-cult of pre-Islamic Sumatra survived, in folk form, into the chronicle and romance tradition of the Atjehnese age.
