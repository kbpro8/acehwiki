W. F. J. Kroon was a Dutch colonial administrator who held the position of *Assistent-Resident ter beschikking van den Gouverneur van Atjeh* (Assistant-Resident at the disposal of the Governor of Atjeh) during the period of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]]. This designation placed Kroon in a distinctive category within the colonial administrative hierarchy: rather than being assigned to a specific territorial division with defined responsibilities, he served as a senior official available for assignment by the Governor as circumstances required. The *ter beschikking* (at disposal) arrangement was a feature of the Dutch colonial personnel system that allowed for flexibility in staffing, particularly in regions like Atjeh where the security situation and administrative needs could shift rapidly. Kroon was present at the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) held at [[Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]], though no recorded remarks from him appear in the official minutes of these proceedings.

## The Ter Beschikking Role in Atjeh

The position of *Assistent-Resident ter beschikking* carried particular significance in the context of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]], where decades of military conflict had created an administrative landscape that was both complex and volatile. An official in this capacity could be deployed to oversee specific operations, conduct investigations, provide temporary coverage for absent officials, or assist with special projects such as the reorganization then underway. However, as noted by [[C. Lulofs]] during the earlier Medan meetings, the *ter beschikking* arrangement had sometimes led to friction with *besturende ambtenaren* (administrative officials) who held defined territorial responsibilities. Officials without fixed portfolios could inadvertently encroach upon the authority of those with clear mandates, creating confusion about jurisdiction and chains of command. The tension inherent in the role reflected a broader structural challenge within the colonial administration: how to maintain the flexibility needed to respond to dynamic conditions while preserving the clarity of authority essential for effective governance.

Kroon's presence at the Koeta-Radja meetings, despite the absence of recorded remarks, indicates that he was considered a sufficiently important figure to be included in the highest-level discussions about the future of Atjeh's administrative structure. His position as an assistant-resident placed him in the senior tier of the colonial civil service, and his assignment to the Governor's personal disposal suggests that he enjoyed a degree of trust and confidence from [[H. N. A. Swart|Gouverneur Swart]]. The fact that he was present for both days of the meetings further underscores his involvement in the deliberative process, even if his contributions were not captured in the formal record. It is possible that he participated in side discussions, provided counsel informally, or served as an observer whose understanding of the proceedings would be valuable in subsequent implementation of the reforms.

## Context of the Koeta-Radja Meetings

The *bestuursvergaderingen* at [[Koeta-Radja]] represented a critical juncture in the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]], bringing together the senior administrative and military officials of the Atjeh government to discuss the implementation of sweeping reforms to the colonial administrative structure. These meetings followed earlier deliberations in Medan and were part of a broader initiative driven from Batavia to reorganize the governance of the Netherlands East Indies. For officials like Kroon, whose *ter beschikking* status meant that his future assignments would be directly affected by the outcome of the reorganization, the discussions held profound professional implications. The reorganization proposed changes to the territorial boundaries of *afdeelingen* (divisions), the reporting relationships between officials, and the fundamental structure of governance in Atjeh—all of which would reshape the operational environment in which he and his colleagues worked. His attendance at these meetings, even without recorded contributions, places him within the circle of officials who were directly engaged with and affected by the transformative administrative reforms being debated.
