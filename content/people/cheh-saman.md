The right-hand man of Teungku Muhamat Amin and eventually his successor as leader of the Tiro religious tradition, son of a simple leube. He became a leading figure in the holy war in Great Acheh until his death, after which his son Teungku Mat Amin succeeded him. His rival Teungku Kuta Karang contemptuously called him "Leube Saman" rather than "Teungku Tiro."

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Cheh Saman (Shaikh Samman)

**Cheh Saman**, also known as **Shaikh Samman** or, after his succession, as **Teungku Saman** and finally **Teungku Tiro**, was the collateral relative and right-hand man of the first [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro Muhamat Amin]], whom he succeeded in December [[1885]] as the head *ulama* of [[Tiro]] in [[Pidie]]. Under him the office of Teungku Tiro attained its greatest prominence; he became, in the words of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], "the famous Teungku Tiro, who died in January [[1891]]" and the most powerful religious leader of the Achehnese war against the Dutch.

When Teungku Tiro Muhamat Amin died, the son of the deceased — though a good scholar — was still too young to take his father's place. The designation of Teungku Tiro therefore passed to Cheh Saman, who had long been the dead man's right-hand man. Favoured by the progress of the war, this man gained for himself a unique position. The Sultan's *chab sikureung* (nine-fold seal) could do no more than set the stamp of officialdom on power he had already won. The authority given him over religious questions in [[Great Acheh]] was just as vague as the definition of the judicial power of the Habib at the establishment of the *bale meunahakamali*, and gave equally free play to the natural course of affairs.

Cheh Saman did not concern himself about the insignificant Sultan, nor, except in so far as was absolutely necessary, about the *uleebalangs*. As the representative of religious law he could assume full powers, and none dared show him open hostility. He worked himself up to the position of commander-in-chief in the war against the Gompeuni (Dutch) and wished to arm himself against the jealousy of the *adat*-chiefs by an official authorisation of the Sultan, whereby he was placed on a level with the *uleebalangs* — or, as he preferred to interpret it, over their heads. The "court" at [[Keumala]] gave him, cautiously enough, a deed of appointment which in appearance made no inroad on the rights of the *adat*-chiefs, since it limited his province to religious matters; but all parties were well aware that in the view of the *ulamas* everything came under the head of religion. Even after this formal appointment Cheh Saman submitted to no legal dictation from Keumala, and refused on more than one occasion to satisfy the Sultan's demands for money contributions.

Among his rivals were [[Habib Samalanga]], the sayyid who came to [[Great Acheh]] to direct the jihad especially in the [[XXVI Mukims]], and [[Teungku Kutakarang]]. The latter had once been banished from the capital in the Sultan's name for the strangeness of his decisions on points of law; in the war he worked at first side by side with — or even under — Cheh Saman, but in the circle of his intimates spoke contemptuously of the great man as **"Leube Saman,"** criticising his rules and decisions, and certainly felt but little sorrow at the death of that honoured and dreaded *ulama*. Teungku Kutakarang opposed Cheh Saman's teaching that the prohibition against wearing gold or silk applied to combatants in the holy war, and classed all his good works — the building of mosques, the repair of chapels, the great *kanduris* — as mere "louse-questions."

Cheh Saman died in January [[1891]], still in the full vigour of life, after a sudden illness. He is said to have expressed the conviction that poison had been administered to him by some agent of his son [[Mat Amin]] — a *teungku* by title and a guerrilla chief by trade, who had lived openly with another man's wife and had been forbidden access to his father's presence. Notwithstanding this, Cheh Saman had Mat Amin summoned to his bedside when dying, and solemnly warned him to go to the devout *ulamas* for advice and to forsake his evil ways. He seemed however to realise how little chance there was of his son's ever proving his true successor in any respect; and indeed, when after Cheh Saman's death Mat Amin gave the rein more than ever to his evil passions, the great body of the adherents of Teungku Tiro at once melted away.

Cheh Saman left no true successor in the office of Teungku Tiro. His son Mat Amin was killed in action in [[1896]] when the Dutch troops captured the fortress of [[Aneu' Galong]]. Two other sons — [[Teungku Beb]] (died [[1900]]) and [[Teungku Mahidin]] — together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died [[1901]]), much respected for his piety and learning, continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the *sabil* contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]; but Great Acheh no longer furnished a field for their labour.
