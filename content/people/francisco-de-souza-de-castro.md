**Francisco de Souza de Castro** was a Portuguese nobleman (*Portugeesche edelman*) who, at the time of [[Pieter Soury|Pieter Soury]]'s [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|embassy to Atjeh]] in [[1641]], was a prisoner in [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. His captivity in the sultanate was a small but consequential piece of the European diplomatic chessboard at the moment when the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]], [[Acheh|Atjeh]] and [[Johor|Djohor]] had just cooperated to drive the Portuguese from [[Malakka]] (also in [[1641]]), and when Portugal had just been freed from Spain (in [[1640]]).

## The Atjeh captivity

Godee Molsbergen's reference is brief: "Toentertijd zat in Atjeh een Portugeesch edelman, Francisco de Souza de Castro, gevangen" — At that time there sat a Portuguese nobleman, Francisco de Souza de Castro, prisoner in Atjeh. The cause of his imprisonment is not stated in *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie*, but the fact of a high-born Portuguese captive in Atjeh in [[1641]] is itself historically plausible: the Atjeh sultans had been at war with Portuguese Malakka for the better part of a century, and Portuguese prisoners of rank were routinely held for ransom or exchange.

## Connection with Soury's mission

De Castro's captivity had a direct bearing on [[Pieter Soury|Soury]]'s embassy. At an audience attended by the *[[Panglima|Panglima's]]* of [[Pedir]] and [[Deli]] and many other Atjeh nobles, the chiefs asked whether the Dutch had brought "Portugeesche jonge dochterkens" — Portuguese maidens — to be delivered as *losprijs* (ransom). The request is treated separately under [[Maagden als losprijs]]. The Atjeh court clearly associated the Dutch visit with the prospect of a Portuguese exchange, and De Castro's person was part of that calculation.

The diplomatic clarification that [[Pieter Soury|Soury]]'s embassy was instructed to provide — that Portugal in [[1640]] had been freed from Spain, was no longer the Republic's mortal enemy, and that the Republic had concluded a ten-year truce with Portugal the following year — also bears on De Castro's situation. The Dutch-Portuguese truce complicated the Atjeh court's calculation of what to do with its Portuguese captive.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, De Castro is significant as the named Portuguese prisoner whose captivity intersected with the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy]] of [[1641]] and with the Atjeh court's wider negotiations over ransom and exchange. His case is one example of how the long Atjeh-Portuguese conflict produced European captives in the sultanate, and of how those captives became pawns in the sultanate's subsequent diplomacy with the Dutch.
