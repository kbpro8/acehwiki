**H. N. A. Swart** — *Hendrik Nikolaas Albert Swart* — known in the Acehnese idiom of the old hunter [[Datoek]] as *Toean Gouverneur Swart* — was a Dutch **lieutenant-general of the infantry** (*Luitenant-Generaal der Infanterie*) of the [[Royal Netherlands East Indies Army|K.N.I.L.]] and colonial administrator who, as **Civil and Military Governor of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]]** from [[1908]] to [[1918]], restored conciliatory governance after the harsh policies of his predecessor [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] had provoked the [[Atjéh-enquête|1907–1908 inquiry]] and a fierce flare-up of armed resistance, and who finally handed a peaceful Aceh over to a civilian Governor in [[1918]] — thereby ending what G. W. T. Dames calls "the lingering disease: the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Atjeh War]]" ("the lingering disease: the Aceh War"). He was the final military governor of [[Atjeh|Aceh]], the *Aceh-connoisseur* whose ten-year administration — "order and peace were consolidated in that province" — was the phase that "brought the [[Aceh War]] to an end," and who throughout his career "altijd en overal het meest voelde voor breken van het verzet zonder bloedvergieten" — "always and everywhere most preferred to break resistance without bloodshed". [[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]]'s *Atjèh* ([[1923]]) catalogues the administrative reforms of his governorship — the restoration of the *Hasil-reglement*, the reform of *wasé oelèëbalang*, the removal of *bestuursbemoeienis* from private-law pepper contracts, and the rapid expansion of *volksonderwijs* — in detail. In the received memory of the late-colonial Dutch community of the Indies, Swart came to be known as the *Pacificator van Atjeh* ("Pacifier of Atjeh") — a title fixed by the elegy of F. Kunst that laments the post-war destruction of his life's work.

> **Note on nomenclature**: One source — the *Notulen der bestuursvergaderingen* of [[1914]] — gives the initials as "H. N. N. A. Swart" (with two N's); all other sources, including Dames, Lamster, and the Encyclopaedisch Bureau, give "H. N. A. Swart". The biography *Generaal Swart* by [[Maurice Henri du Croo|du Croo]] and [[Schmidt|Kolonel Schmidt]] identifies him as **Hendrik Nikolaas Albert Swart**. The double-N form is almost certainly a transcription or OCR variant of the same name.

## Early Career and the Case Against Van Daalen

Swart rose through the ranks during the [[Aceh War|Aceh War]], serving as a young officer under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and earning praise for his energy and sound judgment. [[Hendrikus Colijn|Hendrikus Colijn]], who worked closely with him, described Swart as a "goed verstand, vlot en krachtig werker" ("good sense, fluent and powerful worker") with a talent for handling people. When Van Heutsz proposed [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] as his successor in the Aceh governorship in [[1904]], Colijn privately favoured Swart as the more suitable candidate, though he conceded that Van Daalen's seniority and military experience made him the inevitable choice. Van Heutsz himself acknowledged Swart's abilities but initially hesitated to promote him because of his relatively junior rank.

## Service in Borneo and Celebes

In [[1905]], Van Heutsz appointed Swart as civil and military resident of the [[South and East Division of Borneo|South and East Division of Borneo]] (South and East Division of Borneo), where he successfully suppressed a long-standing rebellion by reorganising the military patrols and restoring order without relying on unreliable Dayak auxiliaries; by [[1906]] the area was pacified. He was later appointed Governor of [[Celebes|Celebes]] and its dependencies, where he oversaw the pacification of [[Boni|Boni]], [[Kingdom of Loewoe|Kingdom of Loewoe]], and other recalcitrant states, implemented the [[Korte Verklaring|Korte Verklaring]] (Short Declaration), and established *landschapskassen* (territorial treasuries). His methodical approach earned him the respect of both the local population and the central government.

## Succession to the Aceh Governorship, 1908

Following the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] and Van Daalen's resignation, Van Heutsz turned to Swart as the obvious successor, at a moment when the territory was still shaken by the [[1907]]–[[1908]] flare-up of armed resistance that Van Daalen's harsh policies had provoked. Swart took office in [[1908]] and immediately reversed Van Daalen's punitive measures: he restored the authority of the *oelèëbalangs* (Acehnese territorial chiefs), reduced the arbitrary fines and released unjustly imprisoned chiefs, and fostered cooperation between the administration and local leaders, while continuing the economic and infrastructural development Van Daalen had initiated, now within a framework of trust rather than coercion.

His response was double in character: first to demonstrate, in the early years of his administration, that Dutch *overmacht* (overwhelming power) remained unshakeable; then, having established that fact, to pivot to a strategy of reconciliation and trust-building. The *volksleiders* (people's leaders) who had been held down by force were "steeds meer ook uit overtuiging aan onze zijde gebracht" — "increasingly brought to the Dutch side by conviction rather than coercion" — and the result was the *periode van rust* (period of rest) that allowed the *tal van maatregelen ter bevordering van den economischen vooruitgang* ("the many measures for the promotion of economic progress") described in *De Buitenbezittingen*.

## The Letter from the Three Hoofden (1910–1911)

Swart's preferred method in the closing years of the Acehnese resistance was to bring moral and political pressure on the remaining resisters through the most authoritative Acehnese leaders rather than military force alone. At his request, "de drie voornaamste Atjehse hoofden" ("the three principal Acehnese chiefs") — two members of the Sultan's family, including [[Toeankoe Machmoed Radja Keumala|Toeankoe Machmoed Radja Keumala]], and the former Sultan's *legeraanvoerder* (army commander) [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] (who had surrendered to [[Hendrikus Colijn|kapitein Colijn]]) — wrote a letter urging the [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri|Tiriō Teungkoes]] to submit. "De Teungkoes bespraken de brief, doch besloten tot het einde te vechten; hen zou geschieden naar Allah's wil" — "The Teungkoes discussed the letter, but resolved to fight to the end; what happens to them will be according to Allah's will." The diplomatic track failed; the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]] was needed to finish the work militarily.

The episode is the clearest example of Swart's preference for negotiation even when he had the military power to crush. He mobilised the most authoritative remaining Acehnese political and religious voices before resorting to the final military solution — a reflection of the *verzoenende en vertrouwenwekkende politiek* (conciliatory and confidence-building policy) that defined his governorship.

## The Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913–1914)

As the highest-ranking Dutch colonial official in [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] during the critical period of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)|Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913–1914)]], Swart played a central role in articulating the unique governance challenges that the territory posed to the broader reform agenda. His detailed explanations of Atjeh's indigenous administrative structures and his candid assessments of the prospects for *zelfbestuur* (self-governance) in the residency made him an indispensable voice in the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) convened by Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff|S. de Graaff]] at [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[20 January|20 January]] [[1914]].

### Views on Atjeh's Governance

Swart declared that the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] could not have local *zelfbestuur* in the standard form because of the territory's distinctive administrative composition. He explained that only [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and part of the *onderafdeeling* (sub-district) [[Singkel|Singkel]] constituted *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* (directly ruled territory), while the remainder of the governorate consisted of *Inlandsche Zelfbesturen* (Native Self-Governments, also referred to as *landschappen*). This meant that the conventional model of decentralised governance being proposed for other parts of the Netherlands East Indies could not be applied uniformly in Atjeh without significant modifications.

Regarding the appointment of indigenous officials, Swart stated that *erfelijkheid* (heredity) in Atjeh *hoofd* (head) appointments existed but was increasingly being set aside in favour of *bekwaamheden* (competence) as the decisive factor. He explained that while the local population was consulted and their recommendations were heard, these could be overridden if the *rechthebbende* (claimant) lacked the required qualifications for the position. Swart expressed confidence that with proper *opleiding* (training), within a small number of years the necessary *Inlandsche bestuurskrachten* (Native administrative forces) would be obtained in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. However, he also raised probing questions about the proposed administrative structure, asking whether the *ambtelijk element* (bureaucratic element) in the proposed [[Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra|Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra]] (Regional Council of North Sumatra) would be overly dominant, and whether the rank of [[Civiel Gezaghebber|Civiel Gezaghebber]] (Civil Authority Holder) would be open to military officers with ambition — questions that reflected his concern about maintaining an appropriate balance between civilian and military authority in a territory where the two had long been intertwined.

### The Sagi System Explanation

One of Swart's most significant contributions to the *bestuursvergaderingen* was his detailed explanation of the indigenous administrative hierarchy of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], prompted by De Graaff's inquiry about institutions comparable to Palembang's *marga*'s. Swart described a three-tiered system comprising three *[[sagi|sagi]]*'s, which he compared to Javanese *regentschappen* (regencies); *[[peleebalangschap|peleebalangschap]]pen*, which he compared to *districten* (districts); and *[[moekimschap|moekimschap]]pen*, which he compared to *onderdistricten* (sub-districts). The comparison to Javanese administrative units was deliberate, as it allowed officials whose experience was primarily in Java to grasp the scale and function of Atjehnese institutions through analogy with the more widely understood *desa* and *regentschap* system of Java. The three-*sagi* federation — of the [[XXV Moekims|XXV]], [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI]], and [[XXII Moekims|XXII]] *moekim*s — was the principal indigenous-administrative description on the basis of which [[S. de Graaff|S. de Graaff]] concluded that only [[Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur|Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur]] was feasible for Atjeh, rather than the local *zelfbestuur* proposed for other territories.

Swart closed the [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta-Radja]] meetings by thanking Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff|S. de Graaff]] for the "*heldere, zakelijke uiteenzetting*" ("clear, business-like explanation") of the reform plan. He expressed hope that this "*grootsche plan*" ("grand plan") would soon be realised, signalling his general support for the reorganisation effort despite the reservations he had articulated about its application to Atjeh's unique circumstances.

## Cultural Initiatives

Swart's governorship is also associated with the promotion of native craft industries. *De Buitenbezittingen* records that "op initiatief van Gouverneur Swart" ("on the initiative of Governor Swart") a half-dozen *handwerkscholen* — handicraft schools — were founded in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and [[Pedir|Pedir]], where pupils received regular instruction in useful and decorative handicrafts, lace-making, weaving, and similar skills, typically taught by Menangkabau girls trained as assistant teachers on [[Sumatra's Westkust|Sumatra's West Coast]]. The aim was to influence "de ontwikkeling der Atjehsche vrouw" — "the development of the Acehnese woman" — and through her the condition of the entire people.

## Kreemer's account: the administrative reforms

Kreemer's *Atjèh* ([[1923]]) catalogues, with dates and *besluit* numbers, the specific administrative reforms by which Swart reversed Van Daalen's exactions and re-founded the governance of the Onderhoorigheden on the principle of *zelfbestuur*. They group themselves under five heads.

### Restoration of the *Hasil-reglement* (1908)

Swart restored the *[[Hasil-reglement|Hasil-reglement]]* on [[30 June]] [[1908]] (Bt. N°. 55), reversing [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]]'s unfavourable reform of [[23 October]] [[1905]]. The restoration included ƒ345,288 total restitution — ƒ163,450 in direct damages and ƒ181,838 in withheld income and warranty — to the *landschapshoofden* who had been disadvantaged by Van Daalen's treatment of *Gouvernementsbestuursvestigingen* as *Gouvernementsgrondgebied*.

### Reform of *wasé oelèëbalang* (1910, 1915)

Swart's *besluit* of [[13 January]] [[1910]] N°. 1 reformed the *[[wasé oelèëbalang|wasé oelèëbalang]]* back from Van Daalen's 10%-of-value system to fixed amounts, halting the pepper decline that Van Daalen's [[1906]] reform had caused. A further reform in [[1915]] (Bt. [[13 September]] N°. 435) removed *bestuursbemoeienis* (administrative interference) from private-law pepper contracts entirely, leaving the relationships between *pangkaj* (financiers), *peutoeha* (garden-heads), and *aneu' seuneubö'* (planters) to the parties themselves.

### Opposition to *voorschotten* (1908)

In his *nota* of [[10 August]] [[1908]] (N°. 2704/22), Swart opposed Van Daalen's interest-free advances scheme and direct credit by the administration, defending the principle of self-rule (*zelfbestuur*) in the Onderhoorigheden. His position prevailed, and the *voorschotten* scheme was discontinued.

### *Wegenfonds* and *boetenfonds*

Swart ordered the *wegenfonds* (road fund) to cease on [[1 September]] [[1908]]. The *boetenfonds* (fines fund) that replaced it was then absorbed by the *landschapskassen* on [[1 January]] [[1912]].

### Expansion of *volksonderwijs*

Under Swart's governorship, the *volksonderwijs* (native education) system expanded from the single *moekimschool* with 38 pupils established by Van Daalen in [[1907]] to 51 schools with 2,009 pupils by [[1909]], and ultimately to 258 schools with 15,476 pupils by [[1919]]. However, the [[1919]] *Schoolrapport* by inspector **A. Vogel** concluded that this growth had been achieved through coercion, not natural demand, and that *volksonderwijs* was unpopular in true Atjèhsche areas.

## The Ten-Year Administration and the Handover of 1918

In Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]), Swart is the figure whose ten-year administration consolidated order and peace in Aceh. "The history of Aceh, for example, repeatedly showed how the most effective ally of the Acehnese against the Government was the Government itself, which constantly struck a new direction, which constantly changed systems of administration, and only during the ten-year administration of the Aceh-connoisseur, General Swart, were order and peace consolidated in that province." The same volume records Buyckjens's testimony: "Under the administration of Governor Swart the Aceh War came to an end… then gradually regulated conditions entered, and everywhere peace, order, and security began to prevail."

The cumulative effect of the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]], the killing of the last Tiriō leaders, and the steady pressure of the patrouilles was that by [[1918]] Swart could at last transfer a peaceful Aceh to a civilian Gouverneur. "Daardoor kon Generaal Swart in 1918 eindelijk een rustig gebied overdragen aan een civiel Gouverneur. Er was een eind aan the lingering disease: the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Atjeh War]]." — "Thereby General Swart was able in 1918 at last to hand over a peaceful territory to a civilian Governor. There was an end to the lingering disease: the Aceh War." The forty-five-year war — from the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|Eerste Atjeh-expeditie]] of [[1873]] to the handover of [[1918]] — was over. The political closure that Swart achieved was distinct from the military closure of [[1911]]; the seven years between them are the years in which the colonial army held a pacified country until the civil administration was ready to take over.

## Datoek's Sobat Kras

The old hunter [[Datoek|Datoek]] of [[Blang Rakal|Blang Rakal]] counted Swart among his *sobat kras* (heavy friends): "I was a 'heavy friend' with Toean Kristoppel, and Toean General Van Heutsz, and Toean Scheepens, and Toean Darlang, and Toean Boreel, and Toean Governor Swart." The *Toean Gouverneur* — the Acehnese idiom for the Governor — is the book's — and Datoek's — memory of Swart as the heavy friend of the old hunter: the *Toean Gouverneur* who was a *sobat kras* of the Acehnese hunter.

## Swart and Dominee Thenu

One personal anecdote from G. W. T. Dames's *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* places Swart in an unexpected register. When [[Dominee Thenu|Dominee Thenu]], after his retirement in [[1934]], was received in audience by [[Queen Wilhelmina|Queen Wilhelmina]] in the Netherlands, he was so taken by the honour that he refused to wash the hand she had shaken. "Lang heeft het geduurd eer hij de hand heeft gewassen, die door de Koningin was gedrukt" — "It was a long time before he washed the hand that had been pressed by the Queen." For more than ten days, the only other hand Thenu allowed to shake his was that of Generaal Swart. The episode places Swart, alongside the Queen, in the small circle of figures whose touch the *Soldaten-Dominee* (Soldiers' Dominee) counted as near-sacramental — a quiet marker of the esteem in which the rank-and-file of the colonial army held their general.

## Later Career and Consolidation

After his term in Aceh, Swart continued to serve in senior colonial positions. He later became Vice-President of the [[Council of the Indies|Council of the Indies]] (*Raad van Indië*) and played a key role in shaping colonial policy in the 1910s.

By the time *De Buitenbezittingen* was compiled, Swart could point to a territory in which, "na de hardnekkigen strijd" ("after the stubborn struggle"), the population was "vrijwel in zijn geheel" ("almost in its entirety") working with Dutch power, and long-neglected agriculture was being taken in hand again. The cautious and gradual replacement of military by civil administrators, and the accompanying reduction of troop strength, were "een krachtig bewijs" — "powerful proof" — of the consolidation that, after years of stubborn struggle, had come "betrekkelijk snel en onverwacht" ("relatively quickly and unexpectedly"). By the time Van Heutsz left office in [[1909]], Aceh was largely peaceful, the success widely attributed to Swart's conciliatory governorship.

## Significance

Swart's success in Aceh demonstrated that military capability alone was insufficient — a governor needed tact, patience, and a genuine respect for local institutions. His appointment marked the definitive shift from the coercive approach of Van Daalen to the collaborative model pioneered by Van Heutsz, and his governorship is often seen as the final consolidation of Dutch rule in Aceh after decades of conflict. In the narrative of *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.*, Swart stands as the quiet counter-weight to Van Heutsz: where Van Heutsz is the conqueror whose *systeem* finished the war at the tactical level, Swart is the general who closed it at the political level.

Swart's significance for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] history is also twofold in the colonial memorial register. First, his role as *Pacificator van Atjeh* — fixed in the colonial memory by the title of the [[Melati en rotan|Kunst]] elegy — makes him the principal Dutch agent of the final pacification of Atjeh, the successor in that role to [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] (whose campaigns brought the war to its military conclusion) and to [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Pel]], [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]], and [[General Vetter|Vetter]] in the central phase. Second, his [[1914|1914]] testimony on the [[sagi|*sagi*]] system is the principal documented indigenous-administrative description of the [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] territorial-political structure on which the post-conquest colonial administrative debate rested. His career thus spans the military, the administrative, and the memorial phases of the Dutch engagement with Atjeh.

## Account from *Atjèh* by J. Kreemer (Deel II)

[[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]] provides administrative details of Swart's governorship ([[1908]]–[[1918]]) that complement the earlier sources:

Upon succeeding Van Daalen in [[1908]], Swart immediately restored the *Hasil-reglement* (the regulation governing import/export revenue) that Van Daalen had altered, issuing Gt. 30 June [[1908]] N°. 55, which required the restitution of ƒ345,288 to those who had overpaid under Van Daalen's revised rates.

Swart reformed the *wasé oelèëbalang* from a percentage rate to fixed amounts by Bt. 13 January [[1910]] N°. 1, removing the incentive for *oelèëbalang*'s to restrict trade by substituting a flat sum for the ad valorem percentage.

By [[1909]], Swart had expanded volksonderwijs (public education) to **51 schools** serving **2,009 pupils** — up from 38 pupils in a single moekimschool under Van Daalen in [[1907]].

Swart formally opposed the *voorschotten* (advance-payment) scheme introduced by Van Daalen, registering his objection in nota 10 August [[1908]] N°. 2704/22.

The *wegenfonds* (road-maintenance fund) maintained by the *oelèëbalang*'s ceased on **1 September [[1908]]**, when its revenue function was transferred to the governmentfunds.

The *boetenfonds* (fines fund), maintained separately, was absorbed into the *landschapskassen* (territorial treasury funds) on **1 January [[1912]]**.

## Account from *Melati en rotan* (1947) — the *Pacificator van Atjeh* elegy

The [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] collection of F. Kunst, published [[1947]] at [[Bandoeng|Bandoeng]], contains the elegy "*[[Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh|Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh]]*" — "On the death of General Swart, the pacifier of Atjeh." The title fixes Swart, in the colonial memory of the mid-twentieth-century Dutch community of the Indies, as the *Pacificator van Atjeh* — the officer under whose command the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] was brought to its definitive close. The poem, however, immediately complicates the title by lamenting not the death of the general but the destruction of his work:

> *"Wanneer wij treuren aan het graf van Swart,*
> *Die nu geland is aan Doods veil'ge kust*
> *En van een daadzwaar, roemrijk leven rust,*
> *Is 't niet om hem, die dwars door vreugde en smart*
> *Atjēh gedragen heeft in zijn groot hart*
> *En van volbrachten plicht zich was bewust,*
> *Toen 's Levens laatst bivakvuur werd gebluscht.*
> *Ons voegt geen rouw om hem, maar het is hard,*
> *Zijn levenswerk roek'loos te zien vernield:*
> *Het heil van 't land, waar hij zoovéél van hield,*
> *Chaos, waar hij eens orde en welvaart bracht,*
> *Het razen van den fanatieken haat,*
> *Waar vredestichten was zijn grootsche daad,*
> *En ergst van al Holland in blindheids nacht."*

The elegy's three complaints — that the country's well-being has been reduced to chaos, that fanatic hatred rages where Swart had brought peace, and that "Holland in night of blindness" has permitted the destruction — fix the poem's occasion as the Japanese occupation and the Indonesian National Revolution, in which the colonial order established by Swart in Atjeh was visibly destroyed. The general's death, in this frame, is the occasion for a lament on the destruction of the colonial achievement itself — a lament that turns the title of *Pacificator van Atjeh* from a celebration into an elegy.

## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].

**Swart** — a Dutch colonel (*overste*) — is the officer whom the [[Raad van Indië]] recommended in [[1905]] for the post of military commander and governor of Atjeh, and whom governor-general [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] passed over in favour of [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]]. The episode is one of the most consequential personnel decisions of the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacificatie]] period: had the [[Raad van Indië]]'s recommendation prevailed, the [[Avondpost "Wekeer" articles (October 1907)|"Wekeer" affair]] of October [[1907]] might never have occurred.

### The 1905 nomination

When, in [[1905]], the post of *militair commandant en gouverneur van Atjeh* fell vacant — [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] having been elevated to the governor-generalship — the [[Raad van Indië]] considered the candidates and recommended Colonel Swart. Governor-general [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] overrode the recommendation: he persuaded the minister to appoint [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] instead. The inspection tour [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] undertook in December [[1907]] — after the [[Avondpost "Wekeer" articles (October 1907)|"Wekeer" articles]] — was to prove the [[Raad van Indië]]'s instinct sound: [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]]'s "verkeerde politiek gevoerd tegenover de hoofden" was the explicit finding of the [[Van Heutsz's report on Atjeh (June 1908)|June 1908 report]].

### The 1908 aftermath

The removal of [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] in [[December 1907]] and the [[Van Heutsz's report on Atjeh (June 1908)|June 1908 report]] installed in Atjeh the "vanouds beproefd gebleken stelsel van bestuur door middel van de hoofden, die men door tactvolle omgang aan het Nederlandse gezag moest binden" — the long-tried system of administration through the chiefs, who were to be bound to Dutch authority by tactful intercourse. [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]]'s maxim had been: "gij kunt die kerels niet vertrouwen, gij moet ze ook niet vertrouwen, gij moet ze slaan en drukken en dan zullen ze u gehoorzamen" — you cannot trust those fellows, you must not trust them, you must beat them and press them and then they will obey you. The [[Raad van Indië]]'s recommendation of [[Swart (colonel, Atjeh)|Swart]] is the institutional record of an alternative policy — one that, in the event, was tried too late.

### Significance

The [[Raad van Indië]]'s recommendation of [[Swart (colonel, Atjeh)|Swart]] in [[1905]] is, in retrospect, the lost alternative of the Atjehnese post-conquest period. The decision of [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] to override that recommendation is the proximate cause of the [[Van Daalen's Gajo-Alas-Batak expedition (1904)|Gajoe-Alas-Batak atrocities]] of [[1904]]-[[1905]] and of the [[Avondpost "Wekeer" articles (October 1907)|"Wekeer" affair]] of October [[1907]].

## Account from *Atjeh* by H.C. Zentgraff (1938)

[[H.C. Zentgraff|Zentgraff]] portrays Swart as the governor who completed Van Daalen's work by different means. He appears at no. 6 in the front rank of the 40-year jubilee photograph of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussée]] of [[2 April]] [[1930]]: *"Luit. Gen. H. N. A. Swart"*.

### The Meurandeh Paja massacre, 26 January 1905

At the time of the [[Meurandeh Paja massacre (1905)|Meurandeh Paja massacre]] of [[26 January]] [[1905]], Swart was *commandant te Lho Seumawé*. He received news of the massacre *lang vóór deze een der garnizoenen kon bereiken* — long before the single fusilier who escaped could reach a garrison — because such messages ran through Atjeh *als hazen* (like hares), each *keudé* passing the message to a fresh runner so that as much as 80 km per day could be covered. Swart *joeg met eene patrouille in den gemeensten marsch naar Meurandeh Paja*, found the sixteen *afschuwelijk toegetakelde* (hideously mangled) bodies — most bearing the traditional *kap in den hals bij den linkerschouder*, the *coup de maître* of the Atjeher with the klewang — and had them buried in a *massagraf*. The investigation pointed to [[T. Tjhi Toenong]] as one of the authors; Swart *liet dit op voorzichtige wijze doen* by luit. Van Vuuren when T. Tjhi Toenong came to [[Lho Seumawé]] on [[5 March]] [[1905]].

### Authorisation of Schmidt's Tiro pursuit, 1908

In [[1908]] Swart gave *luitenant [[Schmidt]], die juist mooi werk had gedaan in Geumpang en Teunom*, the order *zich geheel te wijden aan de opsporing, eventueel vernietiging, van de Tiro-oelama's en hun benden*. Before unleashing the *vernietigingsapparaat*, Swart made one last attempt to bring the Tiro-Tengkoes to submission: he had the three principal chiefs of Atjeh — Toeangkoe Machmoed, [[Panglima Polim]], and Toeankoe Radja Keumala — write the letter of [[18 Radjab 1325]] (the *[[Brief van 18 Radjab 1325]]*) urging the Tiro-oelama's to submit. The brief reached [[Tengkoe Majet di Tiro|Tgk. Tjhi Majet]]; nobody was willing to give up the fight.

### The false T. Radja Sabi, December 1913

In [[December]] [[1913]] the false T. Radja Sabi appeared at [[Lho Soekon]]. Kapitein [[Behrens]] of the 5e divisie at Leuhong examined him at length; the knaap *viel door de mand; hij bekende een onderschoven object te zijn* (fell through; he confessed to being a put-up job). Swart answered Behrens's report: *"nog niet de overtuiging te hebben dat de jongen niet T. Radja Sabi was"* — still not convinced that the boy was not T. Radja Sabi. He ordered one-by-one confrontations with relatives and acquaintances; even after the boy's confession. A few days later Swart *belegde te Lho Seumawé eene vergadering* of all the chiefs and pronounced: *"De jongen is Teukoe Radja Sabi"* — against which nobody protested. Zentgraff calls this *één der grootste, en interessantste, mystificaties welke de Atjeh-historie kent*.

## Origins, childhood and schooling

Swart did not come from a family of officers. His father, Klaas Swart, was a Groninger who worked in the Indies first in the *cultures* (plantation agriculture) and afterwards in the civil service; his mother was Cornelia Maria Johanna Maria Gouay. Born at [[Tjibitoeng]], Swart retained only vague memories of his earliest childhood before, in his seventh year, his father's administrative posting took the family to [[Soekadana]] in [[Borneo]] — a post under a lieutenant with a few European sergeants, from whom the boy learned "a little arithmetic and Dutch" until his ninth year.

In [[1872]] his father was pensioned. The family returned to the Netherlands by the sailing ship *Kosmopoliet II*, a voyage of 121 days round the Cape, touching at [[Mauritius]], the [[Cape of Good Hope]], [[Ascension]] and [[Saint Helena]] — where Swart heard the name Napoleon for the first time in his life. They landed at [[Brouwershaven]], lived for a time in [[Rotterdam]], and finally settled at [[Kampen]]. There Swart went to school for the first time, under the schoolmaster [[Van Duijl]], entering the lowest class with a brother three years his junior. During a free quarter of an hour he wandered into the sixth class, saw a problem in decimal fractions on the blackboard, took a piece of chalk and solved it; the master of the fourth class saw him and Swart was promoted at once to the sixth.

When Swart was twelve, Van Duijl — "a clever, good teacher, whom Swart still vividly remembered" — decided to teach him French so that the boy could enter the *H.B.S.* (*Hogere Burgerschool*). He succeeded. In the highest class he sat the entrance examination for the [[Koninklijke Militaire Academie|K.M.A.]] at [[Breda]]: it was settled between him and his two brothers that all three would become officers of the Indies army. Swart passed, but was ranked eighty-second when only the first seventy-two were admitted.

## From volunteer soldier to officer

Swart chose the solution many took in that period: *adspirant-onderofficier*. On [[16 October]] [[1880]], aged seventeen, he engaged as a volunteer soldier for six years, with a bounty of eighty guilders, in the battalion of the 8th Regiment of Infantry then garrisoned at [[Deventer]]. He deliberately avoided the alternative, the *Instructie Bataljon* at Kampen, because he did not wish to walk about as a private in front of the comrades who had passed for the K.M.A.

He was made corporal on [[16 March]] and sergeant on [[30 October]]. Being a few days too young to sit the examination for the *Hoofdcursus* within the year, he was obliged to remain two years on the course at [[Arnhem]]; after six years in all he passed out through the *Hoofdcursus* as an officer with a middling examination number. He re-engaged for a year on [[16 October]] [[1886]] and on [[21 November]] [[1886]] was appointed *2de luitenant* in the infantry of the army in the Netherlands Indies by royal decree no. 18 of [[24 November]] [[1886]].

As a sergeant Swart once met King [[Willem III]], during a summer detachment in the camp of [[Millingen]]. Commanding a forage column to [[Apeldoorn]] on a sweltering day, he had unbuttoned his tunic and unfastened his belt when he recognised the King approaching on foot.

He sailed for the Indies on [[7 May]] [[1887]] aboard the *s.s. Zuid-Holland*, arrived at [[Batavia]] on [[17 June]] [[1887]], and was posted on [[22 June]] to the 6th Battalion.

## First Atjeh service and the Militaire Willemsorde, 1890

Swart's first contact with [[Atjeh]] came in [[1890]], when he was attached as *2e luitenant* to the expeditionary 6th Battalion of Infantry at [[Idi]] on the [[Oostkust van Atjeh|east coast]]. On [[11 June]] [[1890]], in the [[Attack on the Boekit Roembia (11 June 1890)|attack on the enemy positions on the left bank of the Idi river]], he led the 2nd section of the 2nd company — the section with the least difficult ground to cross — and was first into the fortification on the summit of the [[Boekit Roembia]], followed immediately by an Ambonese corporal and an Ambonese fusilier.

Swart's own recollection, given to du Croo at the age of seventy-nine, was characteristically plain: "I was lucky that day. I was then serving with the 2nd Ambonese company. And with four Ambonese I was the first man in. But as I sprang over the parapet into the stronghold I at once had a shot through the left upper arm" ("I was lucky that day … but as I jumped over the parapet into the *benteng* I at once had a shot through my left upper arm"). The Ambonese behind him had a finger shot away. The battalion band played, the colours were planted, the *Wilhelmus* rang out; [[J. van Teijn|Van Teijn]] and [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] came into the fortification and Van Heutsz tapped him on the shoulder: "you brought that off, my boy!" ("you brought that off, my boy!"). The artillery commander [[Rost van Tonningen]] — later army commander — said to him: "Swart, come and have a glass of beer with me!"

Having been dressed by the *officier van gezondheid* [[J. A. Tamson]], Swart resumed command of his section and took part in the rest of the day's operations. He remained with the troop movements with his arm in a sling and returned to [[Magelang]] after six weeks. By royal decree of [[30 August]] [[1891]] no. 2 he was appointed Knight 4th Class of the [[Militaire Willemsorde]] for his conduct at various encounters with the enemy in Atjeh during [[1890]].

Du Croo drew attention to two peculiarities of this episode. The first was that six weeks of Atjeh service "dat is alles!" — that was the whole of it — for a man who would become one of the Indies' foremost *Buiten-mannen* (Outer-Provinces men); until his promotion to captain in the transitional year [[1898]] Swart obstinately remained on [[Java]] and in the *vredesgarnizoenen* (peace garrisons). The second was that in those young years the pull towards the *buitengewesten* was simply absent: Swart preferred to lay "een draagvlak" for later years, to learn everything he could within the military framework, with the [[Hoogere Krijgsschool]] in the background.

## The Korps Pupillen at Gombong, 1891–1894

On [[20 August]] [[1891]] Swart, still a second lieutenant, was transferred to the [[Korps Pupillen te Gombong]], in the lonely heart of Central Java, and carried *à la suite* of his arm. He was promoted *1ste luitenant* on [[7 April]] [[1892]] with the stipulation that he remain at the Korps Pupillen.

The school — long since forgotten, as du Croo remarked — existed to receive the children of soldiers killed in action or pensioned who could not be properly brought up at home; almost exclusively children of men below officer rank were admitted. Some three to four hundred blue-clad *pupillen* were housed in three companies: the 1st for boys of fifteen to seventeen, the 2nd for twelve to fifteen, the 3rd for six to twelve. Each company had two lieutenants and the necessary cadre, with a captain as corps commander. The boys attended the ordinary primary school under supervision during normal school hours; the afternoon was given to sport, gymnastics and craft instruction (tailor, carpenter), the last introduced for those found unfit for military service at the age of seventeen, who could then find a living as artisans.

Swart, who besides his myopic astigmatism was afflicted with "an unusual interest in education", kept a large and warm place in his memory for the school. When a boy asked him one day whether he could become an officer — "Hm, well, are you really clever enough for that?" said Swart; "Yes indeed, but can you not help me?" — the lieutenants conferred and the result was a *cursus* for boys eligible for more extended education. Swart's verdict: "A really good institution; I have always regretted that it was abolished and that the boys were then distributed among the orphanages" ("A really good institution; I always regretted that it was abolished and the boys then distributed among the orphanages"). Throughout his long service he repeatedly and with great pleasure met old and successful *pupillen*, often as non-commissioned officers and often with the [[Militaire Willemsorde|M.W.O.]] or an honourable mention; a colonel who headed the [[Topografische Dienst]] and a well-known *buiten-werker* had been his pupils at [[Gombong]].

## Staff and adjutant years, 1894–1898

On [[27 October]] [[1894]] Swart was transferred to the *Subsistentenkader* at [[Semarang]]; on [[29 March]] [[1895]] he was appointed adjutant of the 11th Battalion; on [[11 December]] [[1895]] he was honourably relieved of that post and transferred to the *Subsistentenkader* at [[Batavia]]. On [[23 January]] he was transferred to the IInd Division of the [[Departement van Oorlog]] (Head Bureau of Infantry). By decree no. 13 of [[15 September]] [[1898]] he was awarded 825 guilders for translating into Malay the *Uittreksel uit de Recrutenschool* together with the principal duties and rules of conduct of the soldier and of the group commander in action. He was promoted *Kapitein* on [[24 July]] [[1898]].

## Captain in Atjeh, 1898–1900: "Swart and his Jans"

On [[22 November]] [[1898]] — in the midst of the hard fighting and continuous extension of Dutch authority under the new governor Van Heutsz — Swart was posted to Atjeh for the second time, as captain in the 7th Battalion of Infantry, then quartered with three companies at [[Sigli]] and one company in a bivouac some hours outside it. That detached company, composed of [[Soendaneezen|Sundanese]] — a people with few representatives in the army — was assigned to him.

At Sigli he found Van Heutsz, appointed general in November (the Commander's Cross of the M.W.O. followed in January [[1899]]), and paid his respects. Van Heutsz's greeting became famous in Swart's own retelling: "Now then, you had better see what you can do with your company; you have been a long time on Java in all those posts, so now show that you are of use in the field as well" ("Now then, you had better see what you can do with your company; you have been a long time on Java in all those posts, so show that you are of use in the field too").

Swart got on excellently with the cheerful and capable Sundanese, marched out often with them, especially in the [[Padang Tidji]] district and to several places where troops had never been before. He was not an easy chief: a hot-tempered man who could bellow and rage, who set high demands and maintained strict discipline, but who could inspire his men, who was strictly just and who took uncommon care of "een potje" — the cooking pot. Honest and warm-hearted, he stood up where necessary for his subordinates.

When the 7th Battalion returned to Java, Swart was transferred on [[30 May]] [[1899]] to the 12th Battalion at Sigli, whose 1st company had been dispirited. Its Javanese soldiers — *Jannen* in barrack idiom — promptly gave the small, thickset, slightly round-shouldered captain his recognition-name: *"de Bult"* ("the Hunchback"), a name that carried a certain appreciation. A sergeant of the company wrote to an evacuated comrade, sergeant [[Schouten|M. Schouten]]: "And now we have only a little captain!" ("Now we really have a captain!"). Of that *Jannen*-company without élan Swart made an élite unit, and with it earned the third class of the [[Militaire Willemsorde]].

The decisive action came during the [[Pasè expedition (1899)|expedition to Pasè]] led by Van Heutsz in [[1899]]. On [[21 June]] [[1899]], in the operations at [[Geudong]], his company was sent to the front line to support the *marechaussée* and was halted by the greatly swollen [[Pasè river]]. Despite heavy enemy fire at short range, Swart at once took energetic measures for the crossing, went over the river, gathered some seventeen soldiers on the right bank and with great skill and boldness drove the superior enemy with heavy loss from his position to the south by a flank attack, holding the captured position until more troops arrived.

Swart's own account preserved the human detail: crossing on improvised rafts he found beside him, to his surprise, lieutenant [[Mac Gillavry]] of Van Heutsz's staff, who had come along without his knowledge. They worked their way cautiously along the bank towards the *missigit* (mosque), reached the edge of the fortification, and at Swart's signal *"voorwaarts"* leapt over the wall into the midst of a garrison certainly two hundred strong. The surprise robbed the defenders of their courage; after a short but violent fight they fled. Across the water Van Heutsz was seen with his staff, shouting "who is that commander there and with how many men is he posted?" — to which Swart shouted back: "Captain Swart with eighteen men, but he requests reinforcement!"

In another engagement against a fortification on the river he pushed a man in front of him through the *pagger* and ordered an attempt on the parapet; they got over it and the enemy, with nowhere else to go, had to swim. During the fight Swart felt something against his belly and found he had taken a *contourschot* which had not penetrated; the doctor treated it in the evening after his wounded had been dressed — "you got off lightly there" ("you got off lightly"), the doctor said.

Two further wounds are recorded in his *stamboek*: at the storming of the *kampong* [[Bambi]] in [[Pidie|Pidië]] a grazing shot above the right temple on [[8 May]] [[1899]], and at the taking of the positions on the [[Glé Risé]] a *bloedbuil* (blood blister) on the belly from a thrown stone on [[21 November]] [[1899]]. For his distinction in the operations in Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden between [[1 June]] [[1899]] and [[31 December]] of that year he was promoted Knight 3rd Class of the *Militaire Willemsorde* ("Military William Order") by royal decree of [[13 July]] [[1900]] no. 29.

## Leave, marriage and return, 1900–1901

Swart remained continuously on operations in Atjeh until May [[1900]], when he was transferred to [[Buitenzorg]] in connection with his application for leave. There he heard indirectly that, though barely two years a captain, he had already been declared fit for major. His corps commander nevertheless "tested" the prospective leave-taker according to the rules of the trade — battalion school, field service, drill, administration — until he embarked on [[18 July]] aboard the *s.s. Lawoe* and disappeared for a year. He had been granted a year's European leave on [[6 July]] [[1900]] with retention of claim to promotion.

During that leave he noted two particulars: he was appointed to the commission charged with conducting the officers' examination at the K.M.A., and he married. The examination lasted some weeks on end, so that Swart even obtained an extension of leave. One memory remained: riding back to his hotel with the chairman of that part of the commission after a field-service exercise, he discovered just before the hotel that he had lost his M.W.O. The examinees were marching behind them, and to Swart's great joy one of the young men had found the decoration — the same young man who had that morning given the worst examination of the day, provoking a comrade's remark: "Well, old chap, that is the best turn you have given away today!"

He married on [[22 May]] [[1901]] Miss [[Victorina Maria Geertruida Stadlmair]], then twenty-three years old. Swart had asked to be reposted to Atjeh at the end of his leave; at [[Padang]] he learned that the request had been granted — but the newly married officer had been given a station to which his wife was not permitted to follow him. So it was that on [[17 September]] [[1901]] Swart arrived in Atjeh for the third time, alone. He returned from leave on [[21 August]] [[1901]] aboard the *s.s. Koning Willem III* and was posted to the 12th Battalion.

Du Croo underlined the paradox: Swart was a young captain, he had earned an exceptionally high distinction as a fighting officer, he had drawn the attention of Van Heutsz, he was thirty-eight years old — "but as an administrator he had never yet appeared anywhere." His greatest and most fruitful gift, his uncommon aptitude for *pacificeerend bestuurswerk*, had remained unnoticed and therefore unused for more than fourteen years of service.

## Marechaussee and civil authority: Poelò Raja and Lho' Seumawè, 1902–1905

Swart's ambition on returning to Atjeh was placement with the [[Corps of Military Constabulary of Atjeh and Dependencies|Korps Marechaussee]] — and he admitted frankly that what mattered to him in such a placement was the *goede tempat*, the station to which a wife might follow. On [[8 February]] [[1902]] a vacancy arose; Swart was transferred to the 1st Garrison Battalion of Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden and assigned as commander of the 5th Division of the Korps Marechaussee at [[Lho' Nga]]. On [[27 March]] [[1902]] he was charged with the government of the division of the [[XXV Moekims]] with the southern settlements (Afdeeling [[Groot Atjeh]]).

On [[17 July]] he was relieved of the government of the sub-division Sagi of the XXV Moekims and southern settlements and charged with the government of the sub-division [[Poelò Raja]] with the title of *Civiel Gezaghebber* (Civil Authority). "And there, on Poelò Raja and in July 1902, the real administrative work began for Swart" — there, du Croo wrote, Swart's real administrative work began: a relatively small island, virtually pacified, but with an extensive field for action on the mainland where numerous known band-leaders practised the *main prang* (war-game) with their followers. For communication between station and mainland Swart even disposed of a *ship of war*, which he used above all for nocturnal crossings and thus for surprise.

On [[12 October]] [[1903]] he was honourably relieved of the government of Poelò Raja and charged with the government of the sub-division [[Lho' Seumawè]], the assignment being described as temporary; on [[24 October]] [[1903]] he was extraordinarily promoted *Majoor* for outstanding deeds and for extraordinary military talents demonstrated in action. On [[3 April]] [[1905]] he was honourably relieved of the government of Lho' Seumawè.

## Swart's administrative principles

Du Croo set out the guiding principles Swart formulated for administrative work in scarcely subjected districts, where pacification had to be built up from below.

In the first place: only by giving trust can trust be obtained, but the administrator must always keep before his eyes the reputation of the Acehnese in the matter of *vertrouwen*. Therefore: keep both eyes well open and ask always whether the man is *worth* trusting. The application of this principle meant that the good chiefs at once and clearly felt that Swart was well disposed to them; from such chiefs he could obtain anything, and with such chiefs it was ultimately possible to reach an initial form of self-government — du Croo cited [[T. Tjhi' Moehammad Djohan Alamsjah|T. Tjhi' Peusangan]] as the example.

In the second place: he demanded and himself provided proper administration of justice, in which correct sentences were pronounced according to principles of law that the Acehnese understood — that is, a decision as just as possible *"with regard to the [[adat]]* ("customary law")"*, always with the spiritual adviser, the *[[oelama]]*, behind the green table. Such justice caused the Acehnese to judge the *toean* who presided over the *[[Moesapat]]* to be *adil* (just). And whoever truly stood recorded as *adil* could, as an administrator, achieve much if not everything.

In the third place: [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] had rightly wished that roads be laid out everywhere. Such road-building, as a rule combined with *[[heerendiensten]]*, naturally aroused reluctance among an extremely indolent population accustomed to perpendicular footpaths — but where it was well executed and not carried out as a show-piece it reached far above the immediate daily convenience of the *toean*, because it opened up a hermetically closed country, brought better and faster traffic, and so promoted trade and mutual intercourse.

A fourth principle was travel without military escort, a hobby-horse of Swart's and a conception [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] did not share; Van Daalen even forbade administrators to visit their territory without military cover. In [[1902]] this had already caused friction between Swart as *gezaghebber* in [[Lho' Kroeët]] and [[Daja]] and Van Daalen as his assistant resident. Van Daalen noted the prohibition on Swart's journal; Van Heutsz in his turn noted "that Swart could judge this better than he or I could, that appreciation ought to be shown from higher authority in the matter, and that there could be no question of a prohibition where Swart was concerned."

## The first clash with Van Daalen

The very first personal contact between Swart and Van Daalen in the administrative sphere — and precisely on the point where Van Daalen stood diametrically opposed to a healthy, ethical pacificator, namely the treatment of the chiefs — marked their difference of view clearly. Van Daalen, as acting Assistant Resident of the *Onderhoorigheden*, visited Poelò Raja and took part in a patrol. The patrol met an *[[oelèëbalang]]* with his retinue, who paid his respects in the customary and, to Swart's mind, entirely proper manner. Van Daalen nevertheless remarked that this chief had shown insufficient respect, which Swart could not see. Van Daalen lectured him: "look here, you are a full-blooded European and you do not feel that; but with me it is different. We have more sense of these things. Respect is..."

## Borneo and Celebes, 1905–1908

On [[3 March]] [[1905]] Swart was temporarily charged with the civil and military authority in the residency of the [[South and East Division of Borneo]], under the title of *Civiel en Militair Resident*, at a salary of 1,250 guilders a month. He was promoted *Luitenant-kolonel* on [[24 October]] [[1905]] with the stipulation that he remain charged with that authority. In one year he brought about a complete reversal in Borneo; the [[Pacification of the South and East Division of Borneo (1905–1906)|pacification of the Zuider- en Oosterafdeeling]] was accomplished in [[1905]]–[[1906]].

On [[9 July]] [[1906]], under article 43 of the *Reglement op het beleid der Regeering in Nederlandsch-Indië*, he was temporarily charged with effect from [[3 September]] [[1906]] with the civil and military authority in the [[Gouvernement Celebes en Onderhoorigheden]], at 1,500 guilders a month, with the title of *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Celebes en Onderhoorigheden* and simultaneously Military Commander of [[Menado]] and [[Timor]] en Onderhoorigheden. There he oversaw the [[Pacification of the Province of Celebes (1906–1908)|pacification of Celebes]] and, as the closing piece of his eastern service, the [[Onderneming tegen Soembawa (1908)|undertaking against Bima and Soembawa]] in [[1908]] — an operation carried out with a handful of *marechaussée* and described by du Croo as a classic example of rapid and conclusive pacification. He was promoted Commander of the *Militaire Willemsorde* ("Military William Order") by royal decree of [[2 September]] [[1909]] no. 41 for the Soembawa operations of [[16 February]] to [[9 May]] [[1908]], and appointed Knight in the [[Order of the Netherlands Lion]] by royal decree of May [[1909]] no. 7.

## Appointment to Atjeh, 1908

By decree of [[4 May]] [[1908]], with thanks for his many and weighty services in Celebes, Swart was appointed Military Commander of Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden and simultaneously Governor of that province, with the title of *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* at 1,500 guilders a month. He took over the government of Atjeh on [[10 June]] [[1908]].

The appointment was contested. [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]] published in the *[[Nieuwe Courant]]* of [[16 June|16]]–[[19 June]] [[1908]] a hostile judgement: asked whether the new governor's appointment offered any guarantee of better policy, "We must answer this question decidedly in the negative." As a troop commander Swart was valued by the most competent, but by no means placed on a level with Van Daalen; in knowledge of Atjeh and the Acehnese he could not stand in Van Daalen's shadow. Snouck concluded that Swart was "by no means the man to give Atjeh … the combination of means of pacification derived from sound principles of government with the unavoidably hard measures that must break the resistance. It may well be that he would be in favour of it; behind it he is not, and behind it he will not come."

Minister [[Alexander Willem Frederik Idenburg|Idenburg]] wrote candidly to Van Heutsz: "I do not know Swart … I accept on your authority that he is excellent; but for the work of pacification he is no more cut out than Van Daalen, though his character is perhaps somewhat gentler." Van Heutsz answered with an equally sharp defence of the man he had chosen, and the compromise reached was the supervisory visits of the member of the Council of the Indies [[Liefrinck]].

Before departure Swart stayed at the palace at [[Buitenzorg]], where Van Heutsz — feeling himself so often a prisoner of that palace — took him incognito to [[Batavia]] for an evening at the *Restaurant Versteeg* opposite Hotel De Nederlanden, "Haroun al-Rashid and his vizier"; the disguise failed, as ever more polite passers-by came to look at the Governor-General. Van Heutsz's parting words were: "one thing more … you have only to see to it that what happened to Van Daalen does not happen to you!" Swart answered: "I have a different character from Van Daalen." "Accoord," Van Heutsz replied, "I count on it, then, that Atjeh will come right. And then we shall say no more about it."

## Taking over the government, 1908

Swart took over the civil government from Assistant Resident [[Rijckmans]], Van Daalen having already departed. His account of the first weeks is preserved in the biography: "Then I went at once by tram with Colijn eastward as far as Langsa, to get an impression for myself." He discussed everything with [[Hendrikus Colijn|Colijn]], who had been attached to him as *adjunct regeeringscommissaris voor de decentralisatie* for the first months, and together they determined what should be brought to the notice of the Governor-General; Colijn then wrote the letter.

At [[Lho' Seumawè]] Swart held a meeting with all the *oelèëbalangs* of the eastern part. Discussing the situation, he pointed out that in various *landschappen* there were again *[[djahat]]s*. One of the chiefs then took the floor and answered curtly: "not only are there evildoers everywhere, but we ourselves are evildoers too!" — a reply that, as du Croo observed, characterised the state of affairs exactly. There followed the discussion of the disastrous application of the [[Boetenstelsel|system of fines]] and the withholding of the chiefs' incomes, which certainly amounted to a couple of hundred thousand guilders. Swart informed them that whatever in his opinion had been wrongly withheld would be returned at once and without more ado; but the fines and withholdings rightly imposed he regarded as a *waarborgsom* (security) for their good conduct, returnable only after a year or more of good behaviour, depending on the *landschap*, and otherwise irrevocably forfeit.

At the same meeting he raised the unceasing attacks on the tramway, which had become the rule: "I firmly count on it that those attacks are finished from today. Should that not be the case, then I can assure the territorial chiefs that I shall make them feel it severely!" He did not say how or what or where, but they knew him from the time when he had been major at Lho' Seumawè. They answered "you can rely on it" — and from then on the tramway line there was no longer destroyed. In [[Keureutòë]] he met [[Christoffel]] and told him: "you see to it that the resistance in this part is brought to an end, and you may act very severely in doing so." Christoffel answered: "that will be put in order, Colonel."

Swart's verdict on what he found was grim: "The situation greatly disappointed me and proved far less favourable than I had thought, because — and it is painful to have to say this — government had been carried on by superiors who lacked the gift of governing." By the reports of the administrative officials there were certainly five to six thousand men in resistance, if not more, and they were everywhere again.

Relatively few men had to be removed from their functions; against many he had to intervene, but he then gave them a probationary period. "If in that time they really changed, and conformed entirely to my views, then they could stay; if that was not the case, then out they went irrevocably."

## The Liefrinck inspections

For two successive years Swart received the visits of [[Liefrinck]], member of the [[Raad van Nederlandsch-Indië]], sent as a kind of *toeziende voogd* (supervising guardian) to ensure that he would not do what Van Daalen had done. Swart's own comment was dry: "I had little trouble from him, but he had, to be honest, little insight into the matter either." He put a good lieutenant's house near the Governor's residence at Liefrinck's disposal and accompanied him on tour in a directors' carriage of the tramway, stopping at every halt so that the *oelèëbalangs* could pay their respects.

At one halt in [[Pidie|Pidië]] Swart noticed that one of the chiefs was not properly dressed. The man first paid his respects to Liefrinck; when he came to Swart, Swart pointed out his clothing and his deficiency of *[[hormat]]*, refused to receive him and sent him away: "on the return journey, when I come by again, you may pay your respects." Liefrinck asked in astonishment: "how can you do such a thing? I had not even noticed it!" — "Quite so, but those things do strike me," said Swart, "and I do not receive the chiefs in that way." After two years it was clear that Swart could manage perfectly well without the supervising guardian.

## Promotions during the governorship

Swart was extraordinarily promoted *Kolonel* on [[31 March]] [[1909]] for outstanding military deeds and extraordinary military talents; *Generaal-majoor* on [[18 November]] [[1911]]; and *Luitenant-Generaal* on [[6 September]] [[1912]]. He was promoted Adjutant in Extraordinary Service to Her Majesty the Queen by royal decree no. 65 of [[7 April]] [[1916]], and appointed Grand Officer in the [[Orde van Oranje-Nassau]] by royal decree of [[27 August]] [[1921]] no. 49. The *Eereteeken voor belangrijke krijgsbedrijven* was awarded him for Atjeh [[1873]]–[[1890]], Atjeh [[1906]]–[[1910]], South Celebes [[1905]]–[[1908]] and the Lesser Sunda Islands [[1905]]–[[1909]].

The [[Nieuwe Courant]] chronicler of Atjeh affairs — reprinted in the *[[Sumatra Post]]* of [[25 November]] [[1912]] — read the promotion to lieutenant-general as "the confirmation of a policy which from its beginning meant a true revolution, a reversal of ideas concerning our administrative task in the north of Sumatra."

## Method: men, orders and correspondence

Swart's method as governor was to choose men, give them precise tasks, and hold the threads himself. He sat in his office at [[Koetaradja]] — "the small, thick-set figure who now had to leave the leading in the field to others, who held only the threads in his hands" — and with each ring of the telephone could expect news that might break a piece out of his construction. He had adopted Van Heutsz's motto *"alles kan" ("everything is possible")* ("everything is possible").

Three of his subordinates he placed above the rest: [[G. K. Dijkstra|Dijkstra]], [[W. B. J. A. Scheepens|Scheepens]] and [[H. J. Schmidt|Schmidt]]. He placed Dijkstra and later Scheepens at [[Sigli]], the most important troop command on the north coast; the principal bivouac in the plain of Pidië, [[Lam Meulò]], he put under captain Jhr. [[J. J. Boreel|Boreel]]; when resistance revived in the [[Gajò Loeös]] after some painful setbacks of the infantry garrison he sent the 1st Division there under the General Staff captain [[W. Muurling|Muurling]], with his two Atjeh lieutenants [[C. A. Snell|Snell]] and [[Van Eck]].

To energetic *marechaussée* lieutenants he gave special assignments that pricked their ambition and virtually compelled them to succeed. The best known was the commission given to [[H. J. Schmidt|Schmidt]] in December [[1909]] and executed on [[3 December]] [[1911]], after two heavy patrol years in continuous complete independence, when the last surviving [[Tirò Teungkoes|Tirò Teungkoe]], the young [[Tgk. Ma'at]], fell.

He corresponded privately with his most trusted collaborators, a practice for which he was criticised. Du Croo defended it by citing Idenburg's letter to Van Heutsz of [[15 September]] [[1908]]: "the advantage of private correspondence is that it is personal and that one can say exactly what one thinks about a matter." Extracts from [[Swart's letters to Schmidt (1909-1913)|Swart's letters to Schmidt]] of [[26 December]] [[1909]], [[26 September]] [[1911]], [[9 December]] [[1911]] and [[10 August]] [[1913]] are printed in the biography.

## Character, manner and anecdote

Du Croo devoted a whole chapter to the anecdotes that circulated about Swart, in which "truth and fancy were often mixed together." He was quick-tempered and irascible, capable and just, strict but benevolent, above all a good and fatherly chief for the good. He habitually began nearly every sentence with *"en"*, which so puzzled one of the [[Tangsé]] chiefs that the man asked Schmidt whether the Governor was angry when he said *"en"* — "And, Teukoe, is all well?" — until reassured by the translation of the word into Acehnese.

He was fond of the *soerat*, the written certificate the Acehnese prized: "Carolus, give the man a certificate," he would say to his adjutant [[G. K. W. F. de Vaynes van Brakell Buys|Karel de Vaynes van Brakell Buys]], who would write "The Acehnese Joesoef of the village of So-and-so in Pidië is known to me" and Swart would sign — hundreds of such *soerats*, du Croo suspected, were still kept as *poesaka* (heirlooms).

He was an enemy of debts. When a patrol commander who had not drawn his 100-guilder monthly intelligence allowance for fifteen months proposed to spend the accumulated sum, Swart ruled: "I shall have that paid out to you in Holland at 100 guilders a month" — and when the man protested, "heb je me verstáán?" He was also devoted to *zwartzwier*, jugged duck; a nine-day inspection tour in which every post served it produced the sigh "Carolus, I can't bear to look at that stuff any more!"

## Farewell and departure, 1918

On [[12 August]] [[1918]] Swart was appointed, with effect from September [[1918]], Vice-President of the [[Raad van Nederlandsch-Indië]], and honourably discharged from military service with a pension of 9,000 guilders a year, suspended for the duration of his Vice-Presidency. On [[2 September]] [[1918]], at a solemn gathering in the [[Gouverneurswoning te Koetaradja|Governor's residence]], he handed over the government to his designated successor, the civilian Governor resident [[Van Sluys]].

In his [[Swart's farewell address (2 September 1918)|farewell address]] Swart noted that he had been Governor of Atjeh far longer than any of his predecessors, and that with the laying down of his office "an important piece of Atjeh history" was closed, since there would now be an absolute separation of civil and military authority as existed in all other provinces. He admitted his shortcomings but declared himself convinced that the pacification was now established, and that it had "come brilliantly through the test of the last four years." Against the charge that he had shunned publicity and practised secrecy he protested: "I never kept anything hidden; I have never been afraid of criticism."

He left Atjeh in the early morning of [[22 September]] [[1918]] from [[Oelèë Lheuë]], where crowds had come by car and special train. The band played the *Wilhelmus*; Mrs Swart received flowers; the couple boarded the sloop of H.M. *Serdang* and went aboard the *s.s. Nias*, saluted by three cheers and the military salute of the *Serdang*'s crew.

## Vice-President of the Raad van Nederlandsch-Indië, 1918–1922

Swart departed for [[Batavia]] on [[22 September]] [[1918]] to take up the office of Vice-President of the Council of the Netherlands Indies, the second-highest post in the colony. It was not usual for a general to be called to it; but this general had also been an administrator in the barely opened *buitengewesten*, whose development had become a principal task of the Government.

Du Croo declined to treat these three years in depth and quoted instead two press notices. The *[[Java-Bode]]* of [[18 October]] [[1921]] recalled that the ethical press had feared the appointment, but concluded that "if there has been one man who in turbulent times proved better informed of the native political currents than anyone else, it was General Swart"; hoewel militair gebleven, "he set a strong administration above a rule of force." The paper noted his interest in the [[Volksraad]], whose sittings he often attended: "A man who, after a career through the autocratic schools of the Outer Possessions, followed the rise of parliamentarism with so much interest proved that he understood the voice of the times."

The *[[De Locomotief|Locomotief]]* — the paper known as the most ethical, which had disapproved of the appointment — wrote that "like a soldier faithful at his post, Lieutenant-General H. N. A. Swart … remained under arms as long as the country had need of him," and that it had been at royal insistence that the then Governor of Atjeh, wishing after more than thirty years of colonial service to repatriate, allowed himself to be called to the second-highest office.

Swart got on well with the Governor-General: "When the Governor-General went on a journey … he always said: I am glad again that during my absence General Swart is at Batavia." He postponed his own departure once more so that it should not coincide with the change of Governors-General. On [[4 January]] [[1922]], forty-two years after entering service, he was honourably discharged at his own request from the service of the State.

## Retirement and the memory of Bapa Swart

As a private citizen Swart returned to the Netherlands, settling at [[Den Haag]], but Atjeh retained his warm and continuous interest. When resistance broke out again on the [[Westkust van Atjeh|west coast]] of Atjeh in [[1925]], his opinion was sought in Holland and reported by the journalist [[J. Schaap]], formerly editor-in-chief of the *Java-Bode*, in *[[The Weekly of an Amsterdammer]]* of [[14 September|14]] and [[21 September]] [[1927]] — a statement du Croo called "Swart's Atjehsch testament".

He revisited the Indies in [[1927]]/[[1928]], travelling in [[Celebes]] and visiting the *landschapshoofd* of [[Peusangan]]; he spoke again with [[T. Tjhi' Moehammad Djohan Alamsjah|T. Tjhi' Peusangan]]. On his seventieth birthday, [[12 October]] [[1933]], he received the [[Letter of the Acehnese Chiefs to General Swart (26 September 1933)|letter of the Acehnese chiefs]], drawn up by [[T. Maharadja Mangkoeboemi]], *zelfbestuurder* of Lho' Seumawè, and [[T. Tjhi' Moehammad Djohan Alamsjah]], *zelfbestuurder* of Peusangan, and signed by thirty-two chiefs of the north coast — the very region where resistance had raged most fiercely.

He kept up his correspondence with the humblest of his old subordinates. A letter of [[19 February]] [[1937]] to the Ambonese old soldier [[Jacob Lawalata]], celebrating on [[26 February]] [[1937]] his thirty-fifth year as a Knight of the *Militaire Willemsorde* ("Military William Order") at [[Jogjakarta]], is reproduced in facsimile in the biography; a letter to Swart from his old brigade commander [[W. J. Mosselman]], written from *De Hoenderhof* at [[Tandjoengsari]] near [[Bandoeng]] on [[12 September]] [[1936]], is printed beside it.

Swart himself kept no letters and no notes: "Swart kept no letters and no notes; he possesses no archive and no diaries," du Croo recorded, which made the reconstruction of the correspondence difficult.

## Significance (du Croo, 1943)

Du Croo summarised Swart's achievement in ten propositions. The great blessing of his ten-year governorship was that the government remained and could remain for ten consecutive years in the hands of a Governor with such outstanding administrative talents. In the long series of years from [[1873]] to [[1898]] the Acehnese had gained no better experience of Dutch administration than a clear absence of line and direction, both military and civil; only the period of [[Karel van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] formed a favourable exception, and it was too short to leave a lasting result. Swart, by balanced and calm government alongside vigorous action, brought the Acehnese to the conviction that Dutch authority was and remained overwhelming, while at the same time winning the confidence of chiefs and population, so that he was "beside being feared, also respected and beloved."

Because he could so completely win the confidence of the *adat* chiefs and of the simple *gampong* man, the Acehnese attached himself personally to Swart — a typically Acehnese trait being attachment to, and service of, a person. Through that he was able to bring both chiefs and population to trust the administration, so that the well-known phrase in the *[[Memorie van Overgave]]* of his first civil successor — "everything in Atjeh that walks about on two legs hates the European" — was in its generality a gross exaggeration and untrue: everything depended on the person.

His long administration formed an excellent school of government for his officers and administrative officials, provided they served under him long enough; Governor [[Van Aken]] was du Croo's best example of a successor formed in it. He made Atjeh ripe for civil government in its full extent, provided the successor continued along his lines and had the tact to leave the military element a generous place for the time being. The qualities the Acehnese valued in him above all were his honesty and his justice, which he also required of his subordinates.

The simple title *Bapa* — father — became, in du Croo's judgement, the highest distinction Swart ever won among all his high and very high decorations: "Bapa Swart was one of those very few." The biography closes by comparing the Acehnese memory of him with the royal salute *Bayete!* which the Matabele brought unbidden to [[Cecil Rhodes]] in the Matoppo Hills.

## Account from *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* ([[1923]])

**H. N. A. Swart** was a Dutch officer and administrator who as Civil and Military Governor of [[Acheh|Atjeh and Onderhoorigheden]] (1908–1918) completed the [[Pacification of Atjeh|pacification]] of the country, and who later, as old Vice-President of the [[Council of the Indies]], furnished the foreword to [[J. Langhout|Joh. Langhout]]'s *[[Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh]]* ([[1923]]). Under his administration, in the phrase of the writer [[J. Kreemer]], "the swords were forged into ploughshares": the last organized resistance of the [[Acheh War]] was extinguished by [[1913]], and a vast programme of schools, credit institutions, waterworks and roads followed.

### Appointment and early measures

By Government resolution of [[4 May]] [[1908]] Major-General [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|G. C. E. van Daalen]] was, at his own request, honourably discharged as Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh, and Lieutenant-Colonel H. N. A. Swart, then Civil and Military Governor of [[Celebes|Celebes and Dependencies]], was appointed in his place. Swart accepted the administration on [[10 June]] [[1908]]; it would last unbroken for ten years. Because of the circumstances of the change of government, [[F. A. Liefrinck]], Member of the [[Council of the Indies]], twice visited Atjeh as Government Commissioner in the first years of Swart's governorship to guarantee that the government of the province continued to answer to the principles fixed by the Government. Swart none the less followed a different administrative line from his predecessor, while continuing [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]]'s policy of restlessly pursuing and rendering harmless the religious resistance-leaders and band-chiefs.

Among his first measures was a proposal — accepted — to repay the landscape chiefs the share of import and export duties and excises which had been withheld from them "allegedly for their punishment", and to refund their imposed surety sums; Swart everywhere handed these back personally to prove, as he said, that the Government "knew how to practise clemency and to redress wrong". As the political state improved he saw no objection to furnishing several landscape chiefs with rifles and charging them with the maintenance of peace and order in their territories, holding that "by giving trust, trust is won". He likewise sought to win the confidence of the common man by providing quick and cheap justice, through an administrative organisation dividing the province into five departments under assistant-residents, split into many sub-departments, whereby the supervision of native jurisdiction became steadily more accurate.

### End of the war

With the death or submission of the principal religious and secular resistance-leaders and many thousands of their followers in the years 1908–[[1913]], all effective opposition ended by the latter year; a few dozen evildoers holding out in the most inaccessible mountain tracts had no significance. Governor Swart had the exiles whose residence in the province had been forbidden under article 47 R.R. allowed to return, with the exception of the ex-pretender-sultan [[Toeankoe Mochamad Dawot|Toeankoe Moehamad Dawot]] and his son [[Toeankoe Ibrahim]]. From [[1909]] silver and gold stars for loyalty and merit were conferred on higher and lower chiefs for their good cooperation. Submitted resistance-fighters and chiefs whose dwellings or *koeta's* (fortified places) had been destroyed were set on their feet with interest-free advances from funds obtained from the Government.

### Welfare, education and health policy

Convinced that the European government no longer had to act merely punitively, Swart powerfully furthered everything serving the people's welfare. Popular education was his first care, aimed at the practice of life: agriculture teaching for boys, hand- and household-work for girls. The five village-schools with some 300 pupils at his accession grew to 125 schools with 6,000 pupils by [[1911]], and to 242 schools with nearly 13,000 pupils by [[1918]]; a first girls' school opened at [[Oeleë Lheuë|Oelèë Lheue]] in [[1910]], and by 1918 there were fifteen girls' schools with more than 1,000 pupils, through which the government hoped to combat the evil of child-marriage (*kinderhuwelijken*). A Hollandsch-Indlandsche school opened at [[Koeta Radja]] in [[1915]], followed by [[Lho Seumawè]] and [[Langsar]] (1916), and a Hollandsch-Chineesche school at Koeta Radja in 1918; an Ambonese school for children of Ambonese soldiers, opened in 1911, counted nearly 400 pupils in 1918; an *ambachtschool* (native trade school) for carpenters was opened at Koeta Radja in [[1916]]. Agricultural schools at [[Beureunoen]] and [[Oeléë Karang]] kept farming as the first means of existence; chiefs' sons were educated at [[Fort de Kock]], [[Bandoeng]] (later [[Serang]]), the Asahan school, the Cultuurschool at [[Soekaboemi]], the agricultural school at [[Buitenzorg]] and the native law school at [[Batavia]]. Unattended lepers were gathered in colonies, that of Groot-Atjeh being an agricultural colony on the islet [[Po Amat|Pò-Amat]] (1911), and on the island [[Roebia]] in [[Sabang]] bay a quarantine station for returning pilgrims was set up in [[1912]]. When smallpox epidemics broke out in [[1915]] and [[1916]], vaccination was vigorously enforced. Taxes averaged only one guilder per head and compulsory labour two days per month, with redemption of *heerendiensten* (statute labour) permitted by way of trial; the sub-department and landscape treasuries spent the proceeds on works of public utility.

### Economic measures

[[Agriculture in Atjeh|Agriculture]] was stimulated through the agricultural adviser posted in the province since 1908, new irrigation works, the diking of the lower courses of the [[Peusangan River|Peusangan]] and [[Djambo Ajé]] rivers, cattle-selection under the government veterinary surgeon (from 1911), weekly cattle markets, and interest-free advances (including buffalo-advances for plough cattle on the North Coast). The [[Atjeh Tram]] was carried on to [[Koeala Simpang]] (1912) and [[Besitang]] (1916) and transferred to the State Railways on [[1 January]] [[1916]]; the [[Groot-Atjehsche Afdeelingsbank|People's credit institutions]], including the Afdeelingsbank (September 1908), kampong banks, and credit banks at [[Lho Seumawè]] (1913) and [[Langsar]], combated usury. The annual fair exhibition at [[Koeta Radja]] was repeated in [[1911]], and after the [[Semarang]] colonial exhibition of [[1914]] the Atjeh pavilion was re-erected at Koeta Radja as the [[Atjeh Museum]], opened in the second half of [[1915]]. The agency of the [[Javasche Bank te Koetaradja|Javasche Bank]] at Koeta Radja owed its first stone to the Governor. From 1911 Swart worked at the gradual separation of civil and military authority.

### Character

Nineteen years Swart had lived and worked among the Atjehnese; ten years in succession he governed land and people. Receiving author Langhout as old Vice-President of the Council of the Indies, he rejected press talk of an ineradicable hereditary hatred of the Dutch among the kampong women — "Wie dat zegt, kent het volk niet!" ("Whoever says that, does not know the people!") — and told of [[Teukoe Tjhi Peusangan]], of [[Panglima Polem]] and his sister, of Polem's brother-in-law [[Toeankoe Radja Keumala]], of [[Potjoet Meurah|Pòtjoet Meurah]] and her husband [[Toeankoe Machmoed]], and of so many other descendants of sultans and Atjehnese chiefs who entrusted to the *kaphé* (infidel) their "dearest and most secret thoughts". In his foreword of [[31 March]] [[1923]] at The Hague, signed "H. N. A. Swart, Oud Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden", he held that after the long and tenacious struggle a state of rest and order had prevailed for about ten years, which only needed time fully to consolidate. Stepping ashore at [[Sabang]] again at the end of January [[1922]] on his way to Europe, he was told by the Atjehnese chiefs who came to compliment him that the economic development of Atjeh was still progressing.

**H. N. A. Swart** was Civil and Military Governor of [[Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] from [[1908]] to [[1918]], whose administration was the central focus of [[J. Langhout|J. Langhout]]'s jubilee book *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* ([[1923]]).

### Administration as described by Broersma

[[Dr. R. Broersma]] characterized Swart's period as a period of appearance and self-advertisement, elevated in the tasteless advertising of 1923 to a vessel full of "blessings of peace". Broersma considered the departmental support of the Department of Colonies for this advertising as unworthy of the Dutch state, particularly as an English edition was also provided.
