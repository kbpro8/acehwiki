**Cornelis de Houtman** (1565–[[1599]]) was the [[The Netherlands|Dutch]] navigator who commanded the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies ([[1595]]–[[1597]]), and who was killed at [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in [[1599]] on the second expedition under [[Balthasar de Moucheron|De Moucheron]]'s auspices. His death at Atjeh — by *kris*-attack after a quarrel that began with poisoned fruits and drinks — is the first recorded Dutch fatality in the Atjeh sultanate, the opening Dutch casualty of an Acehnese-Dutch relationship that would last, with long interruptions, for the next three centuries, and the trigger of his brother [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]]'s twenty-six-month Atjeh captivity, out of which came the first European Malay dictionary.

## The first Dutch voyage to the Indies, 1595–1597

Cornelis de Houtman had led the pioneering "Eerste Schipvaart" of [[1595]]–[[1597]], the four-ship Amsterdam expedition that reached [[Bantam]] and returned — battered, decimated, but laden with pepper — to Amsterdam in [[1597]]. The voyage had opened the Dutch Indian trade that within five years would crystallise into the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]]. Godee Molsbergen treats the first voyage at length; for Atjeh it is the prologue.

## The De Moucheron expedition of 1598

The South-Netherlander of French descent [[Balthazar de Moucheron|Balthazar de Moucheron]], trusting the experience of the De Houtman brothers, took them into his service on a fleet of two ships, with the English pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] serving as pilot — and as a spy for the Earl of Essex. Stapel records that "on [[11 March]] [[1598]] Balthasar de Moucheron sent out 2 ships under Cornelis and Frederik de Houtman and [[Guyon Lefort|Guyon Lefort]]" (*Op 11 Maart 1598 zond Balthasar de Moucheron 2 schepen uit onder Cornelis en Frederik de Houtman en Guyon Lefort*). The voyage to the Indies took fifteen months.

## Outward voyage via Madagascar

Stapel records the outward route in a single sentence: "The voyage went west of [[Madagascar]] via the [[Comoren|Comoros]] and [[Maladiven|Maldives]] to Atjeh, where one arrived in June '99" (*De reis ging bewesten Madagascar via de Comoren en Maladiven naar Atjeh, waar men in Juni '99 aankwam*). Godee Molsbergen adds that the Madagascar population fled at the reappearance of the rough Dutchmen: this was the second Dutch landing there, the first having left a bad reputation under De Houtman's own first expedition.

## Arrival at Atjeh, June 1599

When at length the fleet reached Atjeh in June [[1599]], the good understanding with the Sultan lasted only briefly. The initial reception was favourable — Stapel notes that "the reception here was initially good" (*De ontvangst hier was aanvankelijk goed*). But the situation was then poisoned by Portuguese intrigue. Godee Molsbergen's account is unadorned: "Met vergiftigde vruchten en dranken, later met een krisaanslag, wilde men van die vreemdelingen afkomen" — With poisoned fruits and drinks, later with a *kris*-attack, they sought to be rid of these foreigners. Stapel records that the sultan was "incited by the Portuguese" (*opgestookt door de Portugeezen*) and "had them treacherously assaulted" (*liet de vorst hen verraderlijk overvallen*).

This Acehnese attack on Dutch merchants under Portuguese prompting set the tone for the entire first phase of Dutch-Acehnese relations: recurrent Portuguese influence at the Acehnese court would bedevil the Compagnie's embassy there until [[Malakka]] fell in [[1641]].

## Death at Atjeh, 1599

There were many dead. Godee Molsbergen: "waaronder Cornelis de Houtman" — among them Cornelis de Houtman. Stapel records that "Cornelis with several others came to their end" (*Cornelis met verscheiden anderen kwamen hierbij om*). His brother [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik]] was taken prisoner and would spend more than two years in Acehnese captivity. See [[Death of Cornelis de Houtman at Atjeh (1599)]] and [[First Dutch voyage to Atjeh (1598-1599)]].

The ships that escaped Atjeh made for [[Pedir]], where they beat off an Atjehnese naval attack; they then touched at the [[Nicobaren|Nicobars]] and, after much misfortune, reached Middelburg in the summer of [[1600]].

## Aftermath

The death of Cornelis de Houtman did not end Dutch interest in Atjeh; if anything, it gave the Dutch a personal stake in the sultanate. [[Balthazar de Moucheron|De Moucheron]] suffered heavy losses; [[John Davis|Davis]] turned his spy-material to English advantage. But [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]]'s captivity became the foundation of European Malay studies, and the [[Maurits van Oranje|Prince Maurits]]-Sultan correspondence of [[1601]]–[[1602]] — for which see [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)]] — was in part motivated by the need to obtain his release. By [[1602]] the deed was done: Frederik was free, and an Atjeh embassy had stood in Prince Maurits's military camp at the siege of Grave.

## Significance

Cornelis de Houtman is significant to [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history as the first Dutchman of note to die there — the eldest casualty of the Dutch-Atjeh encounter, and the human cause of the diplomatic and scholarly chain that produced his brother's Malay dictionary and the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits]]. His death fixed Atjeh in the Dutch colonial memory as a place of danger, treachery, and — paradoxically — of compulsory apprenticeship. In Stapel's structure of the Dutch-Acehnese narrative, his death is the opening Dutch casualty of a three-century relationship.
