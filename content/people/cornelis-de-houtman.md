**Cornelis de Houtman** (1565–1599) was the Dutch navigator who commanded the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies ([[1595]]–[[1597]]), and who was killed at [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in [[1599]] on the second expedition under [[Balthazar de Moucheron|De Moucheron]]'s auspices. His death in Atjeh — by *kris*-attack after a quarrel that began with poisoned fruits and drinks — is the first recorded Dutch fatality in the Atjeh sultanate and the trigger of his brother [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]]'s twenty-six-month Atjeh captivity, out of which came the first European Malay dictionary.

## The first Dutch voyage to the Indies, 1595–1597

Cornelis de Houtman had led the pioneering "Eerste Schipvaart" of [[1595]]–[[1597]], the four-ship Amsterdam expedition that reached [[Bantam]] and returned — battered, decimated, but laden with pepper — to Amsterdam in [[1597]]. The voyage had opened the Dutch Indian trade that within five years would crystallise into the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]]. Godee Molsbergen treats the first voyage at length; for Atjeh it is the prologue.

## The De Moucheron expedition of 1598–1601

The South-Netherlander of French descent [[Balthazar de Moucheron|Balthazar de Moucheron]], trusting the experience of the De Houtman brothers, took them into his service on a fleet of two ships, with the English pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] serving as pilot — and as a spy for the Earl of Essex. The voyage to the Indies took fifteen months. The Madagascar population fled at the reappearance of the rough Dutchmen: this was the second Dutch landing there, the first having left a bad reputation under [[Cornelis de Houtman|De Houtman]]'s own first expedition.

## Arrival at Atjeh, June 1599

When at length the fleet reached Atjeh in June [[1599]], the good understanding with the Sultan lasted only briefly. Godee Molsbergen's account is unadorned: "Met vergiftigde vruchten en dranken, later met een krisaanslag, wilde men van die vreemdelingen afkomen" — With poisoned fruits and drinks, later with a *kris*-attack, they sought to be rid of these foreigners.

## Death at Atjeh, 1599

There were many dead, "waaronder Cornelis de Houtman" — among them Cornelis de Houtman. There were also many prisoners, among them his brother [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik]]. See [[Death of Cornelis de Houtman at Atjeh (1599)]]. The ships that escaped Atjeh made for [[Pedir]], where they beat off an Atjehnese naval attack; they then touched at the Nicobars and, after much misfortune, reached Middelburg in the summer of [[1600]].

## Aftermath

The death of Cornelis de Houtman did not end Dutch interest in Atjeh; if anything, it gave the Dutch a personal stake in the sultanate. [[Balthazar de Moucheron|De Moucheron]] suffered heavy losses; [[John Davis|Davis]] turned his spy-material to English advantage. But [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]]'s captivity became the foundation of European Malay studies, and the [Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]]-Sultan correspondence of [[1601]]–[[1602]] — for which see [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)]] — was in part motivated by the need to obtain his release. By [[1602]] the deed was done: Frederik was free, and an Atjeh embassy had stood in Prince Maurits's military camp at the siege of Grave.

## Significance

Cornelis de Houtman is significant to [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history as the first Dutchman of note to die there — the eldest casualty of the Dutch-Atjeh encounter, and the human cause of the diplomatic and scholarly chain that produced his brother's Malay dictionary and the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits]]. His death fixed Atjeh in the Dutch colonial memory as a place of danger, treachery, and — paradoxically — of compulsory apprenticeship.
