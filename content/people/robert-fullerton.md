**Robert Fullerton** was a British [[East India Company]] official who served as [[Governor of Penang|Governor of [[Penang]]]] (Prince of Wales' Island) in the mid-[[1820]]s, in succession to [[W. E. Phillips]]. He is significant in the Achehnese record for two major minutes on [[Sumatra|Sumatran]] affairs — the [[Fullerton's minute on Acheh (21 March 1825)|minute on Acheh of 21 March 1825]] and the minute of [[16 March]] [[1828]] on the projected [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Dutch *Bellona* mission]] — as well as for his [[Fullerton's letter to Sultan of Siak (7 February 1828)|letter to the Sultan of Siak]] of [[7 February]] [[1828]] and his [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular to nine Siak chiefs]] of [[21 May]] [[1828]]. The 1825 minute, in which Fullerton declared that any attempt to establish European influence at [[Acheh]] without «completely subjugating the country» had proved «utterly impracticable», was quoted at length by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in the Introduction to *The Achehnese* as evidence that the difficulties encountered by the Dutch in the [[Acheh War|Achehnese War]] were not the product of Dutch incompetence alone, but were inherent in the political and social condition of Acheh itself.

## Governorship and the Acheh Question

Fullerton took up the governorship at a moment when the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] was already an acknowledged [[dead letter]]. By the [[London dispatch of 4 August 1824]] the Court of Directors had observed that [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] had never possessed effective authority and that the treaty might «even now be fairly considered as a dead letter», while the [[Bengal inquiry to Penang (28 January 1825)|Bengal inquiry to Penang of 28 January 1825]] had requested the Penang government's advice on the possibility of replacing it with a purely commercial arrangement. Fullerton's reply, dated [[21 March]] [[1825]] and concurred in by his council members [[W. A. Clubley|W. A. Clubley]] and [[R. Ibbetson|R. Ibbetson]] on [[7 April]], is printed as Appendix 10 of [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]]'s article. In it Fullerton observed that any attempt to establish European influence at Acheh without «completely subjugating the country» had proved «utterly impracticable», and warned that the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] placed the petty states of [[Deli]], [[Langkat]] and [[Siak]] — which carried on an extensive trade with Penang — at the mercy of any Dutch interference authorised by Article 2 of the treaty.

The judgment echoed and amplified the warning given nearly four decades earlier by [[Captain Francis Light]], who in [[1786]] had pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal that the Achehnese were «rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous», and that a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary before any settlement of safety and advantage could be formed. Fullerton's statement belongs to the same body of British experience as the contemporaneous correspondence surrounding the [[Raffles Treaty with Acheh|treaty made with Acheh]] by [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] in [[1819]] and the subsequent dealings with [[Jauhar Alum|Johor Allum]] as would-be King of Acheen. The British had come to realise that the country had no central authority capable of guaranteeing the security of foreign trade, and that European nations attempting to settle there had always been driven out by the «oppression, war and confusion» prevailing on the Achehnese coast. The conclusion embodied in Fullerton's memorandum was shortly afterwards overtaken by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]], under which Great Britain withdrew from direct interference in Achehnese affairs and left the question of trade security in Dutch hands. The Dutch, however, were slow to profit from the British lesson: it was only after decades of fruitless treaties and the disasters of [[1873]] that they too came round, under [[General van der Heyden]] and later [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], to the conviction which Fullerton had expressed in [[1825]].

## The Said Akil Affair

Fullerton was governor during the latter part of [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Said Akil's adventure at Deli]]. After [[Sultan of Deli|the Sultan of Deli]] had written to Penang on [[27 August]] [[1824]] complaining of Akil's behaviour and invoking the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] (under which Deli was, he alleged, a dependency of Acheh), the [[Shahbandar of Deli|Shahbandar of Deli]] wrote again in February [[1828]] begging Fullerton to deliver the country from the «robber». Fullerton, after consulting the Malay translator [[John Anderson|John Anderson]], addressed a letter of [[27 August]] [[1828]] to the Sultan, declaring that Penang «could not countenance any interference in the affairs of other countries by one of our own subjects» and that Said Akil, having proceeded to Deli contrary to the injunctions of the Penang government, was «placed without the protection of the British Government». The Bengal government, by dispatch of [[24 October]] [[1828]], approved the measures Fullerton had taken.

## The Bellona Minute and the Siak Correspondence

In late [[1827]] Fullerton learned, through the former Singapore resident [[John Prince|John Prince]] at [[Batavia]], that [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Commissioner-General Du Bus]] proposed to send the frigate *Bellona* to the ports of [[Sumatra's East Coast|Sumatra's East Coast]] in order to induce the chiefs to take their salt from [[Riouw]] rather than from Singapore. In his minute of [[16 March]] [[1828]] Fullerton observed that the Dutch would certainly use the exclusive right they claimed under the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] «to the full extent of their means», and that «the political influence so established will be rendered entirely subservient to the promotion of their own trade and deterioration of ours». To counter the design he had already, on [[7 February]] [[1828]], addressed a letter from Singapore to [[Sultan of Siak|the Sultan of Siak]] expressing British anxiety to continue and promote the growing trade between Siak and the Straits settlements; and on [[21 May]] [[1828]] he issued a [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular to nine Siak chiefs]] offering reciprocal freedom from duties and inviting their merchants to resort to [[Malacca]]. The Sultan of Siak's reply acknowledged receipt of the presents sent with Fullerton's letter and expressed his «ten times as great» anxiety to preserve the friendly relations between Siak and the British settlements.

## Assessment

[[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] treats Fullerton as the most clearsighted of the British officials who succeeded [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] in the Sumatran correspondence. The historian prints Fullerton's minutes in extenso precisely because they afford, in his view, the most candid English exposition of the dangers to which the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] had exposed Netherlands India's commercial rivals on Sumatra. Snouck Hurgronje's recourse to Fullerton's 1825 memorandum, eighty years later in the Introduction to *The Achehnese*, was of the same character: Fullerton's words stand as documentary evidence that the obstacles the Dutch encountered in the [[Acheh War]] were not the product of Dutch incompetence alone, but were inherent in the political and social condition of Acheh itself. The judgment of 1825 was thus to remain, throughout the long Achehnese war, both the earliest and the most candid European diagnosis of the country's resistance to exterior authority.
