**P.P. van Bosse** was a Dutch colonial minister who served from [[November]] [[1877]] to [[February]] [[1879]], a critical period in the [[Aceh War]] when the transition from military to civil governance began to take shape. Born in [[1809]], he was an experienced statesman who had previously served as Minister of Finance and was known for his liberal views and financial expertise.

## Early Career

Van Bosse was a successful manufacturer in [[Weesp]] and later an advocate in [[Amsterdam]]. He entered politics as a referendaris at the Department of Finance ([[1845]]) and served in various roles, including Minister of Finance on multiple occasions ([[1848]]-[[1853]], [[1858]]-[[1860]], [[1866]], [[1868]]-[[1871]]) and Minister of Colonial Affairs ([[1871]]-[[1872]], [[1877]]-[[1879]]). He was a member of the [[Second Chamber]] during several periods ([[1853]]-[[1858]], [[1860]]-[[1866]], [[1867]]-[[1868]]).

## Appointment and Early Policy

Van Bosse took office in [[November]] [[1877]], succeeding [[F. Alting Mees]]. His appointment came at a time of financial strain due to the [[Aceh War]], and his primary goal was to reduce costs while maintaining stability. He wrote to Van Lansberge: "The time has come to break with the exploitation system of the Indies." He supported the principle that Indian needs should come first, rejecting the idea of a fixed contribution to the Dutch treasury.

## Aceh Policy

Van Bosse was cautious about military expansion in [[Aceh]], repeatedly warning against occupation of more territory:

1. **Opposition to expansion**: He stated that "further penetration into the interior is a very reprehensible system" and wanted only "punishment and intimidation, not permanent occupation."

2. **Pressure for cost reduction**: He pushed for rapid reductions in Aceh expenditure, urging Van Lansberge to "cut through" despite opposition from those who benefited from the war.

3. **Civil governance**: Van Bosse was an early advocate for civil governance in Aceh, suggesting either an excellent senior officer or a civil servant (like [[L. de Scheemaker]]) or even a naval officer ([[C.H. Bogaert]]) as governor. He wanted to "put the civil government at the head as soon as possible."

4. **The Habib affair**: Van Bosse reluctantly supported the "buying off" of [[Habib Abdul Rahman]], though he viewed the method as distasteful, agreeing only because he believed it would reduce war costs.

5. **Opposition to expansion into the XXII and XXVI Mukims**: He argued against permanent posts in these areas, fearing they would lead to further expansion.

## Financial Policy

Van Bosse introduced significant financial reforms:

1. **The "batig slot"**: He proposed a fixed annual contribution of 4-5 million guilders, believing that certainty was more important than a high figure. He later adopted a more flexible approach, arguing that "first must be provided for Indian needs."

2. **New taxes**: He supported the introduction of patent and personal taxes on Europeans, overriding resistance from the European commercial community.

3. **Spending control**: He warned against overspending, noting that "with a deficit of 18 million on the ordinary budget, one cannot be too careful."

4. **Railway policy**: Van Bosse was initially opposed to state exploitation of railways but eventually came around to state construction, recognizing that private enterprise was not forthcoming on acceptable terms. He also established a technical bureau at the Department of Colonial Affairs to improve procurement.

## Administrative Reforms

Van Bosse focused on improving the quality of the colonial administration:

1. **Financial specialists**: He sent capable financial experts to the Indies, including Motké and [[J.P. Sprenger van Eyk]], often overriding regulations to do so.

2. **Personnel policy**: He advocated for removing unsuitable officials, stating "we must make a clearing, and bring younger blood into the arteries."

3. **Judicial reform**: He worked to separate administrative and judicial functions, though he acknowledged this was a gradual process.

4. **Education**: He supported the establishment of schools for indigenous chiefs.

## Relationship with Van Lansberge

Van Bosse and Van Lansberge had a generally harmonious relationship, though there were occasional tensions:

1. **Mutual respect**: Van Bosse appreciated Van Lansberge's independence and energy, writing that "as far as I see, we steer in exactly the same direction."

2. **Disagreements**: They differed on some points, including state vs. private railway exploitation (Van Lansberge favored state exploitation) and the pace of civil governance in [[Aceh]].

3. **Support**: Van Bosse consistently supported Van Lansberge, writing "rely on it that I would not abandon you."

## Resignation and Legacy

Van Bosse's health declined during his tenure, and he died on [[21 February]] [[1879]], at the age of 70. His resignation was planned but his death cut short his service.

His legacy includes:
- Financial reform that began to move away from the "batig slot" system.
- Support for the transition to civil governance in [[Aceh]].
- Efforts to improve the quality of the civil service.
- The establishment of the technical bureau for railway procurement.
