**The Rifāʿiyya** — in Dutch scholarship **Rifa'iyah order** — is one of the principal ṭuruq (Sufi orders) of orthodox Sunni mysticism, founded by the saint [[Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī|Ahmad Rifa'i]] — whose tomb at [[Mocha]] is reverenced — in the marshlands of southern Iraq in the twelfth century C.E. The Rifāʿiyya was the silsila into which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] was initiated by his shaikh [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]], and was the spiritual genealogy by which Raniri, on his arrival in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]], claimed the authority of an orthodox mystic against the [[Wujudiyyah]] of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]].

## Origin and early diffusion

The Rifāʿiyya — also called the *Baṭāʾiḥiyya*, from the Arabic *baṭāʾiḥ* ("marshlands") — was founded by [[Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī|Aḥmad ar-Rifāʿī]] in the marshlands of southern Iraq in the twelfth century C.E. The order spread rapidly through the Muslim world in the late middle ages, reaching [[Gudjarat|Gujarat]] by the sixteenth century.

## Transmission to Gujarat and the Malay-Indonesian world

The Rifāʿiyya was transmitted to Gujarat by [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]], who was born at [[Tarim]] in [[1561]] and came to Gujarat at the age of nineteen. Al-ʿAddārs received [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]] into the order at [[Surat]]; Bā Shaybān, in his turn, initiated two of the principal Malay-Indonesian Sufis of the seventeenth century: [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Raniri]] (before his departure for [[Acheh]] in [[1637]]) and [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]] (after his departure from [[Makassar|Celebes]] for [[Mecca]] in [[1644]]). The Rifāʿiyya was thus the spiritual genealogy that linked the orthodox reaction of Acheh with the religious history of Makassar in the second half of the seventeenth century.

## The devotional geography of the order

The devotional geography of the Rifāʿiyya as it was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world is itself a document of the order's diffusion. The tomb of the founder, [[Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī|Aḥmad ar-Rifāʿī]], is reverenced at [[Mocha]] — the coffee port of Yemen on the Red Sea — rather than in the marshlands of southern Iraq where the order was actually founded. The association of the founder's tomb with Mocha is a measure of the devotional geography of the order as it was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world: a geography in which the founder's sanctity was located at a Red Sea port frequented by Indian and Indonesian pilgrims and traders, and so made accessible to the Malay-Indonesian devotees of the order.

## Significance

The Rifāʿiyya is the spiritual genealogy by which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Raniri]] and [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]] claimed the authority of an orthodox Sufi silsila against the [[Wujudiyyah]] of the Malay-Indonesian courts — in [[Acheh]] in the case of Raniri, in [[Makassar]] in the case of Shaikh Yūsuf. The transmission of the order from the marshlands of Iraq to the harbours of Gujarat and the courts of Acheh and Makassar is one of the principal channels by which the orthodox Sufi scholasticism of the Arab and Persian worlds was carried to the Malay-Indonesian archipelago in the seventeenth century. The [[Raniri's public debate against the Wujudiyyah in Acheh|public disputation]] between Raniri and the Wujūdiyyah at the court of [[Iskandar Thani]] in [[1637]] — the central event of the religious history of Acheh in the seventeenth century — was carried out under the authority of this silsila.
