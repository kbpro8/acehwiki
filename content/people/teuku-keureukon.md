**Teuku Keureukon** is the hereditary bearer of a title that originally designated the **royal secretary** of the sultanate of [[Acheh]]. The title, properly ***Keureukon Katib ul-mulk*** ("writer of the kingdom") — vulgarly pronounced ***Katiboy mulut*** in Achehnese — was one of the principal court offices during the period of prosperity of the sultanate, and is the eponym of the III Mukims Keureukon district of [[Great Acheh]].

In the period of prosperity of the sultanate, the Keureukon Katib ul-mulk served as confidential secretary to the sultan and drafted the royal correspondence and edicts. The opening formula of the sultans' letters and edicts, "His Majesty ordered this to be written by the Keureukon katibulmuluk", survives in the documents of the decadent period as a fossil: a literary form retained long after the office itself had fallen into abeyance. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, the work performed in earlier times by the Keureukon Katiboy Mulut, "in so far as it did not altogether fall into abeyance, was carried on by common servitors of no rank, who were called *Krani Poteu* or 'writers to our supreme lord'". The sarakatas (*letters patent*) continued, however, to be modelled on the pattern of those of the prosperous period, and the name of the Keureukon continued to appear at their head.

This title is a classic example of the degeneration of offices in Acheh. As the importance and extent of the sultan's correspondence dwindled with the decay of the court, the title passed from father to son more easily than the art of composing and writing Malay letters could. The hereditary title-bearer thus became an *uleebalang poteu* (lord "of our supreme lord") without work or fixed income and without territory. Since the establishment of one of their number in the Mukim Lhee — the district now officially known as the III Mukims Keureukon — the Keureukons have been among those whose influence is a factor to be reckoned with in that *wakeueh*-district, although they have never come to be recognised as its chiefs.

The Keureukon Katiboy Mulut appears by name in the [[Letters patent with the ninefold seal to Panglima Meuseugit Raya|letters patent granted by Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan]] to [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] on 4 Rabi' al-Awwal 1307 H ([[1889]]): "His Highness sat upon the throne in [[Kuta Keumala]], His exalted Majesty, the Prince of the world spoke unto the Keureukon Katiboy Muluk (or Mulut), bidding him draw up a document with the thunder-seal". Snouck Hurgronje, in a footnote, calls this "imaginary private secretary" — a literary survival rather than a real office — and observes that "some writers have made a false deduction from this circumstance, and would have us suppose that the office of confidential secretary here referred to was in active existence down to the most recent times".
