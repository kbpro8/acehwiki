**Prince Maurits** — *Maurits van Oranje* (Maurice of Orange, 1567–1625), stadtholder of the United Provinces of the Netherlands from [[1585]] to [[1625]] — was the addressee of the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|first Atjehnese embassy to the Netherlands of 1601]], the recipient of the sultan of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]]'s letters and gifts in response to Maurits's own letter and gifts of the same year, and the staff history's principal Dutch royal actor of the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]] narrative of early Dutch-Atjehnese relations.

**Prince Maurits** (1567-1625) — **Maurits van Nassau, Prins van Oranje**, Stadhouder of Holland and Zeeland from [[1585]] and of all the United Provinces from [[1590]] — was the principal Dutch military commander of the Dutch Revolt against Spain and the principal political opponent of [[Johan van Oldenbarneveldt|Johan van Oldenbarneveldt]] in the conflict over the [[Twelve Years' Truce (1609)|Twelve Years' Truce]]. In the Dutch-Atjeh relationship, Maurits is the principal Dutch royal correspondent of the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|Sultan of Atjeh]], whose letters and gifts to the sultan produced the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|first Achehnese embassy to the Netherlands]] and inaugurated formal Dutch-Achehnese diplomatic relations.

## Maurits's letter and gifts to the sultan of Atjeh

The embassy's point of departure is Maurits's own letter and gifts to the sultan of Atjeh, transmitted by the two Zeeland ships of [[1601]]: there were fine gifts on board for the sultan of Atjeh, and also a letter from Prince Maurits. Now the Netherlands came into favour with that prince. He set the prisoners at liberty and gave permission to build a factory, or trading post, in his country. Maurits's letter and gifts were thus the diplomatic instrument that secured the release of the Houtman captives and the permission for a Dutch factory at Atjeh — the diplomatic achievement that the school reader frames as the origin of formal Dutch-Atjehnese relations.


## Significance

Prince Maurits is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal Dutch royal correspondent of the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] and the principal Dutch advocate of the war party against the [[Twelve Years' Truce (1609)|truce of 1609]]. His letters and gifts to the sultan produced the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|first Achehnese embassy to the Netherlands]] and the diplomatic rapprochement that the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush of 1599]] had foreclosed. The reception of the Achehnese embassy at the siege of Grave is the principal Dutch instance of the early-modern practice of receiving non-European envoys in the field, and frames the Dutch-Achehnese diplomatic relationship as an extension of the Dutch Revolt's military culture. Maurits's opposition to the truce — and his execution of [[Johan van Oldenbarneveldt|Oldenbarneveldt]] — also marked the triumph of the war party and the prelude to the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force.



Prince Maurits is, in the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]] narrative, the principal Dutch royal actor of the early Dutch-Atjehnese relations, and the staff history's opening instance of the Dutch stadtholder's personal involvement in the diplomacy of the early Dutch East Indies. The school reader's emphasis on Maurits's reception of the Atjehnese embassy at the siege of Grave — and on the mock battle staged in the embassy's honour — frames Maurits as the courtesy counterpart to the sultan of Atjeh's courtesy in sending the embassy, and establishes the diplomatic reciprocity that the school reader would deploy in its account of the early Dutch-Atjehnese relationship.

## The 1601 embassy and the siege of Grave

The [[De Kleine Oosterling|Wijma school reader]] records Maurits's role in the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|1601 Atjehnese embassy]] as follows. The embassy arrived in the Netherlands during Maurits's siege of Grave: the prince was besieging the city of Grave when the Atjehnese arrived in the Netherlands. Maurits's reception of the embassy was elaborate: he sent his fine coach to fetch them, and paid them great honours. They handed over the letters and gifts to Maurits, while he was surrounded by a multitude of officers. The prince had them shown around the entire army camp and, in their honour, staged a great mock battle.

The Atjehnese embassy was thus received by Maurits in the field — at the siege of Grave, in the presence of his officers — and was honoured with a mock battle staged in its honour. The reception is the staff history's principal instance of the early-modern Dutch diplomatic practice of receiving non-European envoys in the field.


## The colonial policy

Maurits's role in the colonial policy of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] was that of the principal royal correspondent of the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] and the principal Dutch advocate of the war party against the truce. His letters and gifts to the sultan produced the diplomatic rapprochement that the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush of 1599]] had foreclosed; his opposition to the truce produced the political conflict that led to the execution of Oldenbarneveldt and the resumption of the war with Spain. The [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet of [[1607]] also bore a letter from Maurits to the Bandanese chiefs — a letter that, as Van der Woude notes, was "pasklaar gemaakt voor het beoogde doel" — drawn up for the purpose of demanding the spice monopoly and the right to build a fort.


## The letters to the Sultan of Atjeh

Maurits's first letter to the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] was transmitted by the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit of 1600]] — though the sultan set it aside unread, complaining (according to Van Caerden's report) that it was written on pigskin. The [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]] under Hans Huybrecht Tonneman bore a second letter from Maurits, with gifts in money and merchandise; the sultan received the Zeelanders solemnly, released [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] without ransom, and dispatched his own embassy to Maurits — the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|Atjeh embassy of 1601]]. The embassy arrived in the Netherlands during the siege of Grave, where Maurits received the Achehnese envoys in the field, sent his own carriage to fetch them, paid them great honours, and ordered a *spiegelgevecht* — a mock battle — to be staged in their honour.

The reception of the Achehnese embassy at the siege of Grave is the principal Dutch instance of the early-modern practice of receiving non-European envoys in the field — a practice that linked the Dutch Revolt's military culture to the emerging Dutch-Asian commercial diplomacy. The embassy's visit to Amsterdam, and the death of one of its members in the Netherlands, are also recorded by Van der Woude. The reception framed the Dutch-Achehnese diplomatic relationship as an extension of the Dutch Revolt's military culture, and the sultan's release of the Houtman captives and permission for a Dutch factory as the diplomatic achievement of Maurits's letter.


## The Twelve Years' Truce and the conflict with Oldenbarneveldt

The [[Twelve Years' Truce (1609)|Twelve Years' Truce of 1609]] was the principal political conflict of Maurits's career. He opposed the truce — particularly the colonial clause that limited Dutch trade in the East Indies — and used the theological-political conflict between Remonstrants and Contra-Remonstrants to undermine Oldenbarneveldt's position. The execution of Oldenbarneveldt in [[1619]] and the resumption of the war with Spain in [[1621]] marked Maurits's triumph and the prelude to the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force.
