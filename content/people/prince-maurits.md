**Prince Maurits** — *Maurits van Oranje* (Maurice of Orange, 1567–1625), Stadhouder of Holland and Zeeland from [[1585]] and of all the United Provinces from [[1590]] — was the principal Dutch military commander of the Dutch Revolt against Spain, the principal political opponent of [[Johan van Oldenbarneveldt|Johan van Oldenbarneveldt]] in the conflict over the [[Twelve Years' Truce (1609)|Twelve Years' Truce]], and the principal Dutch royal correspondent of the Sultan of [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. His letters and gifts to the Sultan produced the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|first Achehnese embassy to the Netherlands]] in [[1602]]–[[1603]] and inaugurated formal Dutch-Achehnese diplomatic relations. He is the principal Dutch royal actor of the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]] narrative of early Dutch-Atjehnese relations.

## Maurits's letters and gifts to the Sultan of Atjeh

Maurits's first letter to the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|Sultan of Atjeh]] — Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] — was transmitted by the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit of 1600]]. At the solemn reception of the Sultan by [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]], who was to deliver gifts and the letter, a Portuguese monk was present who drew attention to the fact that the letter — written on parchment made from the skin of the unclean pig — could not lawfully be touched by a Mohammedan. "Zoo miste's Prinsen brief zijn doel" — so the Prince's letter missed its mark. The episode is the [[Pigskin parchment letter incident at Atjeh (1600)|pigskin parchment letter incident]] that gives its name to chapter XXXII of *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie*.

The [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]] — four Zeeland ships — bore a second letter from Maurits, this time in Spanish, "waarin Prins Maurits aandrong op de vrijlating der gevangen Nederlanders, verzekerde dat de schepen hulp konden verleenen tegen vijanden van den Vorst, en men peper wilde koopen" — in which Prince Maurits pressed for the release of the imprisoned Dutchmen, assured [the Sultan] that the ships could lend help against his enemies, and proposed to buy pepper. The gifts accompanying the letter — like the letter itself — were welcome. The Sultan promised [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] "en zijn vrouw" (and his wife — for the prisoner's fidelity to his wife in Holland had made a strong impression) compensation for his material losses, and sent three ambassadors — [[Abdoel Hamid]], [[Sri Mohammed]] and [[Mir Hassan]] — to Europe on the returning Zeeland ships.

## The Atjeh embassy at the siege of Grave, 1602

"Prins Maurits belegerde Grave, toen het gezantschap in zijn kamp aankwam" — Prince Maurits was besieging Grave when the embassy arrived in his camp. A *staatsie-karos* (state-coach), attended by a company of cavalry, brought the Oriental gentlemen to a house near the Prince's tent, and after a meal Maurits received them surrounded by a brilliant company of nobles and military commanders.

After greeting the Prince in the Mohammedan manner, the ambassador — probably [[Sri Mohammed]] as the senior surviving envoy, after the death of [[Abdoel Hamid]] at Middelburg in August [[1602]] — delivered the Prince's earlier letter (now marked with the King's seal, "om te bewyzen, dat die brieven wel ontfangen, hem weert ende aenghenaem waren gheweest" — to prove that the letters had indeed been received, welcome and agreeable to him) together with two letters from Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] to the Prince.

The gifts presented were:

- A costly *kris*
- A golden dish and cup, "hierin bevond zich omtrent twee pont Camphora van Borneo, wesende het pont gheestimeert honderd ducaten daer te lande" — about two pounds of Borneo camphor, the pound there valued at a hundred ducats
- A lance, which was on one of the still-sailing ships

The Prince thanked "haren Coningh voor de presenten ende Gode voor haar voorspoedighe reyse ende aencomste" — their King for the presents and God for their prosperous voyage and arrival — and regretted that a slight indisposition prevented him from conducting the guests around the camp.

## The siege-works tour and the cannon-shots

Under the escort of the Counts Ernst and Lodewijk of Nassau, the Atjeh envoys inspected the siegeworks before Grave — "de belegeringsserrkuige, dat dinghen waren, weert om se te sien" — whereupon the principal ambassador discharged two *cartouwen* (cannon-shots) at the city in memory of the occasion. The Atjeh envoys thus participated actively in the Dutch war of independence from Spain. The [[De Kleine Oosterling|Wijma school reader]] records Maurits's role in the embassy: he sent his fine coach to fetch them, paid them great honours, and ordered a *spiegelgevecht* — a mock battle — to be staged in their honour.

## The envoys' fifteen months in the Republic

For fifteen months the ambassadors stayed in the Republic as guests of the East India Company, "gemerckt zyluyden tot dienst ende confirmatie van de negocie gecomen zijn" — since they had come in the service and confirmation of the trade — and then returned to their country, full of wonder at what they had seen: "soms met menschen, sleden, paarden en wagens over de gestolde wateren trekt" — that one sometimes travels over frozen waters with people, sledges, horses and wagons; "dat daar elk man zijn eigen meester en heer is" — that there every man is his own master and lord; "dat daar geen slaven waren, maar vrije menschen" — that there were no slaves, but free men; "die reeds dertig jaar lang oorlog gevoerd hadden tegen den grooten Koning van Spanje" — who had already fought for thirty years against the great King of Spain; "en dat zij toch rijk waren gebleven" — and yet had remained rich. The Sultan was described in the report as "glinsterende als de zon op den middag en als de maan, wanneer zij vol is" — shining like the sun at midday and like the moon when it is full.

## Maurits's equestrian portrait at Kandi

Prince Maurits's image also reached the inland courts of Asia by other routes. When [[Joris van Spilbergh|Spilbergh]] went to Kandi in [[1602]], he gave the King of Kandi an equestrian portrait of the Prince at the Battle of Nieuwpoort — a painting that was still hanging in the audience hall when [[Sebald de Weert|Sebald de Weert]] arrived later the same year. The King of Kandi was so impressed by Maurits's success against the Spaniards that he declared himself willing, "als metselaar kalk en steen te willen sjouwen" (as a mason to haul lime and stone), for a joint fortification against the arch-enemies.

## The Twelve Years' Truce and the conflict with Oldenbarneveldt

The [[Twelve Years' Truce (1609)|Twelve Years' Truce of 1609]] was the principal political conflict of Maurits's career. He opposed the truce — particularly the colonial clause that limited Dutch trade in the East Indies — and used the theological-political conflict between Remonstrants and Contra-Remonstrants to undermine Oldenbarneveldt's position. The execution of Oldenbarneveldt in [[1619]] and the resumption of the war with Spain in [[1621]] marked Maurits's triumph and the prelude to the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force. The [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet of [[1607]] also bore a letter from Maurits to the Bandanese chiefs — a letter that, as Van der Woude notes, was "pasklaar gemaakt voor het beoogde doel" — drawn up for the purpose of demanding the spice monopoly and the right to build a fort.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, Prince Maurits is the principal royal interlocutor of the Dutch Republic in the sultanate's early-seventeenth-century diplomacy — the Prince whose letters were carried to Atjeh in [[1600]] and [[1601]], whose reception of the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|1602 embassy]] at the siege of Grave placed Atjeh inside the Dutch war of independence from Spain, and whose alliance was sufficiently valued by Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] to be marked by a state-embassy, a *kris*, a golden dish and cup, and two pounds of Borneo camphor. The reception of the Achehnese embassy at the siege of Grave is the principal Dutch instance of the early-modern practice of receiving non-European envoys in the field — a practice that linked the Dutch Revolt's military culture to the emerging Dutch-Asian commercial diplomacy.
