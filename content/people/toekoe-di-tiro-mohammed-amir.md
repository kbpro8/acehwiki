**Teungku di Tiro Mohammed Amir** was the *Teungku di Tiro* — the principal *[[ulama]]* of the *kampong* of [[Tiro]] in [[Pedir]] — in the period immediately before and at the beginning of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], and the immediate predecessor of [[Teungku di Tiro|Teungku di Tiro (Cheh Saman)]] in that office. He is named in the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]] as *den beroemden Teungkoe di Tiro Mohammed Amir* — "the famous Teungku di Tiro Mohammed Amir" — and the same identification is preserved in the more analytical account of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *The Achehnese*, where he is called [[Teungku Tiro Muhamat Amin]] and dated to his death in [[December]] [[1885]].

## The Tiro office

The office of *Teungku di Tiro* was, in the late nineteenth century, the most influential single *ulama* position in [[Atjeh]]. The *kampong* of [[Tiro]] was the principal seat of Atjehnese religious learning and the burial-place of many distinguished *ulama*s, and its *Teungku* — by virtue of the chain of teacher-pupil transmissions that linked him to those buried scholars — exercised a religious authority that the territorial *[[Uleebalang|oeloebalang]]*s could not easily contest. Mohammed Amir was the holder of this office in the years immediately before the Dutch attack of [[1873]], and he held it through the first decade of the war.

## The succession of Teungku di Tiro (Cheh Saman)

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] records that the later and more famous [[Teungku di Tiro|Teungku di Tiro (Cheh Saman)]] — the commander-in-chief of the holy war of the [[1880s]] — was *vroeger de rechterhand* ("formerly the right-hand man") of Mohammed Amir. On Amir's death — which [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] dates to [[December]] [[1885]] — the office passed to this collateral relative rather than to Amir's son, on the principle that the son, though a good scholar, was too young for the position. The same pattern of senior-pupil succession is recorded in the [[Sjeih Abdoel bin Djnali]] → Mohammed Amir → Cheh Saman chain, and is the structural reason why the Tiro office retained its authority through several generations despite the absence of strict heredity.

## The "korte tijd" at Lamgoet

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] records that the *kampong* of [[Lamgoet]] — the former seat of [[Teungku di Tiro]] that had caused the Dutch generals [[Demmenie]] and [[Van Teijn]] so much trouble — was, in [[1893]], briefly occupied by a certain *Toekoe Bantah*. The album notes that this Toekoe Bantah could not maintain himself at Lamgoet, and that it was [[Toekoe Oemar]] who, in the same year, brought Lamgoet a second time under Dutch control. The reference connects the early-*ulama* phase of the war (under Mohammed Amir) to the late-*concentratie* phase (under [[Toekoe Oemar]] as Dutch ally) through the symbolic site of Lamgoet.

## Significance

Teungku di Tiro Mohammed Amir's significance in the documentary record of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] is as the bridge between the pre-war Tiro scholarly tradition and the late-war *ulama* resistance. His succession by [[Teungku di Tiro|Teungku di Tiro (Cheh Saman)]] — recorded by both the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]] and [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] — is the moment at which the Tiro office passed from a *prestige-of-learning* authority to a *prestige-of-resistance* authority, and the chain of teacher-pupil transmissions from [[Sjeih Abdoel bin Djnali]] through Amir to Cheh Saman is the structural backbone of the Tiro tradition.
