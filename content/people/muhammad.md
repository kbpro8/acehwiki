**Muhammad** was the founder of [[Islam]] and the final messenger (*rasulullah*) in Islamic belief, born in [[Mecca]] in [[570]] CE, receiving revelation beginning in [[610]] CE, and later becoming the central figure praised in the *Qasida al‑Burda* composed by [[Al-Busiri|al-Busiri]] (also known as [[Al-Busiri|Imam al‑Busiri]]). The poem’s Malay translation in the [[16th century]] spread his veneration throughout the Sultanate of [[Acheh]] and the wider Malay world, where it was incorporated into devotional practice and literary production.

## Historical Overview

Muhammad’s prophetic career began with the first revelation in [[610]] CE, culminating in the compilation of the Qur’an. His teachings spread rapidly across the Arabian Peninsula and, through trade and missionary activity, reached Southeast Asia by the early [[16th century]]. By that time the *Burda* had already become a celebrated work of Sufi devotion in the Arab world, and its translation into Malay allowed Acehnese believers to encounter Muhammad’s virtues directly in their own language.

## The *Burda* and Its Malay Translation

The *Burda*—formally titled *Qasida al‑Burda*—was composed by [[Al-Busiri|al-Busiri]] in the 13th century and quickly entered the Sufi canon. A 16th‑century Malay manuscript (catalogued as DLP‑54) preserves a translation of the poem, identified in the digitised source pages 37‑39 and 59‑65 of the OCR file *20260707_232246_DLP-54‑Een_16de_eeuwse_Maleise_vertaling_van_de_Burda_van_al_Bu_s_i_ri_OCR.txt*. The translation, further examined on pages 73‑79 of the same manuscript, retains the original’s rich imagery while rendering it in the Malay literary idiom of Aceh.

## Theological Significance in the *Burda*

The poem praises Muhammad with a series of exalted epithets—“a flower so tender, as the full moon so great, as the sea so lofty”—and recounts his participation in early‑Islamic battles such as [[Hunain]], [[Badr]] and [[Uhud]]. It celebrates his miraculous night journey ([[Isra]]) and ascension ([[Miʿraj]] and emphasizes his role as intercessor on the Day of Judgment, describing him as the “full sun of God’s grace.” These theological themes have been incorporated into Sufi devotional rituals throughout the Malay world.

## Role and Imagery in the Malay Translation

The Malay version preserves the original’s vivid imagery, likening the Prophet’s words to “pearls in the shell of the speaking mouth.” This phrasing, found in the manuscript DLP‑54, allowed the poem’s devotional language to resonate with Acehnese and broader Malay audiences, reinforcing the spiritual authority of the text in local religious poetry.

## Influence in Aceh

Muhammad’s life and teachings shaped Acehnese law, education, and literature. The Malay *Burda* was regularly recited in mosques and at royal courts, notably under [[Sultan Ibrahim of Aceh]], to affirm the sultanate’s self‑identification as a defender of the true faith. Its recitation contributed to the development of [[Acehnese religious poetry]] and reinforced the integration of Islamic doctrine into Acehnese cultural identity. Acehnese poets such as [[Zuhair (poet)]] embed Muhammad’s name and virtues in their verses, further embedding his presence in the literary imagination of the region.

## Contested Reception

While the *Burda* has been widely venerated, its praise of Muhammad was condemned by some [[Wahhabism|Wahhabi]] scholars as *shirk*. Anti‑Wahhabi scholars defended the poem’s legitimacy, arguing that its reverence aligns with orthodox Sufi practice rather than polytheism.
