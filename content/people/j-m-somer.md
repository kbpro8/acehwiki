**J. M. Somer** was a Dutch captain of infantry (*Captain of Infantry*) and military historian who authored the [[1935]] study *Het Atjeh-probleem van 1890-1900. De stimulans van Van Heutsz en Snouck Hurgronje*, published as an overprint from the *Journal / Organ van de Netherlands-Indies Officers Association*. His work analysed the shifting military-political systems applied during the [[Aceh War]] between [[1873]] and [[1890]] and argued that the absence of a consistent strategic line—not military weakness—had caused the prolonged stalemate.

## The problem of 1890-1900

Somer's essay, published in [[1935]], takes the appointment of [[Generaal Van Teyn]] and the subsequent rise of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as its central analytical thread. He notes that in the first sixteen years of the [[Aceh War]] (from [[1873]] to [[1889]]), thirteen [[Ministers of Colonies]], five [[Gouverneurs-Generaal]], and nine civil and military governors of [[Atjeh]] and its dependencies had raised conflicting opinions on the course to be followed in Atjeh. This rapid turnover of leadership produced what Somer describes as a "stelsel der stelselloosheid"—a system of systemlessness—in which blockades, shipping regulations, the [[Plan-Pel]], reconciliation policies, civil administration, attempts to restore the sultanate, and the [[Concentrated Line]] all followed one another without coherent strategic purpose.

The essay argues that the lack of continuity prevented the military from acting with decisive effect. Political interference repeatedly restrained military action: plans to attack [[Keumala]]—for instance, by [[Governor-General Van Lansberge]] in [[1879]]—were blocked because [[Generaal Van der Heyden]] did not consider them necessary. Meanwhile, the Dutch government underestimated the religious-political dimension of Atjehnese resistance, paying excessive honour to the pretender-sultan in [[Keumala]] rather than confronting the spiritual leaders who organised the resistance.

## The turning point of 1890

Somer identifies the establishment of the [[Corps Maréchaussée]] in [[April 1890]] as a symptom of changed insight into the long-criticised problem. From [[1890]] onward, the appointment of [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] and later [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] gradually replaced the old passivity with active aggression. The essay highlights the collaboration between [[Snouck Hurgronje]] and [[Van Heutsz]] as the decisive factor: their partnership produced the [[Pidië Expedition]] (starting [[1 June]] [[1898]]), the capture of [[Keumala]] ([[14 June]] [[1898]]), and ultimately the pacification of [[Groot-Atjeh]].

Somer emphasises that the two men, despite differing intellectual orientations—[[Snouck Hurgronje]] favouring ethnographic and religious understanding, [[Van Heutsz]] favouring military pragmatism—formed a harmonious operational team. Their cooperation was formalised through three government decisions in [[March 1898]] (the [[5 March]], [[15 March]], and [[25 March]] decrees) that appointed [[Snouck Hurgronje]] as adviser for Arabic and indigenous affairs and [[Van Heutsz]] as military and civil governor of [[Atjeh]].

## Method and sources

Somer's essay relies on the official *Nota's Politiek Beleid* (compiled by "Colijn" in [[1907]]), the work of [[H. T. Damste]] (*Atjeh-historie*, [[1916]]), [[Hotz]]'s *Beknopt geschiedkundig overzicht van den Atjeh-oorlog*, and private information from [[Prof. Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]]. The essay also references [[Van Heutsz]]'s [[1893]] brochure *De Onderwerping van Atjeh* and [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s [[1892]] report on the religious-political situation in [[Atjeh]].

## Significance

*Het Atjeh-probleem van 1890-1900* is a significant primary-source essay that consolidates the strategic debates of the final phase of the [[Aceh War]]. It records the transition from the failed alliance-policy under [[Deykerhoff]]—which placed excessive trust in [[T. Oema]]—to the active subjugation policy executed by [[Van Heutsz]] and guided by [[Snouck Hurgronje]]. Its value lies in its explicit argument that the war's prolongation was a political rather than a military failure.
