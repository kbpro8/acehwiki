**Sultan Ibrahim Mansjoer Sjuh** was the Sultan of [[Atjeh]] under whom [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Habib Abdoe'r Rachman]] was first introduced to the Atjehnese court — a sultan described in the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]] as a man of forty-eight years, of *vrij groot en gezet* (rather tall and stout) build, who felt himself *zwak en afgeleefd* (weak and worn out) through *overmatig gebruik van opium en vele uitspattingen* (excessive use of opium and many debaucheries). The Habib's introduction to Ibrahim Mansjoer Sjuh was the moment of his entry into Atjehnese court politics, and the Sultan's reliance on the Habib — together with the Sultan's own physical decline — was the proximate condition of the Habib's rise to the office of *Grootvizier*.

## The introduction of the Habib

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] records that [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Habib Abdoe'r Rachman]] was introduced to Sultan Ibrahim Mansjoer Sjuh by a certain *Mohammed Mahaldi*, himself a *Habib*. The Sultan — though only forty-eight years old — was already weakened by opium and excesses. His face bore the scars of *kinderpokken* (smallpox), a large *poliep* (polyp) disfigured his nose, and he wore *een slordige hoofddoek* (a slovenly head-cloth) and *een vuil, openhangend baadje* (a dirty, open-hanging shirt), on which the Turkish *Mesdjid-orde* (the Order of the Mosque) was pinned. The album's portrait is deliberately deflatory: it contrasts the *deftige* (respectable) appearance of the Habib with the *slordige* (slovenly) appearance of the Sultan, and the contrast is the visual argument for the Habib's centrality to the late sultanate.

## The Sultan's reliance on the Habib

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] records that the Sultan, *die veel tweespalt onder zijn hoofden te kampen had* (who had to contend with much dissension among his chiefs) and whose power had *gedaald* (declined), found in the *beschaafden en ontwikkelden Habib* (civilised and cultivated Habib) a *gewaarborgd medegever* (reliable adviser), through whose *wenken* (counsels) improvement came in the state of affairs and more order in the administration. The Habib, in turn, was called upon to assist the Sultan — before his death — in securing the succession of the young [[Toeankoe Machmoed|Toeankue Machmoed]], son of the in [[1837]] deceased Sultan [[Sultan Saleiman Iskander|Saleiman Iskander]], whom the Sultan intended as his successor on the *vermolmden troon* (decayed throne).

## The death of the Sultan and the succession

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] does not record the date of Ibrahim Mansjoer Sjuh's death, but the succession narrative places it after the installation of [[Toeankoe Machmoed]] as Sultan under the Habib's regency. The Sultan's reliance on the Habib during his lifetime — and the Habib's role in securing the succession of his chosen heir — was the political foundation of the Habib's rise to the office of *Grootvizier*, and the *zwak en afgeleefd* (weak and worn out) condition of the Sultan was the proximate condition of the Habib's pre-eminence.

## Significance

Sultan Ibrahim Mansjoer Sjuh's significance in the documentary record of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] is as the late Sultan of [[Atjeh]] whose physical decline and reliance on the Habib were the proximate conditions of [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Habib Abdoe'r Rachman]]'s political rise. The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]]'s deflatory portrait of the Sultan — smallpox-scarred, polyp-disfigured, opium-weakened, slovenly-dressed — is itself a document of the way in which the Dutch popular press of the late nineteenth century chose to represent the last sultans of Atjeh: as figures whose personal decline prefigured the political collapse of the sultanate.
