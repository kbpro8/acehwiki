**Teungkoe habib Moehamat bin Sajet Oesoeih** was the *[[Oeléebalang|oeléebalang]]* of the tiny *landschap* of [[Peudawa Rajeu|Peudawa Rajeu]] on the eastern coast of Atjeh. His *weidsche naam* — wide-sounding name — announces his Arabic descent: the title *[[Habib|habib]]* replaces the older *Teungkoe* in the Atjehnese style of those who claim *sajet* (sayyid) ancestry, and *sajets*, as [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] notes, *'hadden godsdienstigen adel en grooten invloed'* — possessed religious nobility and great influence. He wears the gold star of *trouw en verdienste* (loyalty and merit).

## Childhood and the Dutch Recognition
When the Netherlands government established itself in the eastern coast in the years around [[1873]]–[[1875]] and prevented the war of [[Idi]] and Peureula over Peudawa, Moehamat was already chief, *'een knaap van misschien 12 jaar'* — *a boy of perhaps twelve years* — while his mother had remarried with a chief of Baja. His upbringing may have suffered under those circumstances, but his loyalty to the government was not harmed. In [[1875]] he received from the government a large *schenkblad* — a presentation tray — of chased silver; later came the coffee-pot, the milk-jug, the whole service, and the old man of Broersma's visit gladly shows what remains of what he received as a child. Such gifts the government gave in those days to friendly chiefs: [[J. A. Kruyt|J. A. Kruyt]] records that the *vorst van Edi* received a silver coffee-service, another chief a golden watch, a third a silver rice-dish.

## Honours
In [[1895]] Teungkoe habib received the small star of *trouw en verdienste*; in [[1913]] he received the great star. He has preserved the charters. In [[1899]] he signed the *[[Korte Verklaring (1899)|Korte Verklaring]]* in three articles, which the government had then drawn up to replace the *Groote Verklaring* in eighteen articles. Every year, on [[31 August|31 August]], he sends a congratulation to the Queen, the Governor-General and the Governor of Atjeh, with much ceremony and Arabic phrases; some time afterwards there comes a letter from the *controleur* of Idi thanking him. The order for that reply is given by the Queen's Cabinet through the Ministry of Colonies. Broersma finds in that costly wish something touching, *'tenzij...'* — *unless...* (the implication being that the ceremony has become an empty formality).

## Means and Family
His fixed income — the *landschap* of Peudawa has only four hundred souls — is small: f 155 per month. His house is neither large nor beautiful; he is poor, in debt, and lives on advances. But he was rich in something else: *'bij 14 wettige vrouwen verwekte hij 17 kinderen'* — *with fourteen lawful wives he begot seventeen children.* Such an Oriental patriarchy is, Broersma notes, no *unicum* among the chiefs of Atjeh.

## Significance
Teungkoe habib is the type of the small *oeléebalang* whose loyalty to the Dutch was earned young and never faltered — a loyalty that became, in his old age, a matter of ceremony and keepsake rather than of political substance. His *schenkblad* and his silver service are the material survivals of the early Dutch policy of recognition-gifts to friendly chiefs, a policy documented in the literature of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] blockade-period.
