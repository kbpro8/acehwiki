**Fujiwara** was the Japanese major who founded and led the [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] — the Japanese intelligence organisation, also known as the [[F-organisatie|F-organisatie]] (F-organisation), that supported anti-Dutch elements in [[Atjèh|Atjèh]] prior to the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]] and that gave the fifth-column movement in Atjèh its distinctive character and its dangerous quality. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Fujiwara as the architect of the Japanese political preparation of the Atjèhnese terrain — the intelligence officer who, building on the lively trade relations between [[Penang|Penang]] and the Overwal that had been maintained since time immemorial, constructed the network of Atjèhnese collaborators whose activation would accompany the invasion.

## The founding of the Fujiwara-kikan

The [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] — literally "the Fujiwara organisation," deriving its name from its founder — was formed by the Japanese during their advance in Malaya from the Indonesians residing there. After the fall of [[Penang|Penang]] on [[19 December]] [[1941]], Piekaar records, "the construction of this organization could be begun. For on Penang, the lively trade relations, which had been maintained with the Overwal since time immemorial, were concentrated." The free port of Penang — the same entrepôt whose commercial dominance the Dutch had long tried to challenge through the development of [[Sabang|Sabang]] — provided the Japanese intelligence apparatus with the commercial and personal networks through which the F-organisatie's Atjèhnese agents could be recruited and supplied.

## The F-propagandists

The agents of the Fujiwara-kikan — the F-propagandists — were Atjèhnese political dissidents, religious figures, and descendants of the Prophet who had been recruited in Penang and sent back to Atjèh as "refugees" for the Japanese oppressor. Piekaar records that "as most of them were not politically conspicuous, they were, even though they were intercepted and arrested by the coast guard, after having obtained advice from the local Dutch and Indonesian administrative officials, in most cases released after a brief interrogation." The Dutch failure to recognise the danger of the F-organisatie's apparatus — a failure Piekaar attributes to the inconspicuous character of the agents — allowed the network to be activated when the invasion came.

## The 1942 activation

When the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]] came, the F-organisatie's network was activated. The F-propagandists [[Said Aboe Bakar|Said Aboe Bakar]] and [[Said Doellah|Said Doellah]] entered Atjèh with the first Japanese troops at [[Meulabóh|Meulabóh]] and thereupon assembled the governing commissions that displaced the loyal Dutch *oelèëbalang*'s. The intercepted letters found on Said Doellah — addressed to "the same group of persons" who would form the membership of the new commissions — revealed the extent of the F-organisatie's pre-invasion network. The activation of the network was, in this sense, the realisation of Fujiwara's pre-investment in the Atjèhnese terrain.

## The transition to Masubuchi

After the invasion, the Fujiwara-kikan was succeeded by the [[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi-kikan]] — the M-organisatie, under Major (later Lieutenant-General) [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]]. The transition from F- to M-organisation marked the shift from the intelligence-and-fifth-column phase to the occupation-administration phase. Piekaar notes that "in the Japanese Masubuchi, whom we shall get to know better in the second part of this work, this organization found for Aceh an extremely capable leader" — implying that the F-organisatie's apparatus, in Masubuchi's hands, became the institutional foundation of the occupation administration.
