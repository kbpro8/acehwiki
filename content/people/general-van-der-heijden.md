**General van der Heijden** was a Dutch military officer who served as **Governor of Acheh** from [[1877]] to [[1881]], and whose vigorous offensive campaigns through the Achehnese highlands first broke the spell of Dutch invincibility that had settled over the country after the disasters of [[1873]]–[[1876]]. He is one of the principal colonial figures of Snouck Hurgronje's Introduction to *The Achehnese*, where his governorship is treated as the decisive turn that ended the early defensive phase of the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]] and prepared the way for the systematic subjugation carried through under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]].

Snouck introduces van der Heijden as the man who finally taught the Dutch by experience "that while a defensive policy in no way advanced Dutch dominion, a vigorous offensive would make the enemy come to his senses. All Great-Acheh was conquered in this way". The earlier policy had been to occupy a few strong points — the former Sultan's residence and certain harbours in the dependencies — in the hope that the submission of certain principal chiefs would bring the rest of the country under Dutch authority. Van der Heijden's governorship revealed the fallacy of this reasoning: so long as the war-party could draw supplies and recruits from the un-subdued interior, the holding of fixed posts was a work of the Danaides.

The most famous fruits of the new policy were the **[[General van der Heijden's excursions|excursions through the highlands]]** — long marches of armed columns through the mountainous interior of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], which demonstrated to the Achehnese that no district was any longer beyond the reach of Dutch arms. Snouck records that "the marches of the Dutch troops through the highlands established the conviction that further resistance was impossible. The 'sons of the upper reaches of the river' bragged no more but took to flight; the most determined opponents of the invaders retired to distant hiding-places". Even the redoubtable [[Habib Abdurrahman|Habib Abdurrahman]] — the Hadrami sayyid who had assumed the leadership of the resistance — "was only too glad to bargain for a handsome yearly allowance in consideration of his submission". The "feeling towards" the peacefully inclined Achehnese, which had been poisoned by their attitude during the first and second Dutch expeditions, "began to amend" after the excursions of van der Heijden.

Yet the very success of the new policy bred a fresh error. In [[1881]] the colonial government, "thinking that now that the war seemed virtually at an end they could fall back upon a more defensive attitude and introduce a purely civil government", discontinued the offensive. The years [[1881]]–[[1884]] were needed to demonstrate that the establishment of widely extended military posts in Great Acheh and the introduction of a settled administration could never lead to the breaking down of the opposition in the dependencies; and in [[1884]] the disastrous [[Concentration Scheme|concentration-scheme]] was inaugurated instead. Snouck also notes that the Achehnese "constantly express their conviction that they were wrong in ever vacillating for a moment (as they did in the time of General van der Heijden), that Allah is manifestly on their side, and that the Dutch, infidels worn out by defeat after defeat and beset by sickness and other such troubles, must give in at last". This vacillation — the alternation of vigorous offensive with quiescent defence — was the chief cause of the protraction of the war, and van der Heijden's governorship marked the first clear demonstration of what the consistent application of the offensive could achieve.

## Account from *Boon, Van Lansberge* (1943) — K. van der Heijden

**K. van der Heijden** was a Dutch military officer who served as Governor and Military Commander of [[Aceh]] from [[January]] [[1878]] to [[March]] [[1881]], playing a pivotal role in the later stages of the [[Aceh War]]. Born in [[1826]], he rose to prominence through his successful campaigns in [[Aceh]], particularly the victory at [[Samalangan]], which earned him prestige with both Dutch forces and Acehnese chiefs.

## Early Career

Van der Heijden entered military service in the [[Dutch East Indies]] in [[1841]]. He rose through the ranks and was promoted to Colonel in [[1876]]. His decisive action in the [[1877]] campaign against [[Samalangan]], where he was wounded in the left eye, and his subsequent operations in the XXII Mukims, earned him a reputation as a capable and energetic commander.

## Appointment as Governor of Aceh ([[1878]])

Van Lansberge appointed Van der Heijden as Governor and Military Commander of [[Aceh]] in [[January]] [[1878]], a role that made him "even more independent of the army command" and "established the state of peace." Van der Heijden was given a governor's salary while retaining his military status, though this later created complications.

## Military Campaigns

### The XXII and XXVI Mukims Campaigns ([[1878]]-[[1879]])

Van der Heijden conducted aggressive operations in the interior:
- **Capture of Indrapoeri ([[24 March]] [[1879]])**: His forces captured the mosque at Indrapoeri.
- **Capture of Glé Jeuëng ([[9 June]] [[1879]])**: This operation proved costly but ultimately successful.
- **Arrest of T. Moeda Baet ([[9 May]] [[1879]])**: The recalcitrant chief was arrested and exiled to [[Banda]].

### The Habib Affair ([[1878]]-[[1879]])

Van der Heijden was instrumental in negotiating the "buying off" of [[Habib Abdul Rahman]], the influential religious leader who had been a major source of resistance. Van der Heijden proposed a yearly allowance of [[12,000 dollars]] for Habib's departure from Aceh—a method Van Lansberge supported but Van Bosse found distasteful.

### Samalangan ([[1880]])

Van der Heijden led an expedition to [[Samalangan]] that proved controversial. The operation was criticized by Van Goltstein for its lack of decisive results, though Van der Heijden defended it as necessary for Dutch prestige.

## Relationship with Van Lansberge

The relationship between Van der Heijden and Van Lansberge was complex:

1. **Initial support**: Van Lansberge initially praised Van der Heijden's energy and initiative, stating that "he has a great deal of initiative and interest in matters."

2. **Growing tensions**: Van Lansberge became frustrated with Van der Heijden's slow progress and his tendency to ignore advice from capable subordinates.

3. **Distrust**: By [[1879]], Van Lansberge wrote: "With regard to this, I have complete distrust, which is not diminished by the fact that he systematically turns the influence of capable advisers and seeks his inspirations either from his own, often absurd, interpretations or from incompetent persons. I cannot possibly bear the responsibility for the consequences of such a policy."

4. **The civil governance question**: Van Lansberge wanted to replace Van der Heijden with a civil governor, while Van der Heijden resisted. Van Lansberge wrote: "I would prefer a military governor, but there is no suitable person."

## Replacement

Van der Heijden was replaced in [[March]] [[1881]] by [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven]], the first civil Governor of Aceh. The transition was controversial:
- Van der Heijden was informed of his replacement only after the decision was made.
- He was promoted to Lieutenant-General as a consolation.
- The King was displeased with the manner of Van der Heijden's replacement.

## Legacy

Van der Heijden is a controversial figure in Aceh's colonial history:
- **Military achievements**: He was a capable field commander who won important victories.
- **Administrative limitations**: He proved less capable in peacetime governance.
- **The "purchase" method**: His negotiation with Habib established a precedent for dealing with Acehnese leaders.
- **Resistance to civil governance**: His resistance to civil governance contributed to tensions but also highlighted the challenges of the transition.
