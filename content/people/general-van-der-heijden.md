**General van der Heijden** was a Dutch military officer who served as **Governor of Acheh** from [[1877]] to [[1881]], and whose vigorous offensive campaigns through the Achehnese highlands first broke the spell of Dutch invincibility that had settled over the country after the disasters of [[1873]]–[[1876]]. He is one of the principal colonial figures of Snouck Hurgronje's Introduction to *The Achehnese*, where his governorship is treated as the decisive turn that ended the early defensive phase of the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]] and prepared the way for the systematic subjugation carried through under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]].

Snouck introduces van der Heijden as the man who finally taught the Dutch by experience "that while a defensive policy in no way advanced Dutch dominion, a vigorous offensive would make the enemy come to his senses. All Great-Acheh was conquered in this way". The earlier policy had been to occupy a few strong points — the former Sultan's residence and certain harbours in the dependencies — in the hope that the submission of certain principal chiefs would bring the rest of the country under Dutch authority. Van der Heijden's governorship revealed the fallacy of this reasoning: so long as the war-party could draw supplies and recruits from the un-subdued interior, the holding of fixed posts was a work of the Danaides.

The most famous fruits of the new policy were the **[[General van der Heijden's excursions|excursions through the highlands]]** — long marches of armed columns through the mountainous interior of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], which demonstrated to the Achehnese that no district was any longer beyond the reach of Dutch arms. Snouck records that "the marches of the Dutch troops through the highlands established the conviction that further resistance was impossible. The 'sons of the upper reaches of the river' bragged no more but took to flight; the most determined opponents of the invaders retired to distant hiding-places". Even the redoubtable [[Habib Abdurrahman|Habib Abdurrahman]] — the Hadrami sayyid who had assumed the leadership of the resistance — "was only too glad to bargain for a handsome yearly allowance in consideration of his submission". The "feeling towards" the peacefully inclined Achehnese, which had been poisoned by their attitude during the first and second Dutch expeditions, "began to amend" after the excursions of van der Heijden.

Yet the very success of the new policy bred a fresh error. In [[1881]] the colonial government, "thinking that now that the war seemed virtually at an end they could fall back upon a more defensive attitude and introduce a purely civil government", discontinued the offensive. The years [[1881]]–[[1884]] were needed to demonstrate that the establishment of widely extended military posts in Great Acheh and the introduction of a settled administration could never lead to the breaking down of the opposition in the dependencies; and in [[1884]] the disastrous [[Concentration Scheme|concentration-scheme]] was inaugurated instead. Snouck also notes that the Achehnese "constantly express their conviction that they were wrong in ever vacillating for a moment (as they did in the time of General van der Heijden), that Allah is manifestly on their side, and that the Dutch, infidels worn out by defeat after defeat and beset by sickness and other such troubles, must give in at last". This vacillation — the alternation of vigorous offensive with quiescent defence — was the chief cause of the protraction of the war, and van der Heijden's governorship marked the first clear demonstration of what the consistent application of the offensive could achieve.

## Account from *Boon, Van Lansberge* (1943) — K. van der Heijden

**K. van der Heijden** was a Dutch military officer who served as Governor and Military Commander of [[Aceh]] from [[January]] [[1878]] to [[March]] [[1881]], playing a pivotal role in the later stages of the [[Aceh War]]. Born in [[1826]], he rose to prominence through his successful campaigns in [[Aceh]], particularly the victory at [[Samalangan]], which earned him prestige with both Dutch forces and Acehnese chiefs.

## Early Career

Van der Heijden entered military service in the [[Dutch East Indies]] in [[1841]]. He rose through the ranks and was promoted to Colonel in [[1876]]. His decisive action in the [[1877]] campaign against [[Samalangan]], where he was wounded in the left eye, and his subsequent operations in the XXII Mukims, earned him a reputation as a capable and energetic commander.

## Appointment as Governor of Aceh ([[1878]])

Van Lansberge appointed Van der Heijden as Governor and Military Commander of [[Aceh]] in [[January]] [[1878]], a role that made him "even more independent of the army command" and "established the state of peace." Van der Heijden was given a governor's salary while retaining his military status, though this later created complications.

## Military Campaigns

### The XXII and XXVI Mukims Campaigns ([[1878]]–[[1879]])

Van der Heijden conducted aggressive operations in the interior:
- **Capture of Indrapoeri ([[24 March]] [[1879]])**: His forces captured the mosque at Indrapoeri.
- **Capture of Glé Jeuëng ([[9 June]] [[1879]])**: This operation proved costly but ultimately successful.
- **Arrest of T. Moeda Baet ([[9 May]] [[1879]])**: The recalcitrant chief was arrested and exiled to [[Banda]].

### The Habib Affair ([[1878]]–[[1879]])

Van der Heijden was instrumental in negotiating the "buying off" of [[Habib Abdul Rahman]], the influential religious leader who had been a major source of resistance. Van der Heijden proposed a yearly allowance of 12,000 dollars for Habib's departure from Aceh — a method Van Lansberge supported but Van Bosse found distasteful.

### Samalangan ([[1880]])

Van der Heijden led an expedition to [[Samalangan]] that proved controversial. The operation was criticized by Van Goltstein for its lack of decisive results, though Van der Heijden defended it as necessary for Dutch prestige.

## Relationship with Van Lansberge

The relationship between Van der Heijden and Van Lansberge was complex. Van Lansberge initially praised Van der Heijden's energy and initiative; growing tensions arose as Van Lansberge became frustrated with Van der Heijden's tendency to ignore capable subordinates. By [[1879]] Van Lansberge wrote: "With regard to this, I have complete distrust, which is not diminished by the fact that he systematically turns the influence of capable advisers." Van Lansberge wanted to replace Van der Heijden with a civil governor; Van der Heijden resisted.

## Replacement

Van der Heijden was replaced in [[March]] [[1881]] by [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven]], the first civil Governor of Aceh. Van der Heijden was informed of his replacement only after the decision was made; he was promoted to Lieutenant-General as a consolation. The King was displeased with the manner of the replacement.

## Account from *De Atjeh-Oorlog* by J.A. Kruijt (1896)

Kruijt's *De Atjeh-Oorlog* contains the most sustained and laudatory assessment of Van der Heijden in all the contemporary literature — and the most severe indictment of the decision to remove him.

Van der Heijden inherited the policy of *verzoening in Groot-Atjeh* (conciliation in Groot-Atjeh) and *krachtig optreden tegen de nog vijandige kuststaten* (vigorous action against the still-hostile coastal statelets). In [[January 1878|January]] [[1878]] he was definitively appointed *Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*. In [[June 1878|June]] [[1878]] [[Habib Abdul Rachman El-Zahir|Habib Abdul Rachman]] openly invaded the *stelling* with a great force, took the mountain passes, and besieged [[Kroeng Raba|Kroeng Raba]]; Van der Heijden had to return in haste with his troops from the coast. Reinforcements arrived; by mid-[[August 1878|August]] [[1878]] there were again over 10,000 troops in Atjeh. After Habib was driven out, the campaign in the [[22 Moekims|22]] and [[26 Moekims|26 Moekims]] was undertaken.

The conquest of the [[22 Moekims|22 Moekims]] began in [[March 1879|March]] [[1879]] and was completed by half [[June 1879|June]] [[1879]]. The conquest of the [[26 Moekims|26 Moekims]] began on [[1 July]] [[1879]] and was completed with full success in four days. Then there was still an expedition to [[Selimoen|Selimoen]], far in the interior. Remote chiefs submitted. All of Groot-Atjeh was Dutch. "*De schrik was er in*" (the terror was in them). The return march from Selimoen was a true triumph.

Towards the end of [[1878]] Van der Heijden ordered all trading vessels to call first at [[Oleh-leh|Oleh-leh]] before departing for the coasts, to be strictly searched and provided with passes — the beginning of the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]]. The Netherlands Indies government refused to accept this and ordered it withdrawn in [[February 1879|February]] [[1879]]; Van der Heijden reintroduced it in [[July 1879|July]] [[1879]] on his own responsibility. It was abolished in [[1881]] — greatly to be regretted, Kruijt writes, for it contained the principle of the scheepvaartregeling that ought to have been applied from the start.

The *Gouverneur-Generaal* Van Lansberge now judged the war ended and the time come for civil government. Against Van der Heijden's opinion, in [[April 1881|April]] [[1881]] [[Pruijs van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]] was appointed civil Governor. Kruijt's judgement is the most severe in the entire brochure: Van der Heijden's governorship was "*de éénige glans-periode in den ongelukkigen Atjeh-oorlog*" (the only period of glory in the unhappy Atjeh-oorlog). His displacement was "*de grootste fout, die wij hebben begaan*" (the greatest mistake we have made) — "*een ramp, waarvan wij de gevolgen nog thans te dragen hebben*" (a disaster whose consequences we are still bearing today). "*Zij sloopte in korten tijd het door van der Heijden opgetrokken gebouw en deed alle behaalde voordeelen weer verloren gaan*" (it demolished in a short time the building Van der Heijden had raised and caused all the advantages gained to be lost again).

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893)

In the [[1893]] tract *[[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|De onderwerping van Atjeh]]*, [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] places Van der Heijden as the hero of his narrative — the conqueror of the [[XXII Moekims|Sagi XXII]] and [[XXVI Moekims|Sagi XXVI]] of [[Groot-Atjeh]] in the campaigns of [[1878]]–[[1879]], the issuer of the [[Day-order of 5 September 1879|dagorder]] of [[5 September]] [[1879]] declaring that "geheel Groot-Atjeh ten onder gebragt" (all of Groot-Atjeh has been subdued), the General who twice closed the havens of Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden on his own personal responsibility (in [[1878]] and again on [[5 July]] [[1879]]), and the author of the masterly *Nota* of [[19 April]] [[1880]] on the proper form of government for Atjeh. He is the principal "what if" of Van Heutsz's narrative of the war's first twenty years — "ware hij niet in het begin van 1881 op onverantwoordelijke wijze ontslagen, dan behoorde de Atjeh-krijg thans reeds lang tot het verledene" — had he not been dismissed, in irresponsible fashion, in the beginning of 1881, the Atjeh-war would by now long belong to the past.

### Succession of Diemont (June 1877)

Van der Heijden succeeded [[Diemont|Generaal Diemont]] as Governor of Atjeh in June [[1877]]. Diemont had himself taken over from [[Wiggers van Kerchem]] in November [[1876]] and had completed the [[Pel (governor)|Pel]] plan's coastal line in February [[1877]], but was evacuated sick on [[30 June]] [[1877]] — "de Generaal Diemont, die 30 Juni 1877 wegens ziekte geevacueerd werd" — leaving the governorship to be filled, "eerst tijdelijk en later definitief in het bestuur vervangen door kolonel K. v. d. Heijden" (first temporarily and later definitively replaced in the administration by colonel K. v. d. Heijden). Van der Heijden inherited a situation in which G.-G. [[van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]]'s *verzoeningspolitiek* of March [[1877]] had given the Acehnese a year to re-arm, and in which the *onderhandelingen* of [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polem]] and [[Tekoe Moeda Baid|Toekoe Moeda Baid]] had proven insincere. His response, once the *inval* of [[Habib Abdoel Rachman]] in June [[1878]] had exposed the failure of the conciliation policy, was to abandon the coastal expeditions and attack the Sagi federations directly.

### The June 1878 strategic reversal

The decisive moment of Van der Heijden's governorship came in June [[1878]], when the *inval* of Habib Abdoel Rachman into the Dutch-occupied territory triggered a complete change of system in Groot-Atjeh. Van Heutsz dates it precisely: "de bekende inval in het door ons bezette gebied van Habib Abdoel Rachman voorviel, welke inval de aanleiding was tot het op Groot-Atjeh geheel veranderen van stelsel" — the well-known raid, by Habib Abdoel Rachman, into the territory occupied by us, which raid gave occasion to a complete change of system in Groot-Atjeh. The intellectual author of the reversal was Van der Heijden's Chief of Staff, [[Geij van Pittius|Major A. R. W. Geij van Pittius]] — "Het waren vooral de vertoogen van den pas opgetreden chef van den staf, den majoor A. R. W. Geij van Pittius, die den bevelhebber tot dit besluit brachten" — the representations of the newly-appointed chief of staff, Major A. R. W. Geij van Pittius, that brought the commander to this decision — who had argued since [[1877]] for the *Zuidoosterlinie* along the Atjeh river to be shifted into the XXII Moekims. Geij van Pittius "overtuigde den chef van den staf hem, dat men door expedities naar de kuststaten het doel — het einde van den oorlog — niet kon bereiken, en dat alleen eene beslissing kon worden verkregen door in Groot-Atjeh zelf den vijand aan te tasten" — convinced the chief of staff that the goal — the end of the war — could not be attained by expeditions to the coastal states, and that a decision could only be obtained by attacking the enemy in Groot-Atjeh itself.

Van der Heijden accepted the argument and abandoned the planned Pasangan expedition: "toen besloot de Generaal van der Heijden voorloopig van de expeditie naar Pasangan af te zien en met geheel zijne beschikbare macht voor goed te Groot-Atjeh aan het verzet een einde te maken" — then Generaal van der Heijden decided to refrain for the time being from the expedition to Pasangan, and to make an end, with all his available force, to the resistance in Groot-Atjeh for good. The full strength of the Atjeh garrison was concentrated against the Sagi federations, beginning with the XXII Moekims in July [[1878]].

### The XXII and XXVI Moekims campaigns

The campaign against the XXII Moekims opened in July [[1878]] — "de krijgsoperatiën in de XXII en XXVI Moekims, die in Juli van het vorige jaar zijn aangevangen" (the military operations in the XXII and XXVI Moekims, which began in July of the previous year) — and within three months had produced the submission of Habib Abdoel Rachman and [[Tekoe Moeda Baid|Toekoe Moeda Baid]]: "binnen drie maanden na den aanvang van den eersten veldtocht in de XXII Moekims, de onderwerping van Habib Abdoel Rachman en van Tekoe Moeda Baid op den 13en October 1878 volgde" — within three months of the beginning of the first campaign in the XXII Moekims, the submission of Habib Abdoel Rachman and of Toekoe Moeda Baid followed on [[13 October]] [[1878]]. The campaign against the XXVI Moekims followed in [[1879]], culminating in the [[Selimoen tocht (1879)|tocht naar Selimoen]] of September [[1879]]. Van Heutsz's verdict is sweeping: "In September 1879, nog geen 14 maanden na het begin van zijne zegerijke veldtochten, had Generaal v. d. Heijden aan alle verzet een einde gemaakt, was hij de door den vijand gevreesde overwinnaar van het aloude manfeliklijke Atjehsche rijk" — in September 1879, barely fourteen months after the start of his victorious campaigns, General Van der Heijden had made an end to all resistance and had become the conqueror, feared by the enemy, of the ancient manly Atjehnese kingdom.

### The day-order of 5 September 1879

Van der Heijden marked the completion of the conquest with a [[Day-order of 5 September 1879|dagorder]] issued from [[Kota Radja|Kota-Radja]] on [[5 September]] [[1879]], the text of which Van Heutsz reproduces in full in the 1893 tract. The dagorder opens: "Dagorder. Officieren, Ambtenaren, Onderofficieren en Soldaten! Met den veldtocht tegen de XXVI Moekims, schitterend besloten door den tocht naar Selimoen; is geheel Groot-Atjeh ten onder gebragt." — Day-order. Officers, Officials, Non-commissioned officers and Soldiers! With the campaign against the XXVI Moekims, brilliantly concluded by the march to Selimoen, all of Groot-Atjeh has been subdued. It closes with the formula "LEVE DE KONING!" (LONG LIVE THE KING!) and the signature: "KOTA-RADJA, 5 September 1879. De generaal-majoor, adjudant des Konings in buitengewonen dienst, Gouverneur tevens militair bevelhebber van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden. K. VAN DER HEUDEN" (Major-general, adjutant of the King in extraordinary service, Governor at the same time military commander of Atjeh and Dependencies. K. VAN DER HEUDEN). (The "HEUDEN" spelling is the OCR's reading of the printed "HEUDEN" / "HEIJDEN" signature block.) The dagorder was independently confirmed by the visiting Commandant van het Indische leger, who in the same month of September [[1879]] visited Atjeh and "bevestigde in zijn aan de Regeering ingediend verslag geheel, dat de Generaal in zijne zoo even aangehaalde dagorder met recht zeggen mocht, dat geheel Groot-Atjeh ten onder was gebracht" (confirmed entirely, in his report submitted to the Government, that the General in his just-quoted day-order was right to say that all of Groot-Atjeh had been subdued).

### The two haven-sluitingen (1878 and 1879)

Alongside the military campaign, Van der Heijden twice closed the havens of Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden — the first sustained Dutch attempt to translate territorial conquest into economic coercion. The first closure came in [[1878]]: "Reeds in 1878, na hervatting van de krijgsoperatiën in de XXII Moekims, liet hij daarom alle havens van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden, met uitzondering van [[Olehleh|Olehleh]] en [[Edi|Edi]], voor den invoerhandel sluiten" — Already in 1878, upon the resumption of the military operations in the XXII Moekims, he therefore had all havens of Atjeh and Dependencies, with the exception of Olehleh and Edi, closed for the import trade. The closure was revoked by G.-G. Van Lansberge by letter of [[7 February]] [[1879]] — "gelastte daarom bij brief van 7 Februari 1879 dien maatregel onverwijld in te trekken" (therefore ordered, by letter of 7 February 1879, that measure to be withdrawn without delay) — but Van der Heijden, "toen de Generaal opnieuw de vaste overtuiging kreeg dat, wilde men thans den oorlog beëindigd zien, de havens opnieuw moesten gesloten worden" (when the General once more gained the firm conviction that, if the war was now to be seen ended, the havens must again be closed), closed them a second time, "hij sloot ze ten tweedemale en gaf bij telegram d.d. 5 Juli 1879 aan den Gouv. Generaal te kennen … ditmaal op zijne persoonlijke verantwoordelijkheid" — he closed them a second time and informed the G.-G. by telegram of [[5 July]] [[1879]] … this time on his personal responsibility. The second closure, on his own responsibility, was reluctantly tolerated by the G.-G.

The two closures were the operational proof of Van der Heijden's strategic premise: that the submission of the Sagi federations could only be converted into the submission of the entire Atjehnese resistance if the coast — the channel through which the interior drew its supplies — were closed. The first closure had coincided with the XXII Moekims campaign; the second closure coincided with the XXVI Moekims campaign; the submission of the interior followed both. The episode became, in Van Heutsz's later tract, the model for the marine-enforced closure Van Heutsz would prescribe in [[1893]] and implement as Governor after [[1898]].

### The Nota of 19 April 1880

Van der Heijden's constitutional legacy is the *Nota* he submitted to the *Regeering* from Kota-Radja on [[19 April]] [[1880]] — the memorandum in which he set out the proper form of government for Atjeh after the conquest. Van Heutsz treats the Nota as the canonical Dutch text on Atjeh governance: "zijne, daarover aan de Regeering ingediende zeer belangrijke Nota, gedagteekend: Kota-Radja, 19 April 1880" (his, on that subject, very important Nota submitted to the Government, dated: Kota-Radja, 19 April 1880). The Nota proposed that the *controle op handel en scheepvaart* (control of trade and shipping) — the scheme [[Scherer|Scherer]] had first conceived and that Van der Heijden had himself twice enforced by the *haven-sluitingen* — be put on a proper *scheepvaartregeling* (shipping regulation) footing, with Dutch-administered ports levying duties on all Atjehnese trade. The proposal was, in effect, the first systematic Dutch plan for a *scheepvaartregeling*, and it became the textual foundation of every later Dutch proposal for the same instrument, including [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]]'s May [[1883]] scheme and the prescriptions of Van Heutsz's own 1893 tract. Van der Heijden had himself, as *Regeerings-commissaris* (Government Commissioner), proposed the *controle op handel en scheepvaart* to the *Regeering* — "deze zelf met den Generaal van der Heijden, als Regeerings-commissaris, aan de Regeering eene contrôle op handel en scheepvaart had voorgesteld" — before his Nota of [[19 April]] [[1880]] reduced the proposal to writing.

### The "onverantwoordelijke" dismissal of early 1881

Van der Heijden's governorship was cut short by his dismissal in early [[1881]]. Van Heutsz's verdict is one of the most quoted lines in the 1893 tract: "ware hij niet in het begin van 1881 op onverantwoordelijke wijze ontslagen, dan behoorde de Atjeh-krijg thans reeds lang tot het verledene" — had he not been dismissed, in irresponsible fashion, in the beginning of 1881, the Atjeh-war would by now long belong to the past. The dismissal was the act of G.-G. Van Lansberge, against whose policies Van der Heijden had repeatedly closed the havens on his own responsibility; the immediate consequence was the appointment of a *Civiel Gouverneur* (Civil Governor) — "na het ontslag van den Generaal van der Heijden, en diens vervanging door een Civiel gouverneur" — and the abandonment of Van der Heijden's *scheepvaartregeling* programme.

Van Heutsz's counter-factual is sustained. "De Generaal moest heengaan" (the General had to go), Van Heutsz writes, "en hoe zijn opvolger deed wat hij later niet schreef en weinig deed van hetgeen hij later wel schreef en daardoor het schoone door den Generaal van der Heijden opgetrokken gebouw in korten tijd sloopte" — and how his successor did what he later did not write, and did little of what he later did write, and thereby in short time demolished the fine edifice General Van der Heijden had erected. The conclusion is the tribute of the entire book: "De Generaal van der Heijden had daardoor in werkelijkheid wat niemand vóór hem en tot dusverre nog niemand na hem heeft gehad, te Groot-Atjeh den brand onder den voet" — General Van der Heijden had thereby, in reality, what no one before him and so far no one after him has had: in Groot-Atjeh, the fire under the foot. Van der Heijden had had the war won. The broader verdict: "Atjehers hadden ons leeren kennen in onze kracht, vooral tijdens het bestuur van Generaal van der Heijden" — the Atjehnese had come to know us in our strength, above all during the administration of General Van der Heijden — and the loss of that prestige, after his dismissal, was the loss the next twelve years of Dutch policy could not recover. (Van der Heijden would return to the governorship briefly in [[1886]]–[[1887]].)
