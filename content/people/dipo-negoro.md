**Dipo Negoro** (Pangeran Diponegoro, c. 1786–1855) was a Javanese prince of [[Yogyakarta]] whose revolt against the Dutch — the [[Java War (1825-1830)|Java War]] of [[1825]]–[[1830]] — kept the Netherlands-Indian authorities fully occupied in Java and prevented any effective intervention on [[Sumatra's East Coast|Sumatra's East Coast]] until the close of the decade. He is mentioned once in [[P. H. van der Kemp]] (1900), in a footnote to [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minute on Acheh of 16 March 1828.

## The footnote

The footnote, appended to Fullerton's discussion of the projected Dutch [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] to [[Siak]] and of "the probable proceedings of the Netherlands authorities in respect to Sumatra, whenever their affairs in Java will permit," runs simply: "(a) Bedoeld wordt de opstand van Dipo Negoro" — that is, "There is reference to the revolt of Dipo Negoro."

The point is significant. Fullerton, writing from [[Penang]] in [[1828]], assumed that the Dutch would, as soon as they were free of their Javanese entanglements, turn their attention to Sumatra; the editors of Van der Kemp's article add the explanatory note, since by 1900 the name of Dipo Negoro's revolt would not necessarily have been self-evident to a Dutch reader who had not lived through the 1820s.

## The Java War and Sumatra policy

Dipo Negoro's revolt broke out in July 1825, after the Dutch began to build a road across a piece of land that he held to be sacred. It rapidly developed into a widespread guerrilla war, drawing in large sections of the Javanese aristocracy and peasantry, and lasted until Dipo Negoro's capture by treachery at Magelang in March 1830. The war cost the lives of some 8,000 European soldiers and an estimated 200,000 Javanese, and placed an enormous strain on the finances of the Netherlands-Indian government. [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Du Bus de Gisignies]] (governor-general 1826–1830) and his successor [[Johannes van den Bosch|Van den Bosch]] (1830–1834) were preoccupied with the war and its aftermath; substantial forces could not be spared for Sumatra.

This is exactly the situation Fullerton had in mind when, in his minute of [[1828]], he predicted that the Dutch "will avail themselves of the exclusive right which they conceive vested in them by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Treaty of 1824]] of forming political relations with the Chiefs of Sumatra, to the full extent of their means … as soon as their affairs in Java will permit." The Dutch, in other words, had political and treaty claims on Sumatra, but lacked the military resources to enforce them while the Java War continued. The British at [[Penang]] and [[Singapore]] were therefore free, between [[1824]] and the early 1830s, to maintain their commercial and political connections with the east-coast Sumatran states — [[Siak]], [[Deli]], [[Langkat]], [[Batu Bara]] and others — without serious Dutch competition.

## Significance for the article

Van der Kemp's mention of Dipo Negoro is a single footnote, but it points to the larger strategic context of the article: the British in the Straits of Malacca took their reading of Dutch intentions from a realistic assessment of Dutch capacity, and Dutch capacity was, for most of the period covered by the article, absorbed by Dipo Negoro's revolt. The Acheh reservation in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] had committed the Dutch to a vague sphere of influence over Acheh; the Java War meant that, for the time being, that commitment remained, in Van der Kemp's phrase, a [[Dead letter|dead letter]].
