**Teungkoe Koeta Karang** — also written *T. Koeta Karang* and modern *Teungkoe Kutakarang* — was a great [[Achehnese]] Islamic scholar of the [[XXII Moekims]] whose influence, acquired through the practice of *bertapa*, ascetic meditation in a cave, shaped the religious politics of [[Groot-Atjeh]] in the years before his death on [[8 December]] [[1895]], as recorded in A. Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*. His death opened a succession contested between [[Teungkoe Tanah Abée]] (who inherited his authority) and the rival claimants aligned with [[Teuku Umar]], and his earlier practice of *kaloet* in the [[Grot of Daroe|grot of Daroe]] established a model of religious authority that would be invoked by later figures, including [[Teungkoe Panté Gelimah]] in the spring of [[1896]]. Though dead before the central events of *ATJEH '96* begin, Koeta Karang is one of the most consequential background figures in the balso, because his theological doctrine had shaped the apparent ease with which [[Teuku Umar]]'s legion had operated in the XXII Moekims before the defection.

## Theological Doctrine and Influence over the XXII Moekims

Kruisheer describes Koeta Karang as a *prominent oelama* in the XXII Moekims who had been "bought off by Oemar" before the defection — that is, his religious authority had been turned, by persuasion or bribery, to Umar's service. His most consequential doctrine was the preaching "that dying in battle against Oemar's legion did NOT guarantee admission to the Islamic heaven, while dying against the Dutch did". This doctrine explains what would otherwise be puzzling in the colonial record: the near-total absence of serious Achehnese resistance to Umar's operations between [[1893]] and [[1895]] — operations which had culminated in the [[30 November]] [[1893]] conquest of [[Ana' Galong]] and the [[4 August]] [[1895]] recapture of the *bentengs* of [[Glé Broë]] and [[Koeta T. Rajoet]]. The fights which Umar's legion had engaged in during this period were, Kruisheer notes, "sham battles in optima forma" — theatrical displays of resistance that satisfied the formal requirement of combat without endangering Umar's forces. Koeta Karang's theological sanction was the mechanism by which this theatre was made religiously permissible, and his influence thus directly enabled the Dutch *bindingspolitiek* to function in the XXII Moekims.

## The Kaloet Practice and the Grot of Daroe

Koeta Karang's authority rested, in significant part, on the *kaloet* — the Achehnese term for *bertapa*, the practice of ascetic meditation in a cave which was believed to confer baraka (spiritual power) upon its practitioner. The [[Grot of Daroe|grot of Daroe]] in the [[III Moekims Daroe]] was the site of his retreats, and Kruisheer reports that [[Teungkoe Panté Gelimah]] "stayed at [[Mata Air]] (Daroe) with 30 Pedireezen and did *bertapa* in the grot of Daroe, *misschien wel in navolging van T. Koeta Karang, die daarmede destijds nog al invloed verworven had*" (perhaps in imitation of T. Koeta Karang, who had thereby acquired considerable influence in his day). The *kaloet* tradition thus appears in *ATJEH '96* as a recognised mechanism for the production of religious authority within the Achehnese religious landscape, comparable to the *oelama* authority of [[Teungkoe Tanah Abée]] (Koeta Karang's successor) and the *Habib* authority of the Hadhrami descendants of the Prophet. Koeta Karang's death on [[8 December]] [[1895]] — barely four months before Umar's defection — was, in this sense, a foundational event for the religious politics of [[1896]]: it removed the figure whose sanction had made Umar's pre-defection operations religiously tolerable, and it transferred his authority to Tanah Abée, who promptly reversed the doctrine and labelled Umar a *kafir*.

## Significance

Teungkoe Koeta Karang's significance lies in the paradox that his death — rather than his life — was the proximate religious cause of Umar's crisis. As long as Koeta Karang lived, his authority neutralised the XXII Moekims' resistance to Umar; with his death, that constraint was removed, and his successor [[Teungkoe Tanah Abée]] immediately labelled Umar and his bands as unbelievers and called Umar's murder "a work pleasing to God". This reversal forced Umar either to abandon his Dutch alliance and seek legitimacy as an Achehnese resister — which is what he did on [[29 March]] [[1896]] — or to lose the religious cover under which his legion had operated. In this sense, Koeta Karang's death on [[8 December]] [[1895]] is one of the long-arc causes of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]] and therefore of the entire crisis of the first semester of [[1896]].
