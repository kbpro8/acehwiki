**Nja‑Makam** was the *bendehoofd* (bandit chief) of an Atjeh‑aligned insurgent group that entrenched itself in the **Tamiang** region in late **[[1892]]**. Dutch colonial forces under **[[Colonel Van de Pol|Van de Pol]]** launched a punitive expedition against his forces in **[[February]] [[1893]]**, marking the first major clash between the **Royal Netherlands Navy** and the **Atjeh** insurgency in that area. The leader’s activities are recorded in the Dutch naval yearbooks of the 1890s.

## Context

The **Tamiang** river basin, part of the interior of **[[Sumatra]]**, became a focal point of resistance after the **Atjeh** chief **Nja‑Makam** settled there in **[[1892]]**. His group allied with local **Atjeh** fighters, prompting the colonial administration to consider a military response.

## Military Action

On **[[13 February]] [[1893]]**, **[[Colonel Van de Pol|Van de Pol]]** led a force of 130 infantrymen from **[[Deli]]** together with a **[[Dutch Navy|naval]]** flotilla to confront **Nja‑Makam**. The expedition ultimately forced the insurgents to retreat, though the conflict continued in subsequent months.

## Significance

**Nja‑Makam** exemplifies the localized leadership that complicated Dutch attempts to pacify **[[Acheh]]**. His brief control of the **Tamiang** area illustrates the fluid nature of resistance networks in the late‑nineteenth‑century **[[Aceh War]]**.

## Account from *De Atjeh-Oorlog* by J.A. Kruijt (1896)

In [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]]'s *De Atjeh-Oorlog* ([[1896]]), Nja Makam is one of the principal instances of the Dutch policy of conciliation applied to an irreconcilable enemy.

In the period of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] under [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] (1884–1886), the principal leaders of the Atjehese resistance bound themselves afresh under oath against the Dutch, among them the later Dutch "friends" [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] and Nja Makam. In [[January 1886|January]] [[1886]] Nja Makam raided [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] and plundered in [[Langkat|Langkat]]; many statelets on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] helped him. The raid demonstrated the reach of the Atjehese resistance beyond the borders of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] into the neighbouring Dutch-protected territories.

In [[1892]]–[[1893]], at the same time that [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] was being armed and paid by the Dutch as their principal Atjehese auxiliary, Nja Makam — as the Sultan's authorised agent, provided with the Sultan's *tjap* (seal) — travelled the coastal statelets raising money and people to the East Coast as far as [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] to cause unrest. He brought the Dutch tobacco and petroleum districts into violent turmoil, and the Dutch had the greatest difficulty in [[1892]] and [[1893]] to drive him from his positions on the [[Tamiang-rivier|Tamiang-rivier]].

While Toekoe Oemar was the Dutch ally, more and more of his friends and associates came to nibble at the Dutch state trough, among them the longstanding bitter enemy Nja Makam. The incorporation of Nja Makam into the Dutch pay system was, Kruijt writes, one of the most striking instances of the Dutch policy of buying off irreconcilable enemies — a policy that brought on the Dutch "de diepe, innige verachting van vriend en vijand" (the deep, intense contempt of friend and foe alike).
