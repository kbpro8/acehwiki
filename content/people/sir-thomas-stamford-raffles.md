Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles ([[1781]]–[[1826]]) was a British colonial administrator who, as Lieutenant-Governor of [[Bencoolen]] in [[1819]], negotiated the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]] and, in the same expedition, co-founded the settlement of [[Singapore]] with [[Major William Farquhar]]. In the Dutch scholarly literature his Acheh mission is treated as a representative case of British intrigue against Netherlands India in the years surrounding the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]].

## Background and Appointment at Bencoolen

Raffles had served under [[Lord Minto]] in the British interregnum on [[Java]] ([[1811]]–[[1816]]) and was subsequently appointed Lieutenant-Governor of the West Sumatran settlement of Bencoolen. From that post he watched with growing unease the restoration of Dutch authority in the archipelago after the [[Convention of 24 January 1817]], and he repeatedly pressed the Bengal government to take a firmer line against what he represented as Dutch monopolising tendencies. Although the political superintendence of Achehnese affairs had been assigned by Calcutta to the government of [[Penang]], Raffles Intrigued himself into the question on the ground that the Netherlands India authorities were said to have offered support to the rival claimant at [[Pedir]].

## The Acheh Commission of 1819

In late [[1818]] Raffles travelled from Bencoolen to [[Calcutta]] to lay before [[Lord Moira]] the danger that the Dutch might come to monopolise the China route through the Straits. [[Lord Moira|Moira]], already alarmed by the rejection of [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] at [[Pontianak]] by [[Van Boekholz]] and the annulment of Farquhar's treaty at [[Riouw]] by [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]], decided to dispatch a joint commission to [[Acheh]]. Its members were to be Raffles and [[Captain Coombs]], the latter having already conducted [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|a preliminary mission]] under [[Governor Petrie]]. Raffles, as the senior commissioner, was given a casting vote.

The commission reached [[Penang]] early in [[1819]] and immediately collided with [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Governor Bannerman]], who held that the Acheh instructions had to be executed strictly and that the artillery detachment Raffles had brought from Bengal was intended for the Acheh operation rather than for the relief of the Bencoolen garrison. Raffles, on the contrary, claimed a discretion to act as circumstances dictated, and on [[19 January 1819]], acting on a private communication from [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] concerning the advantages of the [[Karimon Islands]], he suddenly weighed anchor and left Penang without taking leave of either Bannerman or Coombs — his [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|detour to the Karimons]] leading directly to the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]].

## Treaty with Sultan Jauhar Alum

Returning to Penang on [[16 February 1819]], Raffles resumed the Acheh commission. He anchored off [[Banda Acheh|the capital]] on [[14 March 1819]] and spent nearly seven weeks there, during which he and Coombs conducted what he himself described as a [[paper war|«paper war»]] occupying more than a thousand pages of the Company's largest-sized paper. Raffles eventually carried his point: the commission sailed to [[Pedir]] and on [[22 April 1819]] concluded the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]]. The sultan received arms and 100,000 rupees; in return he undertook to exclude other European powers and to admit a British resident. Even before sailing for Pedir Raffles had taken the unilateral step of [[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir|dispatching the ship *Minto*]] under [[Lieutenant Criddle]] to notify Sultan Jauhar Alum of the commission's approach, a proceeding that the Bengal government later described as contrary to the spirit of its instructions.

## Subsequent Career and Assessment

The [[Calcutta]] government, while ratifying the treaty, treated it coolly: it observed that the Sultan had no real authority at the capital, that the funds had been negotiated at Penang rather than paid to the Sultan, and that the engagements entered into were unlikely to be observed. Within a few years the treaty had become an acknowledged [[dead letter]], a status confirmed by the London authorities in their dispatch of [[4 August 1824]]. In the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] negotiations, the British plenipotentiaries [[George Canning]] and [[Charles Watkin Williams Wynn]] raised the Acheh engagement at the eleventh hour, and the Dutch, having no copy at hand, were led into acknowledging British interests at Acheh — a manoeuvre that the Dutch historian [[P. H. van der Kemp]] treated as characteristic of Raffles's wider diplomatic style.

Van der Kemp's portrait of Raffles in *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* ([[1900]]) is sharply critical but acknowledges his «great good sense» and «genial insight»; the historian contrasts his decisiveness with the bureaucratic caution of [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]], even while condemning the «over-driving» by which he set aside his colleagues and superiors. Raffles's own justification of his conduct during the commission is preserved in his letter to [[O. F. Metcalfe]] of [[30 September 1819]] (printed as Appendix 5 of Van der Kemp's article) and in his well-known letter to [[Sir Robert Harry Inglis]] of [[12 January 1819]], in which he declared that he had «never had a more disagreeable duty».

## Account from *Als officier van gezondheid naar Nederlandsch-Indië* (Wøller) — Fort Marlborough and the Anglo-Dutch treaty

Dr. [[Johan Wøller]], sailing north to [[Atjeh en Onderhorigheden|Atjeh]] in [[April]] [[1904]], put ashore at [[Benkoelen]] to view [[Fort Marlborough]] — "a real fort with walls and bastions and lunettes, where old cannons still powerless as greybeards stood threatening invisible enemies, while heavy balls rested in neat pyramids beside them." Wøller linked the fort directly to Raffles: "From here ruled Sir Stamford Raffles for five years over Sumatra, after England had to return Java to Holland; this was his last temporary grip on the Indies, which he had governed with such great foresight and as no white man before him had loved and understood." He noted that Raffles's residence there was later destroyed "by an earthquake — on the same day that the Austrian ultimatum was sent to Serbia" — and, contrasting Benkoelen's quiet decline with the settlement Raffles secured for Britain, credited him with having obtained, on relinquishing the fort, "a brilliant compensation — Singapore — which forever prevented that Further Asia became a closed Dutch continent."

Wøller also identified Raffles as the architect of the restrictive [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] that bound Dutch policy toward Atjeh for the following half-century: it "was still the work of Raffles to prevent that the Netherlands would control both sides of Singapore Strait Malacca."
