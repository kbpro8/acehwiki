A leading figure in the early phases of the Aceh War, in the Hikayat Prang Gompeuni. His followers occupied Mengat in early preparations, and he was a chief speaker in the council held in the Dalam when Dutch ships first arrived demanding submission.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teuku Kali

**Teuku Kali** is the Achehnese court title borne by the holder of what was originally the office of chief justice (*kadi*) of the Sultanate of [[Acheh]]. The most celebrated bearer of the title is the **Teuku Kali Malikon Ade** (Qadlu Malikul Adil, "Judge of the righteous King"), whose secularisation of the office and conquest of an *uleebalangship* of twelve *gampongs* on the right bank of the [[Acheh River]] is described elsewhere; but the title itself, and the smaller rival title-bearers who shared it, formed a distinct class of rivals to the hereditary *uleebalangs*.

In the political life of Acheh the Teuku Kali was a **title-bearer without territory** — at least in the original conception of the office — and as such was a typical example of those ambitious men who, in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s phrase, "fight the *uleebalangs* with their own weapons." Such men aimed more or less openly at the *uleebalangship*, the zenith of worldly greatness in the eyes of the Achehnese, and used their position to gain themselves followers and to outshine the *uleebalang* in whose district they resided, so as to compel him either to make them great concessions or to vacate the field in their favour. Snouck Hurgronje instances [[Teuku Nanta Seutia]] and Teuku Kali as examples of this kind of rivalry.

The Teuku Kali also appears in [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]]'s pamphlet to the Dutch Government among the four — Teuku Kali, [[Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa]], the [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] and the [[Imeum of Lueng Bata]] — on whose counsel the Sultan was obliged to depend. Teungku Tiro argued that the Gompeuni should have remembered that the Sultan could do nothing without consulting these four; that these four were in their turn dependent upon the decision of the three *panglimas* of the *sagis*; that the latter had no power without the acquiescence of the seven *kawoms*; and that the people themselves could only act in accordance with the determination of the *ulamas*, "who derive their knowledge from Allah and his Apostle." The pamphlet thus places the Teuku Kali among the constitutional counsellors of the Sultan, while making clear his subordination, in the last resort, to the religious party.

Although the Teuku Kali was originally a *kadi* — a judge learned in the religious law — the hereditary nature of the office in time produced holders who could neither read nor write. [[T. H. der Kinderen|Mr. Der Kinderen]] "quite missed the mark" in finding anything extraordinary in the fact that the "hereditary supreme judge" of the kingdom could neither read nor write: the Teuku Kali was *uleebalang* of twelve *gampongs*, and shared this ignorance with many of his equals in rank. Those who were charged with administering justice according to religious law could all read and write, and most of them had carried their learning much further than these rudiments. Even in the rare cases where the Sultan of Acheh had need of the advice of those learned in the law, his real adviser was [[Cheh Marahaban]] and not the Teuku Kali.

The office of Teuku Kali also had associated with it the position of **kali raja**, which in the later years of the Sultanate was held by Cheh Marahaban. The example of Cheh Marahaban, "the earlier kali raja and ulama of Teuku Kali," showed — at the time when the Dutch still occupied a great portion of Acheh — that some of the *ulamas* would have been quite willing to change sides if they could only have found conclusive arguments strong enough to counterbalance the contumely attending such a step.
