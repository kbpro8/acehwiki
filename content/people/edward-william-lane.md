**Edward William Lane** ([[1801]]–[[1876]]) was an English orientalist, translator, and lexicographer whose celebrated work *Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians* — first published in [[1836]] and reaching a fifth edition in Snouck Hurgronje's lifetime — became one of the standard European accounts of everyday Mohammedan life in an Arabic-speaking country. He is cited by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in *The Achehnese* alongside [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]] and [[E. Sell|E. Sell]] as an authority on the popular Mohammedan usages that have spread, with local variations, from Cairo to Hindustan and the Eastern Archipelago.

## Citation in *The Achehnese*

Lane is cited in connection with the cooking of special viands on [[Ashura]] — the tenth of Muharram, the day of the martyrdom of [[Husain]] at [[Kerbela]]. The custom is described by Snouck Hurgronje as "really no more than an insignificant adjunct of the Hasan-Husain festivals, but ... exist[ing] elsewhere as an independent usage". In Hindustan the chosen dish seems to be that known as *khichri*; in Cairo it is called *hubub* — i.e. "seeds" or "grains" — a usage recorded by Lane (*Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians*, 5th edn., II, p. 149). The parallel custom in Java is the *bubur sura*, made of various grains or seeds such as maize and peas mixed with pieces of cocoanut, while in Acheh itself the corresponding dish is the *kanji Ashura* cooked on the Ashura day and the days following.

## Significance for Achehnese comparative studies

The citation of Lane is characteristic of Snouck Hurgronje's method in *The Achehnese*. By placing the practices of the Achehnese side by side with those of the modern Egyptians (as described by Lane), the Deccanese (as described by [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]] and [[E. Sell|Sell]]) and the Javanese, he is able to bring out both the wide diffusion of certain popular Mohammedan usages and the distinctive local colour they assume in Acheh. Lane's *Manners and Customs* — based on prolonged residence in Egypt in the 1820s and 1830s — supplies the Cairo comparison; and the fact that the same Ashura dish is called *hubub* in Cairo, *bubur sura* in Java, and *kanji Ashura* in Acheh, while in Hindustan it is *khichri*, is presented as a concrete instance of the larger diffusion of popular Mohammedan custom from the central lands of Islam outward to the Archipelago.
