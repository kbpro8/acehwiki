**Majoor Lubeck** was a major of the infantry of the [[Oost-Indisch Leger|Netherlands East Indies Army]] who commanded, in August [[1879]], the column that marched from [[Indrapoeri]] along the [[Acheh River|Atjeh-rivier]] to [[Selimoen]] — the eastern arm of the converging movement that, together with the [[Lamtoba column (1879)|Lamtoba column]] of Luitenant-Kolonel [[Majoor Gerlach|Gerlach]], carried out the [[Selimoen Expedition (August 1879)|Selimoen expedition]] of [[13 August|13]]–[[15 August]] [[1879]] narrated in [[W. Cool]] and [[G. B. Hooijer]]'s *[[Eene schoone bladzijde uit Atjeh's geschiedenis (Cool & Hooijer, 1889)|Eene schoone bladzijde uit Atjeh's geschiedenis]]* ([[1889]]). With his column marched the *controleur* [[Controleur Kamp|Kamp]].

## The approach on Selimoen

The [[March order for the Selimoen expedition (11 August 1879)|order of 11 August 1879]] had prescribed that Lubeck's column, advancing from Indrapoeri along the Atjeh river, and Gerlach's column from [[Lamtoba]], should answer one another's *vuurpijlen* — signal rockets sent up at seven in the evening, answered half an hour later. On the evening of [[13 August]] Gerlach's officers, from a plateau above their bivak, saw three rockets rise at the appointed time exactly where reckoning placed Lubeck — at [[Gleiêng]] — and answered them: contact was thus restored in an unknown, wild and hostile country. It was Lubeck's advance, the authors judged from the evidence of the abandoned *benting* before Selimoen, that spared the Lamtoba column the attack of an enemy who must otherwise have disputed the narrow path commanding his ravines.

On the morning of [[14 August]], from the edge of the berg-plateau, the officers of the mountain column could distinguish Lubeck's column below them in the valley — a fine black streak winding slowly over the yellow-green sawah, small white puffs of smoke rising from it: "zoo waar, dat was de colonne Lubeck, die al vurende voorwaarts trok." The column had met its most serious opposition at [[Lampisang]], where [[Toekoe di Tiroe]] with his band had awaited it. In the afternoon of [[14 August]] it occupied Selimoen, beating Gerlach's column by a day.

## The submission of Toekoe Moeda Daud and the return

On [[14 August]] [[1879]] [[Toekoe Moeda Daoed|Toekoe Moeda Daud]], the head of Selimoen and one of the pretenders to the Sultan's throne, offered his voluntary submission to Lubeck and promised to accompany him to [[Kota Radja]]; upon that promise no hostages were demanded. When Gerlach's column arrived on [[15 August]], it was greeted by the notes of the *Wilhelmus van Nassauwen* from Lubeck's band — the moment the authors made the emotional summit of their book. Through the offices of Controleur Kamp, Lubeck had given Toekoe Moeda Daud reason to hope that the second column's bivak would be placed wholly outside the kampong; Gerlach, now in general command, did not allow this. Lubeck's bivak lay some eight hundred paces from Gerlach's, behind the chalk hills on the bank of the Atjeh river; on the evening after his arrival his bivak received a few shots, upon which a section of infantry was posted on the commanding hill between the two camps.

Having promised in his report to the General to bring the chief with him to the [[Kraton]], Lubeck waited for Toekoe Moeda Daud until within a few hours of his own march, a time of great tension; thechief appeared at last, and Lubeck's column marched back to Indrapoeri in the morning of [[17 August]] [[1879]], accompanied by Toekoe Moeda Daud, [[Toekoe Tjoet Hadjet|Toekoe Ajer Alang]] and a following — received everywhere by the population in the most peaceful manner. Of his remaining provisions he had left more than half a day's store at the disposal of Overste Gerlach.
