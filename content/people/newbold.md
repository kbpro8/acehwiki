**Newbold** — fully **T. J. Newbold** — was a British colonial officer and author of *Political and Statistical Account of the British Settlements in the Straits of Malacca*, a two-volume work first published in the 1830s and standard reference for the early colonial administration of [[Penang]], [[Malacca]], [[Singapore]] and the other [[Straits Settlements]]. He is one of the comparative sources cited repeatedly by Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* whenever Achehnese institutions, vocabulary or material culture have close parallels among the Malays of the Straits.

In the chapters on territorial organisation and on mosque officials, Newbold is invoked for his description of the Malay term *mukim*. Snouck observes that the word has "the same modified meaning" in [[Kedah]] as in [[Acheh]] — that is to say, it denotes a territorial subdivision centred on a mosque rather than simply the body of resident worshippers contemplated by Islamic law. Newbold's further note, that in Kedah each mukim originally consisted of at least forty-four families, is treated by Snouck as a "clear indication of the original intent of this territorial subdivision" throughout the Malay world.

In the chapter on the personnel of the mosque, Newbold is again cited with respect to the office of the *bilal*, the muezzin who intones the call to prayer. Newbold had described the bilal's duties as "mainly sacrificial"; the translator's footnote to *The Achehnese* corrects this, observing that in fact the bilal only intones the call to prayer, while it is the *imam* who recites the *talkin* or funeral service over the grave, and that the bilal often washes and shrouds the dead "only as an eligible elder".

A third, more material citation occurs in the section on fishing appliances, where Snouck quotes Newbold's description of the conical basket used for catching fish in shallows and marshes: "Fish are often taken in shallows and marshes by means of a conical basket open at the top and bottom. The broad end is placed suddenly on the mud where they are supposed to lie; the hand introduced at the narrow upper part of the cone and the ensnared fish taken out." The Achehnese *geuncugón* trap is identified as identical with this Malay appliance, the same in form and use.
