**Joost Schouten** (also called *Justus Schouten* in some sources) was the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Compagnie]]'s envoy to [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] who, in [[15 February|February]] [[1641]], concluded with the newly enthroned [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|sultane of Atjeh]] — [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Tajul-alam]] — the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (15 February 1641)|first major Acehnese-Dutch treaty of her reign]]. In Dr. F. W. Stapel's *Geschiedkundige Atlas van Nederland* he is the diplomat whose embassy opened the era of structured Dutch commercial privilege on the [[Westkust van Sumatra|West Coast of Sumatra]].

## Context

The sultane's accession in February [[1641]] — coincident with the [[14 January|14 January]] [[1641]] fall of [[Malakka|Portuguese Malakka]] to the Compagnie — was the moment at which the long Acehnese-Portuguese alignment ended and a new Acehnese-Dutch alignment became possible. Schouten was the man Batavia dispatched to take the opening. Stapel records that "in 1641 a sultane came to power in Atjeh, daughter of [[Iskandar Muda of Atjeh|Iskander Moeda]]; she concluded, still in the first month of her reign — February — an agreement with the Compagnie's envoy Joost Schouten" (*In 1641 kwam in Atjeh een sultane aan het bewind, dochter van Iskander Moeda; zij sloot, nog in de eerste maand van haar regeering — Februari — een overeenkomst met den gezant der Compagnie, Joost Schouten*).

## The 1641 embassy and treaty

Schouten was dispatched to Aceh shortly after Tajul-alam's accession. He was able to win her favour and conclude a treaty that gave the VOC free access to the pepper ports. The [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (15 February 1641)|Schouten treaty]] had two main elements. The sultane "conceded to the Dutchmen the tolls in the pepper ports [[Tikoe]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]] and [[Indrapoera]], and promised to admit no other European traders there" (*zij de Nederlanders concessies deed op het gebied der tollen in de peperhavens Tikoe, Priaman, Padang en Indrapoera, en beloofde, daar geen andere Europeesche handelaars toe te laten*).

The careful restriction of the exclusion to *European* traders left the Atjehers' own rights on the Westkust intact — a pattern that would recur in every subsequent Acehnese-Dutch treaty: Atjehers-and-Hollanders-only. Schouten's treaty is the first statement of it.

## Earlier 1641 activity: jewel sale

Schouten had also sold jewels in Atjeh in [[1641]] to the value of ƒ28,000 with 61% profit — and even then had not managed to dispose of the entire parcel, "daar er reeds te veel diamanten in Atjeh waren" — because there were already too many diamonds in Atjeh. The Schouten sale was thus also in *Atjehsche mode*, and its partial success — and the saturation of the Atjeh diamond market — was the immediate background against which the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury mission]]'s failure must be understood.

## Significance

Schouten's embassy is the political foundation on which all later Dutch Westkust policy is built. The [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (November 1649)|Truytman treaties]] of November [[1649]] widened Schouten's four pepper ports to the whole Westkust; the [[Treaty of Atjeh-Perak (15 August 1650)|Perak treaty of 15 August 1650]] extended the same formula to the peninsula's tin; the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (5 October 1659)|1659 peace]] and the [[Painansch Contract]] of [[1663]] would progressively dismantle the Atjehsche sovereignty that Schouten had just confirmed. His negotiations are notable for their tact and for securing commercial advantage without military confrontation.

The 1641 embassy is also the first Dutch diplomatic encounter with a female Muslim sovereign. Stapel's editorial judgement on Schouten's opposite number is uniformly respectful — the sultane is described as conducting her own foreign policy, signing her own treaties, and (in [[1656]]) ordering the [[Arrest of Anthony van Voorst|arrest of Van Voorst]] as a sovereign act of reprisal. Schouten's success in February 1641 set the tone of that long diplomatic relationship.
