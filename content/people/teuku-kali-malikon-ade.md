The most important religious-administrative official in the capital of Acheh, the Chief Kali, whose office adjudicated inheritance, marriage, and other religious law matters. He was among those who persuaded Cheh Marahaban to settle at the capital after his return from Mecca. He was a leading figure in the early phases of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], appearing in the [[Hikayat prang Kompeuni|Hikayat prang Kompeuni]] as a chief speaker in the council held in the Dalam when Dutch ships first arrived demanding submission.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teuku Kali Malikon Ade

**Teuku Kali Malikon Ade** is the hereditary Achehnese title whose Arabic form **Qadlu Malikul Adil** signifies "Judge of the righteous King." The title is borne by the holder of what was originally the chief judicial office of the Sultanate of [[Acheh]] — the president of the royal court of justice — but which, in the course of two and a half centuries, had so completely deteriorated that by the late 19th century its holder was no judge at all, but an *uleebalang* of twelve *gampongs* on the right bank of the [[Acheh River]], a court dignitary, and a master of ceremonies at the Sultan's court. As [[Prof. Niemann]] noted, *Malikul Adil* is a common title for *qadhis* in Malay countries.

The original intention of the powerful port-king — whether [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]] or an earlier sovereign — who instituted the title was, according to [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], to have a president of the royal court of justice possessed of an adequate knowledge of the religious law. Some Achehnese chiefs assert that the wish of the creating prince was that his *kali* should apply the *adat* as well as the *hukom*, and on this account gave him, in contradistinction to the Kali Rabon Jale ("Judge of the Almighty Lord") of the three *sagis*, the title of "Judge of the righteous King." Whether the sultan wished at the same time to endow his own judge with a measure of supremacy over the other three chief *kalis* remains uncertain; in practice, any such scheme miscarried, since the great independence of the three *sagis* would have resisted it.

The office very quickly deteriorated. The causes were, first, the hereditary nature of the office, which did not ensure that the heir should be as learned as his predecessor; secondly, the residence of the holder in the immediate neighbourhood of the court, by which he gained an opportunity of having his office converted into a sort of *uleebalangship* — the end and aim of all Achehnese office-bearers; and lastly, the rapid decay of the central power, by which various offices created during the brief period of prosperity lost the reason for their further existence. Even in the later edicts, which always more or less idealise facts, the Kali Malikon Ade is represented as a distinguished courtier, a sort of master of court ceremonies, to whom a fixed portion of the harbour dues was assigned. He became what was called *uleebalang peuteueng*, "uleebalang of our lord" (the sultan), equal in rank to the almost independent provincial chiefs, with all the worldly pretension appertaining to that rank, but without territory. The official title of Teuku which he bore and still bears points unequivocally to the complete secularisation of his office.

Finally one of the holders of this title, making use of the special favour of the prince towards him and of the weakness of the then [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] (the hereditary head of the 24 or more *gampongs* on either side of the Acheh river, in the neighbourhood of the court and principal mosque), succeeded in wresting from that chief half of his territory — twelve *gampongs* on the right bank of the Acheh river. Thus the Teuku Kali attained the acme of the wishes of an Achehnese official: chief of a small but important territory, and at the same time, in his capacity as a court dignitary, not without influence in the choice of a successor to the throne. How little these two *uleebalangs* could be recognised as simply servants of the sultan is shown by the fact that they occasionally made war on one another; the sultan helped first one and then the other with money and munitions of war, and when a dispute arose as to the succession to the throne, the Panglima Meuseugit Raya and the Teuku Kali Malikon Ade usually espoused opposite sides.

In his capacity as master of court ceremonies, the Teuku Kali Malikon Ade was on terms of greater intimacy with the royal family than the rest of the *uleebalangs*. During the great feasts the *uleebalangs* in the neighbourhood of the *Dalam* together with some distinguished *ulamas* used to wait on the sultan and be presented with articles of dress; the Teuku Kali Malikon Ade alone used to come and present his felicitations on the actual feast day, when he, as well as the members of the Sultan's family and his household servants, received a share of the royal slaughter.

Snouck Hurgronje observes that [[T. H. der Kinderen|Mr. Der Kinderen]] quite missed the mark in finding anything extraordinary in the fact that the "hereditary supreme judge" of the kingdom could neither read nor write. Teuku Kali was *uleebalang* of 12 *gampongs*, and shared this ignorance with many of his equals in rank; those who were charged with administering justice according to religious law could all read and write, and most had carried their learning much further. Even in the rare cases where the Sultan of Acheh had need of the advice of those learned in the law, his real adviser was [[Cheh Marahaban]] and not Teuku Kali. In [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]]'s pamphlet to the Dutch Government, the Teuku Kali is named among the four — Teuku Kali, [[Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa|Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa]], the Panglima Meuseugit Raya and the [[Imeum of Lueng Bata]] — on whose counsel the Sultan was obliged to depend.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teuku Malikon Ade

**Teuku Malikon Ade** (Achehnese form of the Arabic *Qadi Maliku'l-Adil*, "Judge of the Righteous King") was the hereditary holder of one of the most venerable judicial titles in the [[Acheh|Achehnese Sultanate]]. Writers on Acheh had accustomed readers to the idea that, above the kalis of the uleebalangs and of the panglima sagi, there was enthroned in the capital a supreme hereditary judge who bore this title. Snouck Hurgronje argues in *The Achehnese* Vol. I that this notion rested largely on the exaggerated self-description of the then holder of the office, whom Mr. Der Kinderen had taken as his chief guide, and that the actual position was far more modest.

Even in the later royal edicts, which always more or less idealized the facts, the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]] appears not as a great jurist but as a distinguished courtier, a sort of master of court ceremonies to whom a fixed portion of the harbour dues was assigned. The title carried with it the rank of *uleebalang poteu*, "uleebalang of our lord" the sultan — equal in rank to the almost independent provincial chiefs, with all the worldly pretension appertaining to that rank, but without any territory of its own. Snouck uses the "wholly unlettered" Kali Malikon Ade of his own day as evidence against the romantic picture of an Acheh once governed by learned jurists, observing that the holder shared the prevailing ignorance of Mohammedan law common to most chiefs.

The ambitious efforts of the Teuku Kali Malikon Ade towards the acquisition of territory were eventually crowned with success. He wrested from the weak [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] half of the latter's domain — twelve gampongs on the right bank of the Acheh river adjoining the [[Dalam]]. The two offices thereafter stood as rival territorial powers; they occasionally made war on one another, and when a dispute arose as to the royal succession the Panglima Meuseugit Raya and the Kali Malikon Ade usually espoused opposite sides, with the sultan's own followers to be found in both camps.

At the solemn installation of a new sultan the Kali Malikon Ade performed his functions within the space surrounding the *branda seumah*, the coronation platform, with his blunderbuss hanging from his shoulder and a white cocoanut spathe wound in his head-cloth. The decay of the central power in [[Acheh]] thus converted an ancient judicial office into a sort of uleebalangship — the end and aim of all Achehnese office-bearers — furnishing a classic example of the degeneration of offices remarked by Snouck throughout the institutions of the sultanate.
