The name **Jamalul Alam** — in Achehnese **Jemnaloj** — was borne in Achehnese history by (at least) two distinct persons, both of whom are mentioned in Vol. I of *The Achehnese*: a Sultan of [[Acheh]] reigning in [[1689]], and an Arab rival claimant to the throne who contested the succession of [[Alaedin Juhan Shah]] in [[1756]]–[[1760]]. The two should not be confused.

## Jamalul Alam, Sultan of Acheh (1689)

The first **Jamalul Alam** is recorded in the Achehnese chronicles as ruling in [[1689]]. He is chiefly known to historians of Acheh through a [[Sarakata (royal edicts of Acheh)|sarakata]] — a royal edict having the force of law — bearing his name and dated [[1689]], which forms one of the small corpus of dated Achehnese sarakatas collected and described by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in Vol. I of *The Achehnese*.

The edict of Jamalul Alam belongs to the same class of documents as the [[Adat Meukuta Alam|sarakatas of Meukuta Alam]] ([[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Iskandar Muda]], [[1607]]–[[1636]]) and of [[Shamsul-alam]] (who reigned only one month in [[1726]]–[[1727]]). It is one of the principal native sources on which our knowledge of Achehnese political institutions rests. The Jamalul Alam sarakata was later revived — that is, confirmed or re-issued — by [[Alaedin Juhan Shah|Alaedin Juhan]], the second prince of the latest (Bugis-origin) dynasty, in [[1752]]. This act of revival reflects the persistent Achehnese practice of legitimising fresh regulations by attaching them, in form, to the authority of an earlier sovereign.

Snouck Hurgronje classes Jamalul Alam's edict with the other sarakatas he obtained copies of during his residence in Acheh, alongside the documents previously published by [[K. F. H. Van Langen|Van Langen]]. These documents, he notes, are "identical in spirit and intention" with Van Langen's set, but contain more detail regarding the ceremonial of court and festival, the collection and disposal of import taxes, and the customs-tariffs of the port.

The sarakatas as a whole represent the only sustained attempts at centralisation of authority, or at social, political, and religious reformation, that Acheh ever knew. Yet, as Snouck Hurgronje observes, hardly a single one of the innovations comprised in these documents "has passed from document into actuality"; the state of things they reveal as already in being simply "continued its existence." The reign of Jamalul Alam falls within the long period of dynastic struggle that followed the [[Female rule of Acheh (1641–1699)|female rule of Acheh]] (1641–1699), when perpetual succession disputes prevented the establishment of solid institutions capable of surviving the overthrow of individual rulers.

## Jamalul-alam, the Arab rival claimant (1756–1760)

A later and quite different **Jamalul-alam** was an Arab claimant to the throne of Acheh, who, in [[1756]]–[[1760]], contested the succession of [[Alaedin Juhan Shah]] (Alaedin Juhan, the second prince of the latest dynasty, the very prince who had revived the sarakata of his earlier namesake in [[1752]]). The conflict was waged on Alaedin Juhan Shah's behalf by his brother, the hero of the celebrated Achehnese heroic poem the ***Hikayat Potjut Muhamat***. Snouck Hurgronje describes this poem as depicting "with no small skill the conflict waged by that prince in behalf of his brother Alaedin Juhan Shah (1756–60) against the latter's Arab rival Jamalul-alam." In spite of "sundry embroideries which characterize" the poem, it was composed shortly after the war it describes and gives, on the whole, an accurate account of the events and of the condition of things at that period.

The conflict between [[Alaedin Juhan Shah]] and the Arab Jamalul-alam — known to Achehnese tradition as the [[Achehnese succession war of 1756-1760]] — is one of the principal episodes of the latest, Bugis-origin dynasty of Acheh. It is in this conflict that the *imeums* first emerge, in Achehnese heroic poetry, as independent military and political actors; the [[Imeum of Chadé]] is recorded as having been the most powerful supporter of the finally defeated candidate in "the last conflict for royal honours before the Achehnese war." The identity of the defeated candidate is not directly stated by Snouck Hurgronje in Vol. I, but the reference belongs to the same recurring pattern of disputed successions, of which the war between Alaedin Juhan Shah and the Arab Jamalul-alam is the most famous eighteenth-century example.
