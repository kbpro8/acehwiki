**Tengkoe Mat Amin di Tiro** — *Tgk. Mat Amin* — was the eldest son and successor of [[Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro]], and the foremost of the four sons of the great Tiro-oelama. Of all the sons he was the principal; he was the one to whom the dying father spoke the words of grave and urgent admonition in [[January]] [[1891]], for this son was of loose and wanton habits, given to women in a way that brought shame on an oelama, proud and obstinate, and free with the funds collected for the [[prang sabil|holy war]]. Yet none of this prevented the father from naming him as his successor; and in [[1892]] the pretendent-sultan confirmed him in that position.

## The defence of Aneukgalong, March 1896

After the defection of [[Teukoe Oemar]] in [[1896]] the post of [[Aneukgalong]] — then called *Anakgalong* — was abandoned by the Dutch and immediately occupied and fortified by the enemy. Tgk. Mat Amin sat there with his brother [[Tengkoe di Boekit|Tgk. di Boekit]] and a couple of hundred men, fighting for everything they were worth. The recapture of the benteng became one of the most *éclatante* feats of the young [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|korps Maréchaussée]]; under [[Graafland|Graafland]] the fort was, in the very early morning, virtually completely surrounded so that the garrison stood, figuratively, with its back to the wall. The Acehnese fought like lions: some flung themselves into the burning barracks rather than surrender. It was bitter hand-to-hand fighting; quarter was neither asked nor given.

Captain [[G.C.E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]], as chief of staff, was present at this feat of arms and gave a sober and matter-of-fact account of it in a letter of [[5 July]] [[1896]] to his wife in Java:

> *"Now an account of my last expedition, the great success of Anakgalong. Precisely 12 o'clock midnight of the 28th we marched off with two battalions and the maréchaussée to Lam baroe, under the orders of overste Van Heutsz, and me as chief-of-staff. After a cup of coffee at overste Bisschoff's at Lambaroe we marched off at half-past two for Anakgalong. It was moonlight, and we had word that the garrison of the benteng was 200 men. On the way two bridges were broken, so that we had trouble with them too. Well, by half past five we were in front of it, and at a quarter to five the first shot was fired and the storming began with man-to-man fighting inside the benteng, and by five o'clock it was all over… The Atjehers left 110 dead in our hands and two living, lightly wounded little boys of 5 and 6 years old."*

Among the dead was Tgk. Mat Amin di Tiro. His body was brought by his own people to kampong Moereuë, not far from [[Indrapoeri]], and buried there beside his father, in the same *kandang*. The ordinary washings and ceremonies were not needed for one who had fallen in the [[prang sabil|holy war]]: such a one is at once received into the felicity of paradise.

## Significance

Zentgraff marks out Mat Amin, despite his wanton life, as a man who "knew how to die as a hero." To fall in battle is, considered in itself, no merit but the war-risk one undergoes; with part of the Tiro-oelama's it was otherwise. In the free choice between life and death they chose the latter, and by Atjehsche reckoning thereby made good more evil than any man in his life can do wrong. Mat Amin's death at Aneukgalong opened the long series of deaths that would, by [[1910]], extinguish the adult line of the Tiro-oelama's.
