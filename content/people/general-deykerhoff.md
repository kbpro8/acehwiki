**General Deykerhoff** was a Dutch military officer who served as Governor of [[Acheh|Acheh]] during the closing phase of the so-called **"Uma-period"** — the three years of the [[Concentration Scheme|concentration-scheme]] policy that gave the adventurer [[Teuku Uma|Teuku Uma]] his fatal influence over the affairs of the colony. Snouck Hurgronje mentions him in the Introduction to *The Achehnese* as the "blindly-trusting General Deykerhoff" on whom [[Teuku Uma|Teuku Uma]] finally turned, in [[March 1896|March 1896]], when the latter judged that the time had come to reveal the long-concealed hostility that lay beneath his professed friendship for the Dutch.

The episode belongs to the history of the Dutch **concentration scheme** of [[1884]]–[[1896]], under which the colonial government sought to reduce its military expenditure in Great Acheh by withdrawing into a fortified "line" around the old seat of the sultanate, and leaving the rest of the country to be pacified in some imagined future by the operation of time. The scheme was disastrous. Snouck describes how "a Governor absolutely unacquainted with native affairs" gave his entire confidence to the adventurer Teuku Uma, "immediately after Uma's submission, and, in defiance of all warnings, persisted in strengthening Uma by generous subsidies of Dutch weapons and Dutch money". The Governor in question was Deykerhoff, who treated Uma as a "trusty councillor" and entrusted him with the command of friendly Achehnese forces.

Armed and subsidised by the colonial government, Uma used his Dutch patron's confidence to deal out, "as his own, plentiful subsidies to his friends in the War-party and tried by all means in his power to acquire the maximum of influence both over the Dutch and their opponents". The only apparent gain which Uma brought to his protector was an extension of the Dutch "line" beyond its original limits — a gain that was, in Snouck's words, "due to and continued to depend on the slender thread of his good-will". When at last, in March 1896, Uma judged that he had sufficient money and arms "to play a leading part among the War-party, and the claims which his friend Deykerhoff pressed upon him began to be burdensome", he threw off the mask and openly defected. Snouck treats the "defection" as a misnomer: "since the real purport of his actions had long been as obvious as the day, we cannot speak except in a highly specialized sense of Uma's 'defection'".

The episode had at least one salutary consequence. "The Uma episode, however melancholy in itself, indirectly exercised a healthy influence upon the management of Achehnese affairs. Its termination opened all eyes to the need of vigorous action if one did not wish to abandon altogether the subjugation of Acheh". In [[1896]] the war was resumed, and from [[1898]] under [[Governor Van Heutsz|Governor Van Heutsz]] the subjugation was systematically prosecuted. Deykerhoff's governorship thus marks the nadir of Dutch policy in Acheh, and the immediate cause of the decisive turn that followed.
