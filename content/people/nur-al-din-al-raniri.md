**Nur al-Din al-Raniri** — Malay/Arabic *Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī*, also written *Noer Oed-Din AR-RĀNĪRI* in the older Dutch orthography — was a Gujarati-born Muslim scholar of Hadrami *sayyid* descent who arrived in [[Aceh]] in [[1637]] and served as the principal religious authority at the court of [[Sultan Iskandar II|Sultan Iskandar Thani]] in the late [[1630s]] and early [[1640s]], composing at the sultan's command the *[[Sirat al-Mustaqim|Sirāṭ al-mustaqīm]]* (begun [[1634]], completed [[1644]]) and the *[[Bustan al-Salatin|Bustān as-Salāṭīn]]* ([[1638]]) — the principal Malay-language works of *fiqh* and of the "mirror for princes" genre produced at the Acehnese court. He is the most celebrated of the Indian-Arab scholars who, during Aceh's golden age, made the capital a centre of Islamic learning for the entire [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]], and — as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s "pandit Raniri" — supplied one of the few firm chronological anchor-points in the early history of the Acehnese Sultanate by testifying to the year [[1636]] as the date of the death of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Meukuta Alam]] (Iskandar Muda). He is equally the agent of the orthodox reaction that closed Aceh's golden age of mystical poetry, and the author whose polemical works preserve — even in their refutation — the names, doctrines, and very words of the writers whose books were burned at his instigation.

## Origin and Identification

The full form of Raniri's name, as given by [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] on the authority of the colophon of the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]], was **Nūruddīn Muḥammad b. ʿAlī b. Ḥasanji b. Muḥammad Ḥamīd ar-Rānīrī al-Kurashī ash-Shāfiʿī** — a *nisba* indicating Arab (Qurayshī) descent and Shāfiʿite legal school, attached to the town of [[Ranir]] near [[Surat]] in [[Gujarat]] (Gudjarāt). Voorhoeve notes that the identification of Ranir with the town near Surat — once contested — has been placed beyond doubt by the researches of [[G. W. J. Drewes|Drewes]]: "er kan thans geen twijfel meer aan bestaan, dat zijn vaderstad Rānīr bij Sūrat in Gudjarāt was." His family was part of the network of Indian Arabs of mixed blood who maintained contact with the motherland by correspondence and study journeys, and who in turn maintained relations with Indonesia — that is, in this period, especially with [[Aceh]].

Neither Raniri's year of birth nor that of his death has been preserved. Voorhoeve fixes, however, the firm chronological pegs of his career:

- He arrived in [[Aceh]] in [[1637]], the first year of the reign of [[Iskandar Thani]].
- In [[1641]] he is found, together with a number of high functionaries of the Acehnese court, taking part in the ceremony of erecting the tombstone for Sultan [[Iskandar Thani|Iskandar II]].
- The dates of his Malay works, so far as they are reliably attested, fall between [[1634]] and [[1644]].
- In [[1644]] he returned to [[Ranir]].
- He must have died before [[1666]].

This chronology was established by [[Hoesein Djajadiningrat|Djajadiningrat]] in *TBG* 69 (pp. 109–111), a study which Voorhoeve candidly confesses he had overlooked when composing his earlier notes in *BKI* 107 (p. 353 ff.). Among the corrections imposed by Djajadiningrat's research is the redating of the [[Sirat al-Mustaqim|*Ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*]]: although begun in [[1634]] (that is, before Raniri's arrival in Aceh), the work was — as Djajadiningrat showed — only completed in [[1644]]. The knowledge of Malay letters displayed in it need not, therefore, have been acquired before [[1637]].

## The Gujarati Milieu and Family Connections

The coastal towns of Gujarat in this period had a very international population. Ships regularly sailed thence to the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malaka]], and among the mercenary troops of the sixteenth century were "Javanese" — that is, in this context, Indonesians. The Malay language was certainly not unfamiliar in the harbour towns; and the family of Raniri had, even before his own time, maintained relations with [[Aceh]]. Between [[1580]] and [[1583]] his uncle [[Muhammad Djailani Hamid]] arrived in Aceh. He wished to teach logic, rhetoric, dogmatics, and the doctrine of duties (*plichtenleer*, "handbook of religious duties"), but found little interest. There was a great influx only for teachers of mysticism. So the scholar sailed on to [[Mecca]]. Some years later he returned, provided with the *venia docendi* as a teacher of mystical theology, and then had great success. Raniri learned from his uncle's experiences: when he himself left for Aceh he was thoroughly prepared. He knew the *[[Bahasa Djawi]]*, the Malay that was the language of the court in Aceh, and had already written two works in it, while a third was in progress.

His Gujarati-Indian origin is central to his literary output: the *[[Bustan al-Salatin|Bustān as-Salāṭīn]]*, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s reading, is "door een Voor-Indier geschreven" (written by an Indian) and "ontleent zijn stof van talrijke anecdoten aan dezelfde en soortgelijke Moslimse verzamelwerken in de Perzische taal uit Voor-Indië" (derives his material, of numerous anecdotes, from the same and similar Muslim compilations in the Persian language from India). The same Persian-language Muslim compilations also supplied the earlier *[[Tādj oes-Salatina|Tādj us-Salāṭīn]]* of [[1607]] — a fact that, in Hooykaas's reading, points to the common Persian-language source tradition on which both Acehnese "mirrors for princes" drew.

## Sufi Initiation: the Rifāʿiyya and the Bā Shaybān *Silsila*

Raniri had also penetrated into mysticism. He was initiated into the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya order]], founded by the saint [[Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī|Ahmad Rifa'i]], whose tomb at [[Mocha]] is reverenced. His shaikh, of Hadrami descent but born in India, was **Sayyid Abū Ḥafṣ ʿUmar b. ʿAbdallāh Bā Shaybān** ([[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban]]). Bā Shaybān had studied in Arabia but later settled again in India, where he was received into the order by [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]] at [[Surat]]. He lived chiefly at [[Bijapur|Bījāpūr]], where he enjoyed the protection of the ruler Muḥammad ʿĀdilshāh; he died in [[1656]] at [[Bilgram|Bilgrām]]. The celebrated **Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar** ([[Yusuf Makasar]]), who in [[1644]] left Celebes for [[Mecca]], studied with the same Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān; he also numbers Raniri among his teachers.

The teacher of Bā Shaybān — and Raniri's spiritual grandfather — was [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]], born in [[1561]] at [[Tarim]] in South Arabia. He came to Gujarat at the age of nineteen, where he succeeded his famous grandfather as religious teacher, first in the capital [[Ahmadabad|Ahmadābād]] and later at [[Surat]]. He died at Surat in [[1620]]. Better known as an author is his uncle, **ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Aydārūs** ([[Abd al-Qadir al-Aidaros]]), who died in [[1628]] at Ahmadabad. Many of his writings are preserved, including correspondence exchanged with a scholar at [[Mocha]]. ʿAbd al-Qādir's father — Muḥammad's grandfather — had come from South Arabia in [[1551]]; ʿAbd al-Qādir's mother was an Indian. This, Voorhoeve concludes, was the milieu from which Raniri sprang: "Indische Arabieren van gemengden bloede, die door correspondentie en studiereizen het contact met het moederland onderhielden, en die anderzijds in verkeer stonden met Indonesië, dat wil in deze tijd in het bijzonder zeggen: Atjèh."

## The Religious Climate in Aceh and Raniri's Polemical Mission

In Aceh, Raniri as an orthodox mystic had during the reign of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] ([[1607]]–[[1636]]) nothing to expect. The "heterodox mystic" [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] dominated the spiritual life of the court, and even after his death in [[1630]] his disciples remained in honour with the sultan. But after Iskandar Muda died in [[1636]] the spiritual climate in Aceh changed. Shortly after the accession of [[Iskandar Thani|Iskandar II]], Raniri arrived in the country; in the presence of the sultan he refuted the adherents of the extreme [[Wujudiyyah|Wujūdiyyah mysticism]] in a public debate. From that time forth he enjoyed royal favour, both from Iskandar II and from his successor [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Ṣalīyatu'd-Dīn]] (Taj al-Ālam). This public disputation — the [[Raniri's public debate against the Wujudiyyah in Acheh|confrontation at the Acehnese court]] — is the dramatic hinge of Raniri's Acehnese career.

The new Sultan is said to have put the disciples of the heretical mystic to death and to have ordered his books burned — an episode of religious persecution in the generation after [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]]'s reign in which Raniri's puritanical influence is commonly reckoned to have played a decisive part. Snouck Hurgronje's reference to Raniri occurs in a passage distinguishing the strangers who wrote works of theology and law under the wealthy sultans in [[Banda Acheh]] from the native-born ulamas of later times. The pandit belongs emphatically to the former category: an immigrant man of letters patronised at the Acehnese court, whose influence "outside Acheh was at least as noticeable as within it."

## Compositions at the Acehnese Court

At the command of Sultan Iskandar II — and in the same year, [[1638]] — Raniri composed three works that together constitute the literary apparatus of his orthodox reform in Aceh:

- The *[[Sirat al-Mustaqim|Sirāṭ al-mustaqīm]]* (March [[1638]]) — the Malay handbook of religious duties (*fiqh*) of the Shāfiʿite school, the composition of which was begun in [[1634]] and completed only in [[1644]]. It is repeatedly attested in [[Over Maleise Literatuur (1947)|Hooykaas's survey]] of Malay literature as a key vehicle for the diffusion of the *fiqh* of the Shāfiʿite school into the Malay-Indonesian world.
- The *[[Bustan al-Salatin|Bustān as-Salāṭīn]]* ([[1638]]) — the Malay "mirror for princes", of which the historical chapter (II.m) on the sultans of Aceh and the religious scholars of the 16th and 17th centuries is, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s judgement, "wel eens de voorkeur blijken te verdienen boven de voorstelling van de SM" (sometimes proves to deserve the preference over the representation of the *Sadjarah Malajoe*).
- The *Bad' khalq as-samawat wal-arwah* ([[1638]]) — a Malay work on the creation of the heavens and the spirits.

The composition of these three works in the second year of Iskandar Thani's reign — at the very moment of Raniri's public disputation against the *Wujudiyyah* mystics — marks the beginning of Raniri's project of orthodox reform: to provide the Acehnese faithful with the literary apparatus of a Shāfiʿite sultanate.

## Comparison with the Earlier Tādj

[[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]] contrasts Raniri's *[[Bustan al-Salatin|Bustān]]* with the earlier *[[Tādj oes-Salatina|Tādj us-Salāṭīn]]* of [[1607]]: "terwijl BOCHARI's Tādj meer eenvoudig stichtelijk gehouden is, is daarentegen RANĪRI's Boestān meer het werk van een geleerde, die zich niet ontziet zijn lezers te vergasten op een hoofdstuk dat belangrijk moge zijn voor een theoloog (I) of een geschiedvorser (II), maar het lezend publiek wel eens zwaar gevallen zal zijn" (while Bochari's *Tādj* is more simply edifying, Raniri's *Bustān* is more the work of a scholar, who does not refrain from treating his readers to a chapter that may be important for a theologian (I) or a historical researcher (II), but may have been heavy going for the reading public).

## Raniri's Malay Writings before his Arrival in Aceh

An unresolved question is where Raniri composed the two Malay works which he had completed before his arrival in Aceh in [[1637]]: a work on the articles of faith (the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]]) and a collection of sacred traditions in Arabic with Malay translation. Voorhoeve had once supposed, on the strength of a passage in the *Sajarah Banten*, that Raniri sojourned in Mecca before his departure for Aceh, and that he might be the scholar referred to as "Ibn ʿAlī" or "Ibn ʿAlam" who refused to return with the Banten envoys to their country. On further reflection Voorhoeve withdrew this hypothesis: it seems much more probable that the "Ibn ʿAlam" of the *Sajarah Banten* is in fact **Ibn ʿAlān** — that is, Muḥammad ʿAlī Ibn ʿAlān as-Siddīqī (d. 1057 H / [[1648]]), of whom we know for certain that he was repeatedly consulted from Banten: by Sultan Abū 'l-Mafāḥir ʿAbd al-Qādir on the *Naṣīḥat al-mulūk* of al-Ghazālī (on which Ibn ʿAlān had written a commentary), and by Abū 'l-Maʿālī Aḥmad on the *Tuḥfa al-mursala* of Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh. Among the Arabic sources used by Raniri, Voorhoeve found not only famous and widely diffused books but works of Indian authors and literature current in India, and not a single writing by older contemporaries in Mecca. To learn Malay, Raniri had no need of distant travel: there were Malays enough in Gujarat, and his uncle will have spoken that language too. Whether he wrote his first Malay books there nonetheless remains an open question.

## Works Composed in Aceh — the Anti-Wujūdiyyah Polemics

Of the works Raniri composed in Aceh, most are directed against the [[Wujudiyyah]]. This side of Raniri's activity has been treated at length by [[C.A.O. van Nieuwenhuijze|C. A. O. van Nieuwenhuijze]] in his Leiden dissertation *Sjamsu'l-Dīn van Pasai* ([[1945]]) and in a special study in *BKI* 104. In those studies two short texts and a portion of Raniri's "book of sects" — the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]] — were already edited. Voorhoeve's [[1955]] edition gives the complete *Tibjān* in facsimile from the Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 3291, together with the shorter [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq*]] — itself directed entirely against the Wujūdiyyah — reproduced from the manuscript in the Maxwell collection of the [[Royal Asiatic Society]] in London. The *Tibjān* enumerates the pre-Islamic religions and the sects within Islam, with the aim of warning the faithful against all these aberrations and in particular against the doctrine of the heretical Wujūdiyyah.

In addition to the works already named, Raniri's writings include the [[Hall al-zill|*Ḥall aẓ-ẓill*]], the [[Djawahir al-'ulum|*Jawāhir al-ʿulūm*]] (which survives in a London manuscript and which cites al-Jīlī's [[al-Insan al-Kamil|*al-Insān al-kāmil*]] repeatedly, as does the *Ḥall aẓ-ẓill*), the lost [[Lata'if al-asrar|*Laṭāʾif al-asrār*]] (already cited in the *Tibjān* as a source for the refutation of the Wujūdiyyah), and the *Badʾ khalq as-samāwāt wal-arwāḥ*.

## Sources of the *Tibjān*

Voorhoeve's examination of Raniri's sources is itself a substantial contribution to Malay religious philology. The principal source of the *Tibjān* is the *at-Tamhīd fī bayān at-tawḥīd* of [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|Abū Shakūr as-Sālimī]], a Persian Shāfiʿite of the second half of the fifth century H. More than a quarter of the text of the *Tibjān* is translated literally from the *Tamhid*, especially its last chapter on orthodoxy and the rejection of innovation (*bidʿa*). The Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 5664 — a Banten copy sent to Leiden by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in [[1906]] — preserves a text practically identical to Raniri's own exemplar, including shared errors such as "Kubtiyah" (Copts) for "al-Muʿattila" and a lacuna that caused Raniri to announce four groups of the Tanāsuḥiyyah while treating only three.

A second source was the [[al-Insan al-Kamil|*al-Insān al-kāmil*]] of [[Abd al-Karim al-Jili|ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī]], from which Raniri drew his account of the Barāhima. The schematic overview of the seventy-two sects — six groups of twelve — comes from the widely diffused popular tract known as the *Maʿrifat al-madhāhib*, sometimes attributed to [[Abu Hanifa|Abū Ḥanīfa]], of which an Arabic exemplar in Leiden (Cod. Or. 1255) shows a strikingly close correspondence with the *Tibjān*, even in peculiar variants. The section on the quasi-Sufi sects is drawn from the small tract *Kitāb fī bayān madhāhib al-mutaṣawwifa* of [[Najm ad-Din an-Nasafi|Najm ad-Dīn an-Nasafī]].

## Raniri's Polemical Method in the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*

The *[[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq]]* — "the argument of the pious one for the refutation of the heretic" — sets out, in Voorhoeve's analysis, four positions on the relation of God's being to the world: (1) that of the dialectical theologians (*mutakallimūn*), who distinguish two beings, the necessary being of God and the contingent being of the created world; (2) that of the Sufis, who accept the theologians' doctrine but employ a different terminology in which "being" (*wujūd*) signifies only the divine essence, while the world is mere manifestation (*maẓhar*) and shadow (*ẓill*); (3) that of the philosophers (*falāsifa*), who hold God's being and the world to be co-eternal; and (4) that of the heretical Wujūdiyyah, who teach that being is one and identical with God, who is "enclosed in all that is created" — so that "the world = God, God = the world." Raniri's method is to distinguish sharply the orthodox Sufi position from the heretical Wujūdiyyah by arguing that the latter, in saying "God and world are one," contradicts both revelation and reason; he cites in support authorities including [[Ibn Arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] (in the *Futūḥāt Makkīya*), [[Ahmad al-Mahaimi|ʿAlī b. Aḥmad al-Mahāʾimī]] (in the *Ithbād an-naṣīḥa* and *Irāʾat ad-daqāʾiq*), Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh (in the *Tuḥfa al-mursala*), and the lost *Laṭāʾif al-iʿlām*. He argues, finally, that ecstatic utterances (*shathīyāt* — [[Shathihiyat|shathihiyat]]) such as *anā al-ḥaqq* ("I am the Truth") of [[al-Hallaj]] are to be regarded as latah-like trance states, not blasphemous self-deification, when uttered by entranced mystics; but that the same utterances, when pronounced by one in his senses, deserve death and eternal punishment.

## The Diffusion of the *Sirat al-mustaqim*

[[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]] records one of the key episodes in the early diffusion of Raniri's *[[Sirat al-Mustaqim|Sirāṭ al-mustaqīm]]*: when the king of Kedah converted to Islam under the influence of *Sjaich 'Abdoellāh Jamani* and was given the throne-name Sultan Moezaffar Sjah, "Toen men dit in Atjéh vernam, liet de Moslim geworden vorst aldaar Sjaich Noeroddin diens boek Sirāṭ al-moestakīm naar Kēdah brengen ter onderrichting in de Wet" (When this was heard in Aceh, the Muslim ruler there had Sjaich Noeroddin's book *Sirāṭ al-mustaqīm* brought to Kedah for instruction in the Law). The episode — which casts Raniri as the *Sjaich Noeroddin* of the Kedah chronicle — illustrates the role of Raniri's Acehnese compositions as the principal vehicle by which the *fiqh* of the Shāfiʿite school was transmitted from Aceh to the Malay Peninsula in the early seventeenth century.

## The Testimony to the Death of Iskandar Muda

Raniri's treatise — printed at [[Mecca]] in 1281 Hegira on the margin of another edition — supplies one of the few firm chronological anchor-points in the early history of the Acehnese Sultanate. He states that he came to Aceh in [[1637]] while [[Iskandar Thani]] was king. Since Iskandar Thani succeeded Meukuta Alam, this testimony fixes the death of the great Sultan at [[1636]], a date that "is confirmed as the correct date" against the uncertainties of native chronicle and tradition. Raniri's arrival thus falls in the very first year of the new reign, and his presence in Aceh places him at the centre of the learned and religious life of the capital under the wealthy sultans of the seventeenth century.

## Significance

Raniri occupies a singular position in the religious history of Aceh: he is at once the agent of the orthodox reaction that closed Aceh's "golden age" of mystical poetry, and the author whose works preserve, even in their polemic, the names, doctrines, and very words of the writers whose books were burned at his instigation. Without the *Tibjān* and the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq* our knowledge of [[Hamzah Pansuri]]'s *[[Asrar al-'arifin|Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] and of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]'s *[[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn*]] would be far scantier than it is. The Voorhoeve edition of [[1955]] gave scholars, for the first time, a complete facsimile of the *Tibjān* and of the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq* — placing Raniri's Malay polemics, and the lost literature they alone preserve, at the centre of any future history of Acehnese religious thought.

His three compositions of [[1638]] — the *[[Sirat al-Mustaqim|Sirāṭ al-mustaqīm]]*, the *[[Bustan al-Salatin|Bustān as-Salāṭīn]]*, and the *Bad' khalq as-samawat wal-arwah* — together constitute the literary apparatus of his orthodox reform of the Acehnese court, and the *Bustān*'s historical chapter on the sultans of Aceh is, in Hooykaas's judgement, occasionally more reliable than the *Sadjarah Malajoe*. His diffusion of the *Sirāṭ* to Kedah is the principal evidence for the role of the Acehnese court as the centre of Shāfiʿite *fiqh* diffusion to the Malay Peninsula in the early seventeenth century.
