**Habib Teupin Wan** was an Acehnese religious leader — *de afstammeling van de profeet* (a *sayyid*, descendant of the Prophet Muhammad) — venerated as a saint, who in [[September 1911]] was killed in the closing phase of the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]] and whose death left only the fifteen-year-old *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* of the whole *Tiriō bende*. His son was killed in the assault on the schuilplaats in [[May 1911]] — one of the "enkele belangrijke onderaanvoerders" who fell — and his own death two months before Tjhi Ma'at's is the book's marker of the disintegration of the band's religious leadership.

## The sayyid as saint

"De afstammeling van de profeet, Habib Teupin Wan, die als een heilige werd vereerd." The phrase *de afstammeling van de profeet* marks him as a *sayyid* — a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatimah and his cousin/son-in-law Ali. In Acehnese society such descent carried automatic religious prestige; the book's phrase "als een heilige werd vereerd" indicates that the prestige had ripened, in Teupin Wan's case, into active veneration. He was not merely of the Prophet's line; he was held to be a saint.

His son, killed in the assault on the schuilplaats in [[May 1911]], was one of the "enkele belangrijke onderaanvoerders" who fell in that overval — the small set of sub-leaders whose death left the bende without its middle ranks.

## The death of September 1911

"In September 1911 sneuvelde de grote en heilige Habib Teupin Wan; slechts de jonge Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at was er over van heel de Tiri bende." With the sayyid's death, only the boy was left. The book's placement of Teupin Wan's death between the May assault and the December killing of Tjhi Ma'at is precise: by September the bende had lost its patriarch (*[[Tgk. di Boekét]]* dead of wounds), its operational leaders (the May assault), its venerated saint (Teupin Wan), and was reduced to one fifteen-year-old boy.

## Significance

Teupin Wan's significance in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* is as the religious authority of the Tiriō bende — the saintly figure whose presence gave the band its charisma, and whose death stripped that charisma away. The book's quiet argument is that the colonne Schmidt was not just killing fighters; it was killing, one by one, the religious leaders in whose name the resistance was carried on. By the time the boy *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* was alone, the doctrinal structure of the resistance — the patriarch *[[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri]]*, the sons, the saintly sayyid — had been destroyed, leaving only the heir whose death would make the doctrine immortal.
