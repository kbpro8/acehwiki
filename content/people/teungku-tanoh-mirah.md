A teacher and kali of the IV Mukims of the VII (sagi of the XXVI), son of Teungku Tanoh Abie, who like his father acquired his learning at Lam Nyong under Teungku di Lam Nyong.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teungku Tanoh Mirah

**Teungku Tanoh Mirah** was the learned *kali* of the IV Mukims of the VII of the [[XXVI Mukims]] — that is, of one of the sub-districts of the third *sagi* of [[Great Acheh]]. He is named by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as a typical example of those *ulamas* who, in spite of the indignant remonstrances of their more ambitious colleagues, held themselves aloof from the bickering of rival religious leaders and behaved as though no jihad existed.

The occasion of the mention is Snouck Hurgronje's summary of the pamphlets of [[Teungku Kutakarang]], the active but peculiar *ulama* who, after the death of [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]], sought to supplant the dead man's authority. In his pamphlets Teungku Kutakarang complained that some *ulamas* "sit on their prayer-carpets or lose their senses over ascetic exercises" — an allusion to the Teungku's rival [[Habib Samalanga]] — while others "devote themselves to the repair of mosques far away from the scene of the war, and hold great *kanduris* or religious feasts, squandering the money subscribed for the war on purposes for which it was never intended" — an allusion to Teungku Tiro. From this spirit of discord among the *ulamas*, Teungku Tanoh Mirah is distinguished as one who held himself entirely apart.

Teungku Tanoh Mirah's behaviour, as Snouck Hurgronje describes it, was that of the learned *kali* who, in the midst of a holy war, persisted in the life of study and the discharge of his ordinary judicial duties, refusing to be drawn into the rivalries of the war-leaders. The fact that his name is recorded at all, in a passage devoted to the contrasts among the *ulamas*, shows the respect in which his attitude was held: while [[Cheh Marahaban]] "would have been quite willing to change sides if he could only have found conclusive arguments strong enough to counterbalance the contumely attending such a step," and while Habib Samalanga, Teungku Tiro, and Teungku Kutakarang contended for the leadership of the jihad, Teungku Tanoh Mirah represented the learned and retiring type of *kali* — the scholar who, in a country given over to war, kept himself apart for study and the ordinary business of justice.

His example is the more noteworthy because the *kali* of the [[IV Mukims]] of the VII of the [[XXVI Mukims]] was, in ordinary circumstances, one of the lesser learned offices of Great Acheh. The reputation of Teungku Tanoh Mirah was due not to his office but to his personal learning and to the consistency of his abstention from the politics of the jihad.
