**F. N. Nieuwenhuizen** (full name: Frederik Nieuwenhuizen) was Vice-President of the [[Raad van Nederlandsch-Indië|Council of the Netherlands-Indies]] who served as the first Government Commissioner (*Gouvernements-Commissaris*) for the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|First Aceh Expedition]] of [[1873]]. Appointed by [[Governor-General of the Netherlands-India|Governor-General]] [[J. Loudon|Loudon]], Nieuwenhuizen was charged with the political direction of the expedition — the task of securing from [[Sultan Aladin Mahmoed Shah|Sultan Mahmud Shah]] a treaty recognizing Dutch sovereignty over [[Groot-Atjeh|Atjeh]], failing which the military forces would compel compliance. His handling of the negotiations in [[March 1873]] was sharply criticized by [[Jan van Swieten|Lieutenant-General Van Swieten]] as a principal cause of the expedition's failure and the subsequent war.

## The First Expedition

By the secret decision of [[4 March]] [[1873]] No. 1, [[J. Loudon|Governor-General Loudon]] ordered that a military expeditionary force be sent to Atjeh "with the objective of compelling the rulers of the realm of Atjeh, in the event of the peaceful efforts to be made beforehand by the Government Commissioner proving unsuccessful, to satisfy the demands to be made upon them by that Commissioner, if need be by force of arms."

Nieuwenhuizen departed [[Batavia]] on [[7 March]] [[1873]] aboard {{HMS Citadel van Antwerpen}}, commanded by Captain-Lieutenant [[J. W. Binkes]], accompanied by the government steamer {{HMS Siak}} under [[J. H. Meijer]]. At [[Riouw]], the gunboat {{HMS Coehoorn}} under [[J. J. Engelvaart]] joined the squadron. Arriving at [[Pinang]] on [[16 March]], Nieuwenhuizen found {{HMS Marnix}}, commanded by [[A. Rietveld]], already awaiting orders. At [[Pinang]], [[Th. E. R. Kraaijenhoff|Controleur Kraaijenhoff]] placed himself at the Commissioner's disposal, along with the interpreter team including [[Sidi Tahil]], [[Datoe Setia Aboe Hassan]], [[Toekoe Mohamad Arifin]], and others.

## The Pinang Intelligence

At [[Pinang]], Nieuwenhuizen received reports that deeply influenced his subsequent conduct. From various quarters he learned that in Atjeh, a passionate agitation reigned against the Dutch, as a consequence of the military preparations that had become known. The Acehnese, it was reported, longed for the Dutch arrival and the moment to measure themselves against them, and felt confident of victory based on the substantial forces gathered there.

[[Toekoe Pajah]] and the [[Radja of Simpang-Olim]], along with other Acehnese notables, were at [[Pinang]] at the same time as the Commissioner, ostensibly to observe Dutch movements. They were "bitter beyond measure" against the Dutch and had rejected a sideways invitation to meet with the Commissioner, declaring themselves resolved to fight to the last man and then to test what resistance the women and children could offer.

Furthermore, it was reported that since August [[1872]], more than 15,000 rifles and 5,000 barrels of gunpowder had been imported into [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The Dutch vice-consul at [[Pinang]] had communicated this officially.

These reports, Nieuwenhuizen believed, warranted an immediate bombardment upon the declaration of war to deny the Acehnese time to prepare. Captain [[J. W. Binkes|Binkes]] shared this view, writing that "probably several prominent houses and perhaps the Kraton itself lay within range of the ships' rifled guns."

## The Failed Negotiations

Nieuwenhuizen's conduct of the negotiations — from the dispatch of [[Sidi Tahil]] with the first letter on [[22 March]] [[1873]], through the exchange of increasingly frustrated correspondence with the Sultan, to the formal declaration of war on [[26 March]] — was later subjected withering criticism by [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]]. The central charges were:

1. **Wrong intermediary**: [[Sidi Tahil]], a merchant from [[Priaman]], was not a suitable person to represent the Netherlands-India Government before the Sultan of an independent realm. He lacked diplomatic standing and, by all accounts including that of [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]], behaved clumsily.

2. **Refusal to use Kraaijenhoff**: [[Th. E. R. Kraaijenhoff|Kraaijenhoff]], who had previously spoken with the Sultan and knew him personally, had offered to carry the letter himself. Nieuwenhuizen refused, deeming it too dangerous for Kraaijenhoff's life. Van Swieten considered this fear unfounded and argued that the Commissioner had "sacrificed a probable great advantage to avoid an imaginary danger to individuals."

3. **Failure to anchor properly**: The ships anchored aggressively close to shore instead of at the customary roadstead, projecting a threatening posture before any negotiation had been attempted.

4. **Premature rupture**: The negotiations were broken off too quickly, without sufficient exploration of the possibility that the Sultan, while indecisive, was not unwilling.

## The Telegram and the Governor-General's Reply

Nieuwenhuizen's indecision manifested most clearly in his telegram of [[17 March]] from [[Pinang]] to [[J. Loudon|Loudon]], requesting permission to bombard immediately upon declaration of war. Loudon, perceiving in this request a reluctance to act on his own responsibility, telegraphed back: he gave Nieuwenhuizen full authority to act as he thought fit, even outside his instructions; he would cover him with his responsibility. "If peaceful solution is impossible, the watchword must be: act vigorously. Trust in me, as I do in you."

[[Van Swieten]] noted that a Government Commissioner must have the courage to act on his own responsibility in everything concerning his mission, even contrary to his instructions, when circumstances arise that the instructions did not foresee. To shrink from acting on one's own responsibility and shift the burden to a Governor-General who is not present and cannot assess the necessity of deviation is, Van Swieten wrote, "not to be approved."

## Legacy

Nieuwenhuizen's role in the opening chapter of the Aceh War remains one of the most contested in Dutch colonial history. Van Swieten placed the principal blame for the war's outbreak — and its subsequent duration — on the failures of March [[1873]], and Nieuwenhuizen's handling of the negotiations was, in his judgment, the proximate cause. The moderate Acehnese voices that might have produced a peaceful settlement, notably [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]], were alienated by the clumsy and aggressive posture of the Dutch approach. "Probably the war would not have come," Van Swieten wrote, "if the negotiations of March [[1873]] had been better conducted."
