**J. Loudon** was a Dutch colonial administrator who served as [[Governor-General of the Netherlands India|Governor-General of Netherlands India]] from [[1 January]] [[1872]] until his honourable discharge by [[Royal Decree]] of [[17 December]] [[1874]], his term dominated almost entirely by the outbreak and early years of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. He took over the administration from his predecessor [[Mr. P. Mijer|P. Mijer]] on [[1 January]] [[1872]], and his governorship is chiefly remembered for the disastrous [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]] of [[April 1873]] and the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second expedition]] that captured the [[Kraton of Aceh|kraton]] in [[January 1874]] but failed to end Acehnese resistance.

## Outbreak of the Aceh War

The immediate origin of the war lay in the long series of Dutch-Acehnese disputes over piracy, sovereignty on the Sumatran coasts, and the international guarantee of Acehnese independence contained in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|tractaat of 1824]]. After the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra-tractaat of 2 November 1871]] freed the Netherlands from the British reservation regarding expansion in Sumatra, the Loudon government moved to assert Dutch sovereignty over [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. In [[September 1871]] the controller [[E. R. Krayenhoff]] was sent aboard the *Djambi* to deliver a letter from the Governor of Sumatra's Westkust to the [[Sultan of Aceh|sultan]], the fourteen-year-old [[Aladin Mahmood Shah]], who ruled under the regency of the Arab sayyid [[Habib Abdoerrahman|Habib Abdoe'r Rachman]].

When the sjabandar of Atjeh, [[Panglima Tiban Mohammed]], arrived in [[December 1872]] at Riouw with credentials from the sultan, the Indian government decided to support the sultan against the hostile Arab party — but only on condition of recognition of Dutch supremacy. After the [[Consul-General W. H. Read|Consul-General in Singapore]] reported on [[16 February]] [[1873]] that the Acehnese envoys had sought American and Italian intervention, Loudon's government concluded that "longer dralen was ongeoorloofd" and authorised the dispatch of a powerful naval force to demand clarification and recognition.

## The decision to send the expedition (February 1873)

On [[22 February]] [[1873]], Loudon informed the Hague — by telegram — that the [[Raad van Nederlandsch Indie|Council of the Netherlands Indies]], presided over by the Governor-General and augmented by the commander of the army and the commander of the navy, had unanimously decided to send [[T.N. Nieuwenhuyzen|the vice-president of the Council]] as *gouvernements-commissaris* to Atjeh with the necessary military force "to instil awe and to give weight to our demands." Loudon stated that he had already asked [[T.N. Nieuwenhuyzen|Nieuwenhuyzen]] whether he was inclined to undertake this mission before receiving the ministerial telegram of [[18 February]] — a statement that contradicted [[I.D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]]'s representation in the [[Tweede Kamer|Second Chamber]] that the initiative had come from the Governor-General rather than from the Minister.

In a letter of [[25 February]] [[1873]] (No. 9 of the overgelegde stukken), Loudon wrote: "De noodzakelijkheid der expeditie is een uitgemaakte zaak. Zelfs al mogt de verzekering worden ontvangen dat Amerika zich even weinig als Italië zal bemoeijen. Aan de dubbelenzinnige politiek van Atjeh dient een einde te komen. Zoolang het onze souvereiniteit niet erkent, blijft vreemde tusschenkomst ons als het zwaard van Damocles bedreigen. Het fait accompli is het eenig redmiddel." — "The necessity of the expedition is settled. Even if assurance were received that America would as little meddle as Italy. Atjeh's ambiguous policy must end. So long as it does not recognise our sovereignty, foreign intervention threatens us like the sword of Damocles. The fait accompli is the only remedy." This passage, cited during the [[Tweede Kamer debate on Atjeh war origins (April 1874)|Tweede Kamer debate]], demonstrated that the expedition was intended regardless of whether foreign interference actually existed.

## The ultimatum and departure from ministerial instructions

Loudon's *gouvernements-commissaris* [[T.N. Nieuwenhuyzen|Nieuwenhuyzen]] departed from the ministerial instructions in a crucial respect: the ultimatum delivered to the Sultan on [[22 March]] [[1873]] demanded recognition of Dutch sovereignty as the first requirement, with only 24 hours (changed from the originally stipulated 14 days) for compliance, and then bombardment of the Sultan's residence would follow. The manifest sent ashore stated that it would be delivered to the Sultan that same day and would be confirmed "by the firing of some projectiles into or near the Sultan's residence, from which we are only 3,600 metres distant." [[I.D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] had telegraphed that sovereignty or war as a first demand would make a bad impression both in the Netherlands and abroad; Loudon insisted on it. The Minister's telegram of [[7 March]] stated: "Wij mogen en willen uwe handen niet binden door verbod, maar meenen dat souvereiniteit of oorlog als eerste eisch hier en elders slechten indruk zou maken" — "We may not and will not bind your hands by prohibition, but we believe that sovereignty or war as a first demand would make a bad impression here and elsewhere."

When Loudon nevertheless persisted — telegram of [[9 March]]: "Geen andere waarborg denkbaar dan souvereiniteits-erkenning; zonder dit expeditie geen zin" — and asked for definite orders or freedom to act on his own responsibility, the Minister ultimately acquiesced. The command was not refused.

## The First Expedition (April 1873)

Loudon appointed [[F. N. Nieuwenhuysen]] as commissioner and [[J. H. R. Köhler|Major-general J. H. R. Köhler]] as military commander of what became the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]]. The expedition — three bataljons of the Indies army, a bataljon of the Barissan, and a naval landing division — sailed from Batavia on [[22 March]] [[1873]] and arrived at the roadstead of Atjeh on [[5 April]]. After the [[Battle of Kotta Mogat|failure at Kotta Mogat]] on [[8 April]] and the [[Battle of the Missigit (10 April 1873)|bloody fighting at the missigit]] on [[10 April]], General Köhler was mortally wounded on [[14 April]] while inspecting the captured mosque. A council of war on [[18 April]] advised against renewed attack, and on [[29 April]] the transport fleet left the roadstead of Atjeh — the first campaign had failed, chiefly because of "the unfamiliarity with the situation, the too low opinion of the enemy's strength and perseverance, and the consequent inadequate preparation of the expedition."

## The Second Expedition and Capture of the Kraton

Preparations for a second expedition were put in hand immediately. In the Netherlands troops were levied to bring the army up to strength, all troops were equipped with breech-loading rifles, and the artillery was considerably reinforced. The expedition, 6354 infantry besides officers, with cavalry, artillery and engineers, was placed under the retired Lieutenant-general [[Jan van Swieten|Jan van Swieten]], restored to active service, with [[G. M. Verspyck|Major-general G. M. Verspyck]] as second-in-command. After delays caused by cholera on Java and aboard the transports, the fleet arrived at Atjeh between [[20 November|20]] and [[29 November]] [[1873]]. On [[24 January]] [[1874]] the [[Kraton of Aceh|kraton]] itself was found abandoned and occupied; the Dutch flag was hoisted amid the playing of the *Wilhelmus*. Two days later, on [[26 January]] [[1874]], the sultan died of cholera at Pagger Ajer, and on [[31 January]] Van Swieten issued his [[Van Swieten's Proclamation of 31 January 1874|proclamation]] annexing Groot-Atjeh by right of conquest.

## Domestic Reforms

Loudon's governorship also produced important legislative and administrative reforms. The [[ordonnantie]] of [[6 May]] [[1872]] established the penal code for natives (*wetboek van strafrecht voor inlanders*), which came into force on [[1 January]] [[1873]]; [[Mr. T. H. der Kinderen|T. H. der Kinderen]], president of the two high courts of Netherlands India, rendered great service in its drafting. The inspector-general of finances [[P. H. B. Motké]] was appointed to investigate the financial administration but died in [[December 1872]] before completing his task. The first steps were taken toward the great harbour works at [[Tanjung Priok|Tandjong Priok]], and the agrarian regulations were amended by Royal Decrees of [[16 April]] [[1872]] and [[3 December]] [[1873]].

## The Conversie Quaestie and Dismissal

Loudon's downfall arose from the question of the conversion of communal into individual land ownership (*conversie van communaal in individueel bezit*). The general secretary [[H. D. Levyssohn Norman|Levyssohn Norman]], in July [[1874]], undertook a journey through Java and Madura to encourage such conversion — going beyond his formal instructions and acting on telegraphic authorisation from Loudon. The Minister of Colonies, [[W. van Goltstein|Van Goltstein]], sent a telegram on [[4 November]] [[1874]] ordering the journey to be halted, and the Governor-General was requested to refrain from any further pressure for conversion and from issuing any general ordinance without consultation with the Opperbestuur. Loudon complied but at the same time requested honourable discharge, which was granted by Royal Decree of [[17 December]] [[1874]]. He was succeeded by [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Mr. J. W. van Lansberge]].

## Assessment in the Tweede Kamer debate

During the [[Tweede Kamer debate on Atjeh war origins (April 1874)|Tweede Kamer debate]] of April [[1874]], multiple speakers criticised Loudon's conduct. [[Haffmans|Haffmans]] argued that Loudon was inspired by the desire to please the Minister, believing the Minister to be energetic, and that Loudon would have acted entirely differently with a cautious Minister — "Stoute hoofden maken stoute handen" (bold heads make bold hands). [[Nierstrasz|Nierstrasz]] concluded that the honour and moral judgment of the war belonged exclusively to the Minister of Colonies, since the Minister had set the process in motion with the telegram of [[18 February]]; Loudon had merely responded to the Minister's initiative, though he went further than the Minister had indicated, and the Minister approved the further step on [[22 February]]. The Minister himself acknowledged the difference: "Het verschil betreft inleiding en vorm" (the difference concerns introduction and form) — meaning that on the substance, the two were agreed: both wanted the war.
