**Sultan Jauhar Alum** (also rendered *Djohor Alum*, *Jowhar 00l Aulum*, *Johar al Allum Shah*) was the [[Sultan of Acheh]] from [[1802]] until his death in November [[1823]], "so far as circumstances allowed" — a qualification that captures the fragility of his reign. He is best known as the Achehnese party to the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of Pedir]] signed with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Sir Stamford Raffles]] on [[22 April]] [[1819]], an instrument that, in the words of the Bengal government, was to remain a [[dead letter]] from the day it was signed.

## Background and the Acheh succession dispute

Jauhar Alum succeeded his father on the Achehnese throne in [[1802]], but, as had been the case with every predecessor, he faced a powerful domestic opposition. After ten years of his rule, that opposition judged itself strong enough to seek a replacement, and a faction of the Achehnese chiefs turned to [[Said Hussein]], a wealthy Penang trader of Arab descent (a *said*) and a remote relative of the sultan's family. Hussein declined the crown for himself but accepted it on behalf of his second son [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] (the future Sultan Saif ul-Alum), who until then had served as boatswain on his father's vessels. An expedition fitted out at [[Penang]] placed Syf on the throne at [[Acheh]] in mid-[[1815]], and Jauhar Alum was driven from the capital.

Expelled from [[Banda Acheh]], the old sultan established his residence at [[Pedir]] on the north coast, where he was protected by the powerful local chief [[Tunku Pakih]]. For several years Acheh thus had two rival sultans: Jauhar at Pedir (called in the British records the *Sultan Lama*, *Rajah Lama*, or "old king") and Syf, who, when in turn driven out of the capital, fixed his abode at [[Telok Semawé]] on the east coast. At Acheh itself the effective authority lay with the federation of the [[Three Sagis]] and, above all, with [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polim]], chief of the [[XXII Mukims]].

## Raffles, the Acheh Commission and the Treaty of Pedir (1819)

When, in early [[1819]], the Bengal government under [[Lord Moira|Lord Hastings]] dispatched a joint commission of Raffles and [[Captain Coombs]] to Acheh, Jauhar Alum was Raffles's preferred candidate. Raffles went so far as to send the Company's ship *Minto*, under [[Lieutenant Criddle|Lt. Criddle]], ahead to Pedir to assure Jauhar Alum of the Commission's approach and to enquire into rumours of Dutch negotiations with him — a proceeding Raffles undertook without consulting either Coombs or [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Governor Bannerman]] of [[Penang]]. The Commission itself, after seven fruitless weeks on the roadstead of Acheh, eventually proceeded to Pedir, where Jauhar Alum received the commissioners and, on [[22 April]] [[1819]], concluded the treaty that bears Pedir's name.

Under the treaty the Sultan promised eternal friendship, granted free trade to the British in his ports, engaged to receive a British resident, and (in the celebrated article 6) undertook to exclude the subjects of every other European power, and all Americans, from fixed residence in his dominions. In return, the British government promised to help him remove the pretender Syf, and immediately placed in his hands arms, ammunition and bills on Penang to the value of 100,000 rupees. Before Syf's submission could be enforced, the [[Shahbandar of Acheh]] was sent to Pedir to offer the old sultan the submission of the three Sagis — a feint that ended in the [[Death of Tunku Pakih|murder of Tunku Pakih]] by the Shahbandar.

## A "dead letter" and final years

The Bengal government's report of [[7 October]] [[1820]] made clear that Jauhar Alum never recovered his lost authority. He "never was in a situation to enable him to carry into execution any stipulation of that treaty"; his remaining years were spent "in one stockade at the mouth of the [[Acheh River]] without commanding influence or even respect", while the whole power at Acheh remained in the hands of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polim]]. A letter from the Sultan to Penang of [[15 April]] [[1822]] — in which he explained that he could not move to the capital because Syf had ruined its houses and carried off its guns, and that he commanded only some 250 sepoys — confirms the picture. After an exchange of letters with the [[Three Sagis]] in [[1822]] and a final [[Sartorius mission to Acheh|mission by Mr. Sartorius]] in [[1823]], the Penang government gave up all serious hope of restoring him. Jauhar Alum died in November [[1823]], leaving legitimate descendants and a disputed succession that further weakened the sultan's party.

## The treaty's afterlife

Although the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] was recognised by the London authorities as early as [[4 August]] [[1824]] as a dead letter, the British plenipotentiaries at the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London negotiations of March 1824]] unexpectedly raised it at the eleventh hour. The result was the insertion of an Acheh reservation in the Notes exchanged alongside the treaty, by which the Netherlands undertook to respect Acheh's independence — a clause that, as [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] argues at length, became the Trojan horse of Anglo-Dutch relations over [[Sumatra]] for half a century, until the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871]] finally removed the reservation.
