**Paulus van Caerden** was a Dutch vice-admiraal in the service of the [[Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie|Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie]] — and after [[1602]] of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie]] — who commanded the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|second Dutch commercial visit to Atjeh]] in [[1600]]-[[1601]], conducted a punitive campaign against the Spanish in the Moluccas in [[1606]]-[[1608]] in which he was captured and held prisoner, and was the elder kinsman (probably uncle) of the better-known **Cornelis van Caerden** also in VOC service. He is, after the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers]], the principal Dutch commander at Atjeh in the pre-VOC period.

## The Atjeh visit (1600-1601)

Van Caerden sailed from the Netherlands on [[21 December]] [[1599]] as vice-admiraal of the fleet of [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] for the [[Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie|Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie]]. Sent by his admiral to the west coast of Sumatra, he visited [[Priaman]], [[Tikoe]] and [[Pasaman]] without result, and decided to try Atjeh for cargo, "hoewel hij wist wat [[Cornelis de Houtman|De Houtman]] daar was overkomen". He arrived off Atjeh on [[21 September]] [[1600]].

The full account of his Atjeh visit is given in the entry on the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|visit itself]]. In summary: he obtained the release of the surviving [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman captives]] including [[Hans Decker|Hans Decker]]; signed a pepper contract with the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]] on [[28 December]] [[1600]]; received the escaped [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] on [[30 December]] [[1600]]; seized pepper from Arab junks at anchor on [[12 January]] [[1601]] after the sultan's sabander rearrested Houtman; was fired on by the shore batteries; burned several coastal craft; released his prisoners; and sailed for [[Bantam|Bantam]] by way of the west coast of Sumatra, taking on no cargo.

Van der Woude's verdict on his conduct at Atjeh is mixed. The Zeeland merchants [[Gerard le Roy|Gerard le Roy]] and [[Laurens Bicker|Laurens Bicker]] later argued that Van Caerden had exceeded his commission in seizing the Arab junks, since the contract gave the Dutch a monopoly only on the new crop, not on cargo already in port — and they undertook to compensate the Arab claimants for the ƒ 51,048 damages. Van der Woude defends Van Caerden against the rederij: "Van Caerden was gealarmeerd door de berichten van de ontvluchte Hollanders en wantrouwde den vorst, indachtig [[Frederik de Houtman|De Houtman]]'s waarschuwing. Bovendien was hij er op bedacht waar te ontvangen voor het voorgeschoten geld." His conduct, in Van der Woude's view, was the natural response to the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]]'s "onbetrouwbare, weifelmoedige houding", though "onvoorzichtig op te goed vertrouwen concessies deed".

## Capture by the Spanish (1607)

After the founding of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] in [[1602]], Van Caerden continued in Company service. In [[1606]] he commanded a fleet sent to the Moluccas against the Spanish, where the Spaniards had recently re-established themselves at [[Ternate|Ternate]]. In [[1607]] — Van der Woude's note at chapter X records — he "inmiddels met zeventien man in handen van de Spanjaarden was gevallen". His capture deprived the VOC of one of its most experienced Indies commanders at a critical moment. He was held prisoner by the Spanish for several years; the eventual fate of his captivity is not pursued in Van der Woude's narrative.

## Significance

Paulus van Caerden is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal Dutch commander at Atjeh in the pre-VOC period, and the principal instance of the difficulty the early Dutch companies faced in operating at the Achehnese court. His Atjeh visit produced the first formal Dutch-Achehnese pepper contract, the release of the surviving Houtman captives, and the first armed clash between the Dutch and the Atjeh shore batteries — three "firsts" that together frame the visit as the pivot of the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship. His subsequent capture by the Spanish in the Moluccas deprived the VOC of an experienced commander and contributed to the strategic difficulties of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] era. The controversy over his seizure of the Arab junks at Atjeh — and the long-running compensation claim that followed — also established the legal-political pattern that would recur throughout the Dutch-Atjeh relationship: aggressive commercial action by a Dutch commander, controversy at home about whether his instructions permitted it, and a long-running compensation claim.
