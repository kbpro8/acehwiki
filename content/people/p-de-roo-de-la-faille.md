**P. de Roo de la Faille** was a Dutch colonial-era scholar whose posthumously circulated monograph *[[Historische Curiositeiten uit Malajoe en Java]]* (Den Haag, [[March]] [[1954]]) gathers nine "curiosities" from the historical literature of the [[Malay Archipelago|Malajoe]] and [[Java]], all read through the lens of *[[bedekte taal]]* — the veiled or allegorical register that, in his reading, hides references to the [[Aroe|Aru]] country and the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] sphere beneath the surface of Javanese and Malay chronicles. He dedicated the work to [[Dr. D. J. Hulshoff Pol|Dr D. J. Hulshoff Pol]], whom he addressed as a former Officier van Gezondheid at [[Tandjoeng Poera]] with the [[K.N.I.L.|K.N.I.L. Leger]] — that is, as a man who had himself served in the very [[Aroe]] country the book places at the centre of early Malay history.

## Method and intellectual debt

De Roo de la Faille writes from within the Dutch-Indological tradition of [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Veth]], [[Dr. J. Brandes|Brandes]], [[G. P. Rouffaer|Rouffaer]], [[Dr. H. N. van der Tuuk|Van der Tuuk]] and the *Bijdragen van het Koninklijk Instituut*, but he reads these authorities against the grain. Where Rouffaer, for instance, identified Marco Polo's *Gauenispola* with the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait]] light-tower on [[Pulo Bras|Poelo Bras]] and explained the name from Malay *gawé*, De Roo de la Faille presses further: the name is, in his reading, a *wangsalan* (riddle) on *Ka-poe-laga-nis*, a concealed form of *Kapoelaga-Qaciolah*, the camphor-name by which the Hindu-Javanese chronicles denoted the ancestral land of "Taroema" at [[Aroe]]. He is similarly prepared to correct Rouffaer on *Pati Oenoes* and Pigafetta's list of Javanese cities, and to take [[van Stein Callenfels]] to task for dismissing the allegorical geography of the Old-Javanese *Sang Satyawan* as "zuivere nonsens".

His interpretive confidence rests on the claim — repeatedly illustrated — that Javanese and Malay lettered men, when they wished to record matters that touched on royal shame or ancestral displacement, did so in *bahasa kapoer* (the "lime-language" of [[Malajoe]]), and that the foreign traveller (Pinto, [[Nicolo de Conti|De Conti]], [[Marco Polo]], [[Ibn Battuta]], [[Pigafetta]], [[Tome Pires|Tome Pires]]) was systematically gulled by it. The *Curiositeiten* is thus as much a key to the method as a catalogue of instances.

## Connection with Acheh

De Roo de la Faille's importance for [[Acheh Wiki History|AcehWiki]] lies chiefly in the central chapter on [[Mendez Pinto]]'s [[1539]] voyage, which is the only sustained modern retelling of the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjeh-Aru war of 1539]]: the [[Sultan of Atjeh|Atjehnese sultan]]'s campaign against the [[Radja of Aroe|radja of Aroe]], the [[Alleredin Mahomet|Atjehsche vloot]] of 130 vessels, the fall of Aroe, and the Queen of Aroe's flight to [[Malacca]] and [[Bentan]] and her remarriage to the [[Sultan of Djohor|Sultan of Johor]]. He also reproduces, in the chapter *Over Soenan Kalidjaga*, the legend of [[Puteri Idjo|Putri Hijau]] — the fairy-tale queen of [[Deli]]/[[Asahan]] whose rejection of the [[Sultan of Atjeh]]'s marriage suit and whose glass-coffin voyage to Atjeh, lost at sea on the way, are among the few widely-circulated Acehnese legends of the east-coast polities.

## Significance

For the wiki, De Roo de la Faille's significance is double. He is, first, the conduit through which the Portuguese and Italian sources on sixteenth-century Atjeh reach a Dutch-reading audience in a single interpretive frame; and second, the principal exponent of a hermeneutic — *bedekte taal* — that, whether or not one accepts each of his particular identifications, must be reckoned with whenever an AcehWiki entry touches on the disguised geography of the Malay and Javanese chronicles.
