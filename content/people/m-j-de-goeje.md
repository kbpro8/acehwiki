**M. J. de Goeje** (Michael Jan de Goeje, 1836–1909) was a distinguished Dutch Orientalist and professor of Arabic at [[Leyden]], whose name is cited by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* in connection with the comparative study of popular demonological beliefs.

The reference occurs in a footnote appended to Snouck Hurgronje's discussion of the **burong** — the evil spirits which, in Achehnese popular belief, are believed to possess pregnant women and women in childbirth. The Achehnese *burongs*, Snouck Hurgronje explains, bear a close resemblance to the **zars** which "are wont to 'possess' the women of [[Mecca|Mekka]]." As in Mecca, so in Acheh, these spirits have "certain desires which they express to the comprehension of expert enquirers through the lips of the victims whom they have bereft of their senses." The Achehnese experts, like their Meccan counterparts, do not confine themselves to reciting a *tangkay* or exorcising incantation; "they must first know what burong it is they have to do with," and so enquire whence they come and what their intentions and wishes are. For the comparative material on the Meccan side, Snouck Hurgronje refers the reader to his own *Mekka*, vol. II, pp. 124 et seq., and to "M. J. de Goeje and Th. Nöldeke in the *Zeitschrift der Deutschen Morgenländischen Gesellschaft*, Vol. XLIV, pp. 480 and 701."

The two citations in the same volume of the *ZDMG* — pp. 480 and 701 — show that de Goeje and [[Th. Nöldeke|Nöldeke]] were engaged, in the late 1880s, in a scholarly exchange on the subject of spirit-possession in the Muslim world. Their work, together with Snouck Hurgronje's own observations in *Mekka* (vol. II), provided the comparative framework within which the Achehnese material on the *burong* could be interpreted. The reference is a small but characteristic instance of the method that pervades Vol. I of *The Achehnese*: the institutions and beliefs of Acheh are continuously illuminated by comparison with those of the Arabs, the Indians, and the other Muslim peoples among whom Dutch, German, and British Orientalists had begun, in the late nineteenth century, to lay the foundations of comparative Islamic studies.

De Goeje was one of the leading figures of that enterprise. As professor at Leyden and as a long-serving editor of the *ZDMG*, he played a central role in the institutional consolidation of Arabic and Islamic studies in the Netherlands, and trained or influenced a generation of scholars including Snouck Hurgronje himself. His collaboration with Nöldeke on questions of popular demonology is thus part of the broader intellectual genealogy of *The Achehnese* — a work that would have been impossible without the comparative scaffolding erected by the Leyden Orientalists and their German counterparts in the closing decades of the nineteenth century.
