**E. de Waal** (Engelbert de Waal) was a Dutch colonial administrator and man of letters, the author of the multi-volume *Onze Indische Financiën* ("Our Indies Finances") and of *Nederlandsch-Indië in de Staten-Generaal*, who is cited both by [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] in his 1900 monograph on the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Raffles–Coombs convention]] and by [[P. Brooshooft|Brooshooft]] in his popular history of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] ([[1886]]). De Waal's writings furnished later historians with a sustained fiscal and political analysis of the diplomacy that led to the [[sumatra-tractaat-(1871)|Sumatra Treaty of 1871]] and so ultimately to the Aceh War.

## Author of *Onze Indische Financiën*

De Waal's *Onze Indische Financiën*, of which the sixth volume is most frequently cited by Van der Kemp, was a sustained analysis of Netherlands-Indian fiscal and colonial policy in the second half of the nineteenth century. It combined statistical depth with political judgment, and Van der Kemp draws upon it for a series of observations on the diplomacy of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of London (1824)]], on the [[Pinang Nota (1825)|Pinang Nota]] of [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]], and on the Sumatra-tractaat of [[1871]]. He is also the author of *Nederlandsch-Indië in de Staten-Generaal*, volume I of which (p. 82) Van der Kemp cites as one of the standard printed collections of the Anglo-Dutch treaty documents of 1824.

Brooshooft listed De Waal's *Onze Indische Financiën* among his principal secondary sources, using it for the financial and constitutional background of the war — above all for his argument that the war had been forced on the Netherlands by Acehnese piracy and intransigence rather than by the recklessness of any single minister.

## On Palmerston and the Letter of the 1824 Treaty

The most striking of De Waal's observations, drawn upon by Van der Kemp, concerns the use made by Lord Palmerston of the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824. Palmerston, De Waal wrote, "simply attached himself to the letter of the treaty of 1824" in resisting Dutch extensions of authority on Sumatra. The treaty provided only that England would not establish itself on Sumatra; it did not confer upon the Netherlands an explicit right to extend its authority there. This narrow reading of Article 2 of the 1824 treaty, in conjunction with the Dutch declaration of Acehnese "independence" annexed to the treaty, allowed Palmerston to protest Dutch advances on the East Coast of Sumatra as inconsistent with the spirit, if not the letter, of the 1824 settlement.

## On the Cape of Guinea Trade-off

De Waal's most substantial intervention in the Aceh debate concerns the celebrated trade-off between the Sumatra-tractaat and the surrender of the [[cape-of-guinea|Cape of Guinea]] in the same year. Public opinion in the Netherlands had long held that the Dutch abandonment of the Guinea Coast was the price exacted by Britain for the withdrawal of its Aceh protest. De Waal, in *Onze Indische Financiën* (volume VI, pp. 69, 71), "condemned this view as an historical inaccuracy." According to De Waal, he himself, as minister, had offered the Guinea Coast to England "without particular occasion," in order to put an end to the fighting among the peoples of the coast, and it was only afterwards that England demanded that the two treaties "should stand and fall together."

## On the Style of the British Notes

"The style of the British notes," De Waal dryly observed, "really did not always excel in politeness." The remark, from page 57 of the same sixth volume, is cited by Van der Kemp during his discussion of the British protests against the Dutch differential tariff policy of the 1820s–1830s, and is emblematic of De Waal's combination of fiscal expertise with an acute sense of diplomatic style. De Waal's writings furnished later historians with the historical sensitivity that the more pedantic narratives of [[G. Lauts|Lauts]], [[smulders|Smulders]], and [[J. J. Meinsma|Meinsma]] lacked.
