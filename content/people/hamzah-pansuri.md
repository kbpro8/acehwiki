**Hamzah Pansuri** — in Kreemer's spelling **Hamzah Pansoeri** (also **Hamzah FanQoerï**), in [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]]'s text **Hamzah Fansurī** — was the principal pantheistic mystic of [[Acheh]] in the late sixteenth century, "of [[Baros|Baros]]" on the Westkust of Sumatra in the Tapanoeli residency, and the founder of the school of [[Wujudiyyah]] mysticism against which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] directed his polemic after his arrival in Acheh in [[1637]]. He was one of the most famous pantheistic mystics of [[Aceh|Atjèh]] and one of the earliest Malay mystical poets; his works are among the oldest Malay mystical literature. He was executed by Sultan [[Sultan Iskandar II|Iskandar Thanï]] after the orthodox victory under [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Noer-ad-dïn ar-Ranïrï]], and his works were burned before the great mosque **Beit ar-rahman**.

The forerunner and spiritual predecessor of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Sumatra]], a highly celebrated heretical mystic who flourished before the early 17th century, Hamzah championed a pantheistic mysticism later continued by Shamsuddin. Sultan Meukuta Alam's successor had his disciples put to death and his books burned, yet many manuscripts survived and his works are still read and admired. [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Abdurra'uf of Singkel]] is described in spiritual genealogies as being "of the tribe of Hamzah Pansuri," despite Abdurra'uf's own opposition to Hamzah's pantheism.

## The doctrine of the school

The doctrine of Hamzah Pansuri's school is the [[Wujudiyyah]] in its most extreme Achehnese form. The [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] of Raniri places Hamzah among the lower-wind (*benedenwindse*) Wujūdiyyah — that is, the Achehnese branch of the school — and treats his doctrines alongside those of the Tanāsuḥiyya (metempsychosists), the Falāsifa (philosophers), the Barāhima-Wathaniyya, the Sāmaniyya of Tibet, and the Ḥulūliyya (those who teach the indwelling of God in creation). The juxtaposition is itself the principal scholastic characterisation of Hamzah's school that we possess.

## Works cited by Raniri

The [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] of Raniri cites two works of Hamzah by name:

1. The **[[Asrar al-'arifin|*Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]]** ("The Secrets of the Gnostics") — cited in the section of the *Tibjān* on the pre-Muslim religions, where Raniri inserts a quotation from Hamzah into his translation from the [[Tamhid fi bayan at-tawhid|*Tamhīd*]] of [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|as-Sālimī]] in order to refute it. The passage cited concerns the fall of Iblīs from the light of God, and is paralleled at p. 142 of the [[J. Doorenbos|Doorenbos]] edition of the *Asrār al-ʿārifīn*.
2. The **[[Kitab al-Muntahi|*Kitāb al-Muntahī*]]** ("The Book of the Ultimate") — cited and refuted at length in the section of the *Tibjān* on the Wujūdiyyah (pp. 97–105 of the Voorhoeve facsimile). Raniri cites the *Kitāb al-Muntahī* literally, section by section, and refutes each citation in turn.

A notable passage of the *Kitāb al-Muntahī* cited by Raniri contains a play, in the language of [[Pasai]], on the words *kuphō* (Arabic *kufr*, "unbelief") and *kuphu* (Arabic *kufʾ*, "equal, like"). The play, as [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] notes, depends on the fact that *pada* in Achehnese means both "equal" (Javanese *pepada*) and "sufficient" (Malay *pada*). Hamzah's utterance — "if there is no *kuphō*, there is no meeting with *kuphu*" — Raniri takes as a covert profession of unbelief; Voorhoeve suggests that Hamzah's intended meaning was that the highest mystical state is not attained so long as the gate of the perishable world is not closed.

## The orthodox victory and the burning of the books

When [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Noer-ad-dïn ar-Ranïrï]] returned to Atjèh in [[1637]], he found the court under Sultan [[Sultan Iskandar II|Iskandar Thanï]] still influenced by the heterodox teachings of Hamzah Pansoeri and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Sjamsoeddin van Pasè]]. Ar-Ranïrï's conflict with them led to the orthodox victory: both were executed by Iskandar Thanï, and their works were burned before the great mosque **Beit ar-rahman**.

The burning was not wholly effective: manuscripts of the *[[Asrar al-'arifin|Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and the *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] survive — the latter in Cod. Or. 7291(3) and Cod. Or. 1952, with a Javanese translation in Cod. Or. 5716(2) and 7736(1) — and the *Asrār al-ʿārifīn* was edited by [[J. Doorenbos|Doorenbos]].

## Role as editor of the Malay translation of the Burda

In a sixteenth-century Malay translation of the *Burda* of [[Al-Busiri]], Hamzah Pansuri is identified as one of the editors who supplied emendations to the text. His contributions appear in footnotes such as "80) hs. manusjia; Ar. fPl‑djasam, in het vette der aarde. De tekestverbetering is ingegeven door Hamzah Fansoeri ed. J. Doorenbos bl. 46: lemah manis terlalu njaman." These notes show his work in correcting archaic Malay spellings and providing Arabic glosses through the medium of the [[J. Doorenbos|Doorenbos]] edition of the *Asrār al-ʿārifīn*. This editorial tradition — Hamzah's textual authority being invoked in the apparatus of a later Burda edition — attests to the long shadow that Hamzah's scholarship cast over Malay Islamic literary culture well after his own death and the burning of his books.

## Literary legacy and the èleumèë salé' tradition

Despite the condemnation and burning of his works, Hamzah Pansoeri's poetry survived and continued to influence Malay mystical literature throughout the Archipelago. His "of Baros" epithet connects him to the Westkust town of [[Baros|Baros]] in the Tapanoeli residency. Kreemer records that Hamzah Pansoeri's teaching lived on in the *[[Èleumèë|èleumèë salé']* ("science of the wanderer") tradition — a continuation of his pantheistic mysticism. Around [[1860]], **Teungkoe Teureuboeë** of the IX Moekim's Keumangan (Pidië) was a controversial practitioner of the *èleumèë salé'*, who was murdered by the Oelèëbalang of Pidië for heretical teachings.

## Significance

Hamzah Pansuri is the principal pantheistic mystic of [[Acheh]] and the founder of the school of [[Wujudiyyah]] mysticism against which Raniri directed his polemic. The Voorhoeve account of [[1955]] adds substantially to the picture drawn by Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* by documenting the textual basis of Hamzah's school: the *[[Asrar al-'arifin|Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and the *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] are the principal surviving works of Hamzah, and the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] of Raniri preserves, in its polemical citations, the only extended fragments of these works. The play on *kuphō* and *kuphu* in the *Kitāb al-Muntahī* is a remarkable trace of the linguistic specificity of the *[[Bahasa Djawi]]* of the Achehnese court. Kreemer's account, in turn, supplies the additional detail of the *èleumèë salé'* tradition through which Hamzah's heterodox teaching survived into the nineteenth century — a thread that runs from the late-sixteenth-century mystic of Baros to the heretical Teungkoe Teureuboeë, murdered by the Oelèëbalang of Pidië around [[1860]].
