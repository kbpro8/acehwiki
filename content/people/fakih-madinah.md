**Fakih Madinah** — Atjehnese form *Pakeh Madina* — is the literary character of the Atjehnese and Malay chronicle-epic tradition who serves, in the [[Hikajat Malém Dagang|Atjehnese *Hikajat Malém Dagang*]] and its Malay reworking the *[[Hikajat Sultan Atjeh Marhoem]]*, as a *pawang* (ritual specialist) aboard an Atjehnese war-fleet, despite his Islamic office as a *fakīh* (jurisconsult) "uit Madīnat al-Nabī, stad van de Profeet" (from Madīnat al-Nabī, the city of the Prophet). He is, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s reading in [[Over Maleise Literatuur (1947)|his survey]] of Malay literature, the principal literary evidence for the survival of pre-Islamic *pawang* practice in the Islamicised Atjehnese court.

## The Name and Office

The character is named *Fakih Madinah* in the Malay *[[Hikajat Sultan Atjeh Marhoem]]* and *Pakeh Madina* in the Atjehnese [[Hikajat Malém Dagang|original]]. The name is, in both forms, the Arabic *Faqīh Madīnah* — "the jurisconsult from Medina" — and the character's office is correspondingly Islamic: he is, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s gloss, "wetgeleerde, kenner der Moslimse plichtenleer, uit Madīnat al-Nabī, stad van de Profeet" (jurisconsult, expert in the Muslim law of duties, from Madīnat al-Nabī, the city of the Prophet). He is, in other words, a scholar of Islamic law from the city of the Prophet — a figure of considerable religious authority in the Islamic world.

## The Pawang Function

Despite his Islamic office, the *Fakih Madinah*/*Pakeh Madina* functions, in the chronicle-epic, as a *pawang* — the pre-Islamic Malay-Indonesian ritual specialist who controls the weather, calms the sea, and (above all) consults the *kětika* (auspicious-moment) tables to determine the right moment for any important undertaking. The *pawang*, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s chapter on folk-belief, is "de oude volkspriester, elders doekoen geheten en pēmangkoe, zich handhavend ondanks Islam en Hindoeisme" (the old folk-priest, elsewhere called *doekoen* and *pēmangkoe*, maintaining himself despite Islam and Hinduism). The *pawang* had originally served as the ritual specialist of the pre-Islamic Malay courts — responsible for the *pawang* of the fishery, the *pawang* of the rice-planting, and (above all) the *pawang* aboard the merchant and war fleets, who "verstand moest hebben van weer en wind en water" (had to have understanding of weather, wind, and water).

The *Fakih Madinah* of the chronicle-epic has, in Hooykaas's reading, taken over the *pawang* function aboard the Atjehnese war-fleet, despite his Islamic office: "Hieruit blijkt, dat de Moslimse omgeving van de pawang niets zag in zijn functie, dat strijdig geacht werd met de orthodoxe leer" (From this it appears that the Muslim environment of the *pawang* saw nothing in his function that was considered incompatible with orthodox teaching). The literary portrait of the *Fakih Madinah* is thus the evidence for the survival of the pre-Islamic *pawang* tradition in the Islamicised Atjehnese court, with the Islamic specialist taking over the ritual functions of the pre-Islamic priest.

## The Kětika Episode

The principal literary episode involving the *Fakih Madinah*/*Pakeh Madina* is the *kětika* consultation before battle. [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]] summarises the episode, in his commentary on the *Sadjarah Malajoe*:

"In de Atjehse Hikajat Malém Dagang, waarvan de Maleise Hik. Sultan Atjeh Marhoem een zwakke omwerking is, zien we de kětika dan ook zelfs gehanteerd door Pakeh Madina, d.i. de schriftgeleerde, kenner der Moslimse plichtenleer, uit Madinat al Nabi, stad van de Profeet. Dagenlang verbiedt hij de strijd, totdat de kětika gekomen is, en de Atjehers natuurlijk winnen!" (In the Atjehnese *Hikajat Malém Dagang*, of which the Malay *Hikajat Sultan Atjeh Marhoem* is a weak reworking, we see the *kětika* thus even handled by Pakeh Madina, that is, the scripture-scholar, expert in the Muslim law of duties, from Madīnat al-Nabī, the city of the Prophet. For days he forbids the battle, until the *kětika* has come, and the Atjehers naturally win!)

The episode is, in Hooykaas's reading, "niet historisch dan toch realistisch" (not historical but at any rate realistic) — it is not a record of a historical event, but it is a realistic portrait of the way the *pawang* tradition persisted in the Islamicised Atjehnese court, with the Islamic specialist taking over the *kětika* consultation that had once been the pre-Islamic *pawang*'s principal ritual function.

## The Pre-Islamic Survival

The *Fakih Madinah* episode is, in [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s broader argument in his chapter on folk-belief, part of the evidence for the survival of pre-Islamic ritual practice in the Islamicised Malay-Indonesian world. The *pawang*, in Hooykaas's reading, originally "verrichtte vele rituële handelingen voor het welzijn van allen en ter verkrijging van voorspoed, reciteerde gebeden gericht tot de geesten die de mensen welgezind zijn" (performed many ritual acts for the well-being of all and to obtain prosperity, recited prayers directed to the spirits that are well-disposed to people). With the arrival of Islam, the *pawang*'s "wegen kruisten niet die van de imam, de Moslimse voorganger van de rituële godsdienstoefening, noch die van het moskee-personeel" (ways did not cross those of the imam, the Muslim leader of the ritual worship, nor those of the mosque staff). The literary portrait of the *Fakih Madinah* — an Islamic specialist who serves as a *pawang* aboard a war-fleet — is the evidence that this practical coexistence persisted in the Atjehnese court well into the seventeenth century.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, the *Fakih Madinah*/*Pakeh Madina* is significant as the principal literary evidence for the survival of pre-Islamic *pawang* practice in the Islamicised Atjehnese court. His portrait in the [[Hikajat Malém Dagang|Atjehnese *Hikajat Malém Dagang*]] and the Malay *[[Hikajat Sultan Atjeh Marhoem]]* — an Islamic jurisconsult from Medina who serves as a *pawang* aboard a war-fleet and consults the *kětika* tables to determine the moment of battle — is, in Hooykaas's reading, the literary expression of the way the pre-Islamic *pawang* tradition persisted in the Islamicised Atjehnese court, with the Islamic specialist taking over the ritual functions of the pre-Islamic priest.
