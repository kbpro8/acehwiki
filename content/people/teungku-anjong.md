**Teungku Anjong** was the popular Achehnese name of **Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih**, an Arab *sayyid* of the Hadrami Bil-Faqih family who died on the 14th of Ramadhan 1196 H ([[August]] [[1782]]) and whose tomb, under the popular name *Teungku Anjong* in [[Gampong Jawa]] (also [[Kampong Java|Gampong Java]]) in [[Banda Acheh]], is — at the time of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s writing — "the tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh." His veneration had, by the close of the nineteenth century, completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh|Teungku di Kuala]] (alias [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Abdora'oh]], i.e. Abdurra'uf of Singkel), which had enjoyed the highest reputation in former times. The tomb served in [[1822]] as the site of the formal oath by which the chiefs of the [[Three Sagis]] — the [[XXII Mukims]], [[XXV Mukims]] and [[XXVI Mukims]] — renounced the deposed [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and committed themselves, by a binding religious act, never to receive him back as their ruler.

## The Sayyid's Name and Descent

The Sayyid's name itself reveals his descent. **Abu Bakar** (the Achehnese form of the Arabic *Abū Bakr*) was the name of the first of the four Companions; the patronymic **bin Husain** shows that he traced his descent through the [[Husain|Prophet's grandson Husain]], as all sayyids of the archipelago do; and **Bil-Faqih** — "of the jurist" — is the name of a celebrated Hadrami family, members of which settled in many parts of the archipelago. The Sayyid was thus an immigrant from the Hadramawt, or a descendant of recent Hadrami immigrants, who settled in [[Banda Acheh]] and there acquired, by the conjunction of his descent with the reputation of his personal piety, the supreme place in Achehnese veneration that his tomb now enjoys.

His popular name, *Teungku Anjong*, has a humbler origin. His real name being known to the learned, the Achehnese in general remember him only by his dwelling-place: he had lived during his lifetime in a house which was separated from the neighbouring house by an *anjong* (a kind of raised platform or erection between two houses), and from this circumstance he came to be universally called "Teungku Anjong." The same saint is named **Tunhar Anjung** in the spelling of the [[Three Sagis]]' letter to [[Penang]] of [[4 December]] [[1822]] — a Malay rendering that reflects the orthography of the chiefs' Malay scribe rather than Achehnese pronunciation.

## The Tomb and the Displacement of the Older Saints

Snouck Hurgronje writes: "The tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh is that of Teungku Anjong in Gampong Jawa. It is the last resting-place of a certain Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih, and has completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Cheh Abdora'oh|Teungku di Kuala]] alias Abdora'oh (Abdurra'uf of Singkel) which enjoyed the highest reputation in former times." The displacement of the older saint by the newer is a notable fact in the religious topography of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] devotion: it shows how the focus of popular veneration has shifted, in the course of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, from the great saints of the classical period to those of more recent memory. The sayyid's reputation as the great saint of Acheh is now so universally accepted that vows may be made to him in the same manner as to the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] himself or to the saint [[Mirah Sab]] (who lies buried at [[Nagore]] in [[British India]]). When a gift has been vowed to Teungku Anjong, the vow may be fulfilled by handing over the thing promised to a *teungku* (religious teacher) for his own use; all that the latter need do is recite over the gift the appropriate prayers.

A curious testimony to the rapid growth of his cult is the appearance of his tomb in the so-called "laws of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Meukuta Alam]]" — a body of Achehnese customary law traditionally ascribed to the great sultan of the early seventeenth century, but in fact containing much later material. Snouck Hurgronje remarks that "Teungku Anjong (†1782 A.D.) was not even born in the reign of Meukuta Alam (†1636 A.D.)," so that "how little reliance can be placed on Achehnese data in respect to the origin of such written laws" may be deduced from the very mention of his tomb in those laws. The cult of Teungku Anjong, in other words, has so completely captured the Achehnese imagination that his name has been read back, anachronistically, into the legislation of a sultan who died more than a century before his birth.

## The Annual *Kanduris*

According to the Achehnese tradition, Teungku Anjong died on the 14th of Ramadhan 1196 ([[August]] [[1782]]). Three principal annual *kanduris* (religious feasts) are held in the *deah* (prayer-house) at his tomb in [[Gampong Jawa]], on dates fixed by the Mohammedan calendar.

The first takes place on the 12th of *Mo'lot* — the actual birthday of the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] — when one or more buffaloes are slaughtered; besides those who assemble in the *deah* to partake of the feast, sundry *ulamas* have a share therein, and the festival is one of the great Achehnese observances of the year. The conjunction of his cult with the celebration of the Prophet's birth is itself significant, since the sayyids, as descendants of Mohammad, are the natural beneficiaries of the honours paid to his memory.

The second, called the **kanduri Teungku Anjong**, is held on the night before the 14th of *Puasa* (Ramadhan) and is more especially dedicated to the saint himself, being the anniversary of his death.

The third, on the 18th of *Rajab*, is held in honour of his consort. This consort, whose tomb stands close to his, is commonly known as **Aja Eseutiri** ("my lady the consort"). Her real name was [[Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman 'Aidid|Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman ʿAidid]], a daughter of a sayyid of the famous clan of ʿAidid; she appears to have died on the 18th of Rajab 1235 (May [[1820]]). The *kanduri Aja Eseutiri* resembles exactly the two others celebrated at the sacred tomb.

The annual cycle of feasts at his *deah* — *Mo'lot* in spring, the night before the 14th of *Puasa* in the fasting month, and the 18th of *Rajab* in midsummer — gives the Achehnese ritual year three of its principal fixed points, and his cult is interwoven with the wider Achehnese observance of the Prophet's birthday and the fast of Ramadhan.

## The Cult in Achehnese Ritual Life

The rite of the *kaoy*, or vow, is described by Snouck as one of the most characteristic Achehnese observances. It is a common custom for the father or mother to vow at the sick-bed of a child which has not yet been "brought down" — that is, has not yet made its first contact with the earth — that in the event of its recovery they will not merely bring it to the nearest holy tomb, but will take it on a pilgrimage to the resting-place of one of the great saints of Acheh, such as Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh or Sayyid Teungku Anjong. In such cases the child is escorted to the sacred spot by a great procession of men and women, and cattle are slaughtered and a feast given at the tomb. Where the vow includes the expression of an intention to head the procession with *geundrang*, the two drums or *geundrang* and the flute (*srune*) are marched in front.

The non-fulfilment of a vow is regarded by the Achehnese as apt to be attended by disastrous consequences. If a child suffers from persistent ulcers on its head, ears, or eyes, or is slow in recovering from an illness, or suffers for an unusual length of time from the effects of circumcision, people say: "That child has a *biinaran*" — which means either that a vow has been made in its behalf and remains unfulfilled, or that one of those feasts or ceremonies which are generally the subjects of vows should be held for its benefit. The pilgrimage to the tomb of Sayyid Teungku Anjong is one of the most solemn of the *biinaran* by which a parent can discharge such a debt of gratitude.

The veneration paid to Teungku Anjong's tomb is one of the chief manifestations of the saint-cult (*kramat*) of Acheh. His cult is supported by *waqf* endowments, and the slaughter of buffaloes at his *kanduri Mo'lot* places him, in the scale of popular honour, on a level with the great saints of the archipelago.

The settlement of the *Sayyid* colony in Gampong Java, where the saint's tomb is situated, is itself part of the larger history of the Hadrami diaspora in the Eastern Archipelago. The veneration paid to Sayyid Teungku Anjong as the most eminent of Achehnese saints reflects the growing prestige, in the nineteenth century, of the *Aidarus* and related Hadrami families throughout the Archipelago — a prestige reinforced by such living representatives as the celebrated [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir]] of [[Banda Acheh|Banda Acheh]].

## The Oath of the Three Sagis, 4 December 1822

The most consequential political attestation of the saint's cult is the oath sworn at his tomb on [[4 December]] [[1822]] by the chiefs of the [[Three Sagis]]. In that year, three years after the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]] and the [[Treaty of Pedir]] had attempted to seat [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] firmly on the throne of Acheh, the Three Sagis — the chiefs of the [[XXII Mukims]], [[XXV Mukims]] and [[XXVI Mukims]] who together ruled the capital — addressed a letter to the Governor of Penang ([[W. E. Phillips|W. E. Phillips]]) declaring that they had deposed Jauhar Alum and would never receive him back. The relevant passage runs:

> "As to the old Rajah we acquaint our friend that we will never have him for our Rajah more, for we have deposed him and sworn to it on the tomb of Tunhar Anjung of Kampong Java, and we never can alter. Moreover we acquaint our friend the Honble the Governor of Penang that we request his kindness to us not to examine strictly into the respectfulness of our address, for we are peasants and not well acquainted with Malay languages, but let our friend consider only the meaning of our letter."

The oath at the tomb of Teungku Anjong was, in other words, the Achehnese chiefs' formal and religiously binding act of deposition. By invoking the saint's tomb, the Three Sagis declared that they could not now, even if they wished, take back Sultan Jauhar Alum, "the old Rajah." The plea that they were "peasants" unversed in the polished Malay of formal correspondence was an apology for the roughness of their address, but the substance of the message was uncompromising. The Sagis went on to ask Phillips to order back their sovereign [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syful Allum]] (i.e., [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum]]) to Acheh, "so that we may be happy and our friend's vessels go to and from Acheen with safety whether on commerce or for amusement." The oath at the tomb of Teungku Anjong was the public-religious act by which the Sagis committed themselves to the rejection of Jauhar Alum and to the recognition of Saif ul-Alum as the new sultan.

[[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] cites the oath at Kampong Java in the broader context of the [[Panglima Polim's supremacy at Acheh|supremacy of Panglima Polim]] after the deposition of Jauhar Alum, and of the failure of the British attempt to seat Jauhar Alum firmly on the throne. A footnote in Van der Kemp adds, by way of parallel, that the Achehnese had also told [[Pické]] — referring to [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]'s interlocutor — that, when the English had asked who was king in Acheh, the answer was: "what is that to you?" The same robustly independent attitude underlies the invocation of Teungku Anjong's tomb.

## Significance

Teungku Anjong is the only named Achehnese religious figure (other than the sultans themselves) to be invoked in the documents printed by Van der Kemp; his tomb at Kampong Java is the only specifically Islamic sacred site in Banda Acheh mentioned in the 1900 article. The oath sworn there by the Three Sagis was a binding religious-political act: it placed the chiefs' deposition of Jauhar Alum under the sanction of a saintly intercession, and made it impossible — in the chiefs' own account — for them to retract. The episode demonstrates the autonomy of the Three Sagis from the British-imposed settlement of [[1819]], and the depth of the Achehnese political reorganisation that had overtaken the arrangements of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]] within three years of the Treaty of Pedir. The same tomb that anchored the Achehnese ritual year — *Mo'lot*, *Puasa*, *Rajab* — and drew the vows of parents on behalf of sick children, thus also anchored, in 1822, the Achehnese political rejection of an externally-imposed sovereign: a striking convergence of devotional and constitutional authority at a single saint's resting-place.
