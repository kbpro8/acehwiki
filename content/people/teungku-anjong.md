An Arab sayyid who died in [[1782]] and is the most revered saint in Acheh at the time of Hurgronje's writing, having somewhat eclipsed the older saint Abdurra'uf. His tomb attracts constant pilgrims; oaths sworn there are considered especially binding, and he is believed to have a crocodile as a guardian animal in Kuala Acheh.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih

**Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih** was a venerated deceased sayyid whose tomb, under the popular name **Teungku Anjong** in [[Gampong Jawa]], is — at the time of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s writing — "the tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh." He is mentioned in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* in the chapter on the sayyids, where Snouck Hurgronje describes the special veneration paid to the descendants of the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] in the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]].

Snouck Hurgronje writes: "The tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh is that of Teungku Anjong in Gampong Jawa. It is the last resting-place of a certain Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih, and has completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Cheh Abdora'oh|Teungku di Kuala]] alias Abdora'oh (Abdurra'uf of Singkel) which enjoyed the highest reputation in former times." The displacement of the older saint by the newer is a notable fact in the religious topography of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] devotion: it shows how the focus of popular veneration has shifted, in the course of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, from the great saints of the classical period to those of more recent memory.

The cult of Teungku Anjong is attested elsewhere in Vol. I. In the description of the religious calendar, Snouck Hurgronje notes that the great feast of the *Mo'lot* (the commemoration of the birth of the Prophet on the 12th of *Rabīʿ al-Awwal*) is marked in [[Acheh]] by the holding of "a specially great kanduri at the tomb of Teungku Anjong in Gampong Jawa." The annual feast at the tomb is the most conspicuous public observance attached to the saint's name; and the conjunction of his cult with the celebration of the Prophet's birth is itself significant, since the sayyids, as descendants of Mohammad, are the natural beneficiaries of the honours paid to his memory.

The Sayyid's name itself reveals his descent. **Abu Bakar** (the Achehnese form of the Arabic *Abū Bakr*) was the name of the first of the four Companions; the patronymic **bin Husain** shows that he traced his descent through the Prophet's grandson Husain, as all sayyids of the archipelago do; and **Bil-Faqih** — "of the jurist" — is the name of a celebrated Hadrami family, members of which settled in many parts of the archipelago. The Sayyid was thus an immigrant from the Hadramawt, or a descendant of recent Hadrami immigrants, who settled in [[Banda Acheh]] and there acquired, by the conjunction of his descent with the reputation of his personal piety, the supreme place in Achehnese veneration that his tomb now enjoys.

A curious testimony to the rapid growth of his cult is the appearance of his tomb in the so-called "laws of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Meukuta Alam]]" — a body of Achehnese customary law traditionally ascribed to the great sultan of the early seventeenth century, but in fact containing much later material. Snouck Hurgronje remarks that "Teungku Anjong (†1782 A.D.) was not even born in the reign of Meukuta Alam (†1636 A.D.)," so that "how little reliance can be placed on Achehnese data in respect to the origin of such written laws" may be deduced from the very mention of his tomb in those laws. The cult of Teungku Anjong, in other words, has so completely captured the Achehnese imagination that his name has been read back, anachronistically, into the legislation of a sultan who died more than a century before his birth.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sayyid Teungku Anjong

**Sayyid Teungku Anjong** — known in his full Arabic name as **Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih** — is the [[Sayyid|Sayyid]] saint whose tomb in [[Gampong Java|Gampong Java]] is, at the time Snouck Hurgronje wrote, "the tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh." His veneration had, by the close of the nineteenth century, completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Teungku di Kuala]] alias Abdora'oh (Abdurra'uf of Singkel), which had enjoyed the highest reputation in former times. He is one of the great saints of the Achehnese religious geography, invoked by name in the vows and pilgrimages of parents on behalf of sick children.

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Sayyid Teungku Anjong in two passages of *The Achehnese* Vol. I, both of which throw light on the manner of his veneration. In the first, he is identified as the Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih whose last resting-place has become the chief object of saintly pilgrimage in Acheh; in the second, he is named alongside Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh, the saint of [[Singkel|Singkel]], as one of the great saints of Acheh to whose tombs parents vow a pilgrimage in the event of the recovery of a sick child.

The rite of the kaoy, or vow, is described by Snouck as one of the most characteristic Achehnese observances. It is a common custom for the father or mother to vow at the sick-bed of a child which has not yet been "brought down" — that is, has not yet made its first contact with the earth — that in the event of its recovery they will not merely bring it to the nearest holy tomb, but will take it on a pilgrimage to the resting-place of one of the great saints of Acheh, such as Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh or Sayyid Teungku Anjong. In such cases the child is escorted to the sacred spot by a great procession of men and women, and cattle are slaughtered and a feast given at the tomb. Where the vow includes the expression of an intention to head the procession with *geundrang*, the two drums or *geundrang* and the flute (*srune*) are marched in front.

The non-fulfilment of a vow is regarded by the Achehnese as apt to be attended by disastrous consequences. If a child suffers from persistent ulcers on its head, ears, or eyes, or is slow in recovering from an illness, or suffers for an unusual length of time from the effects of circumcision, people say: "That child has a *biinaran*" — which means either that a vow has been made in its behalf and remains unfulfilled, or that one of those feasts or ceremonies which are generally the subjects of vows should be held for its benefit. The pilgrimage to the tomb of Sayyid Teungku Anjong is one of the most solemn of the *biinaran* by which a parent can discharge such a debt of gratitude.

The settlement of the *Sayyid* colony in Gampong Java, where the saint's tomb is situated, is itself part of the larger history of the Hadrami diaspora in the Eastern Archipelago. The veneration paid to Sayyid Teungku Anjong as the most eminent of Achehnese saints reflects the growing prestige, in the nineteenth century, of the *Aidarus* and related Hadrami families throughout the Archipelago — a prestige reinforced by such living representatives as the celebrated [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir]] of [[Banda Acheh|Banda Acheh]]. The popular devotion to the tomb of the Sayyid at Gampong Java gave the descendants of these immigrants a place in the Achehnese imagination not inferior to that of the older saints of the country.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teungku Anjong

**Teungku Anjong** is the popular name by which the Achehnese know **Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih**, a sayyid of the stock of Bilfaqih whose tomb in [[Gampong Jawa]] is, in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s phrase, "the tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh." His real name being known to the learned, the Achehnese in general remember him only by his dwelling-place: he had lived during his lifetime in a house which was separated from the neighbouring house by an *anjong* (a kind of raised platform or erection between two houses), and from this circumstance he came to be universally called "Teungku Anjong."

The tomb of Teungku Anjong has completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh|Teungku di Kuala]] (alias [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Abdora'oh]], i.e. Abdurra'uf of Singkel), which in former times enjoyed the highest reputation in [[Acheh]]. The sayyid's reputation as the great saint of Acheh is now so universally accepted that vows may be made to him in the same manner as to the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] himself or to the saint [[Mirah Sab]] (who lies buried at [[Nagore]] in [[British India]]). When a gift has been vowed to Teungku Anjong, the vow may be fulfilled by handing over the thing promised to a *teungku* for his own use; all that the latter need do is recite over the gift the appropriate prayers.

According to the Achehnese tradition, Teungku Anjong died on the 14th of Ramadhan 1196 (August [[1782]]). Three principal annual *kanduris* (religious feasts) are held in the *deah* (prayer-house) at his tomb in [[Gampong Jawa]], on dates fixed by the Mohammedan calendar. The first takes place on the 12th of Mo'lot — the actual birthday of the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] — when one or more buffaloes are slaughtered; besides those who assemble in the *deah* to partake of the feast, sundry *ulamas* have a share therein, and the festival is one of the great Achehnese observances of the year. The second, called the **kanduri Teungku Anjong**, is held on the night before the 14th of Puasa (Ramadhan) and is more especially dedicated to the saint himself, being the anniversary of his death. The third, on the 18th of Rajab, is held in honour of his consort.

This consort, whose tomb stands close to his, is commonly known as **Aja Eseutiri** ("my lady the consort"). Her real name was [[Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman 'Aidid|Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman ʿAidid]], a daughter of a sayyid of the famous clan of ʿAidid; she appears to have died on the 18th of Rajab 1235 (May [[1820]]). The *kanduri Aja Eseutiri* resembles exactly the two others celebrated at the sacred tomb.

The veneration paid to Teungku Anjong's tomb is one of the chief manifestations of the saint-cult (*kramat*) of Acheh. His cult is supported by *waqf* endowments, and the slaughter of buffaloes at his *kanduri Mo'lot* places him, in the scale of popular honour, on a level with the great saints of the archipelago. The annual cycle of feasts at his *deah* — Mo'lot in spring, the night before the 14th of Puasa in the fasting month, and the 18th of Rajab in midsummer — gives the Achehnese ritual year three of its principal fixed points, and his cult is interwoven with the wider Achehnese observance of the Prophet's birthday and the fast of Ramadhan.
