**Teungku Anjong** — in Kreemer's spelling **Teungkoe Andjöng** — was the popular Achehnese name of **Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih**, an Arab *sajet* (sayyid, descendant of the Prophet) of the Hadrami Bil-Faqih (Balfaqlh) family who died on the 14th of Ramadhan 1196 H ([[August]] [[1782]]) and whose tomb (*koeboe kramat*), under the popular name *Teungku Anjong* in [[Gampong Jawa|Gampöng Djawa]] (also [[Kampong Java|Gampong Java]]) in [[Banda Acheh]] near [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]], is — at the time of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s writing — "the tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh" and the most important pilgrimage site in Atjèh. His veneration had, by the close of the nineteenth century, completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh]] (alias Abdoerraoef van Singkel), which had enjoyed the highest reputation in former times. The tomb served in [[1822]] as the site of the formal oath by which the chiefs of the [[Three Sagis]] — the [[XXII Mukims]], [[XXV Mukims]] and [[XXVI Mukims]] — renounced the deposed [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and committed themselves, by a binding religious act, never to receive him back as their ruler.

## The Sayyid's Name and Descent

The Sayyid's name itself reveals his descent. **Abu Bakar** (the Achehnese form of the Arabic *Abū Bakr*) was the name of the first of the four Companions; the patronymic **bin Husain** shows that he traced his descent through the [[Husain|Prophet's grandson Husain]], as all sayyids of the archipelago do; and **Bil-Faqih** — "of the jurist" — is the name of a celebrated Hadrami family, members of which settled in many parts of the archipelago. The Sayyid was thus an immigrant from the Hadramawt, or a descendant of recent Hadrami immigrants, who settled in [[Banda Acheh]] and there acquired, by the conjunction of his descent with the reputation of his personal piety, the supreme place in Achehnese veneration that his tomb now enjoys.

His popular name, *Teungku Anjong*, has a humbler origin. His real name being known to the learned, the Achehnese in general remember him only by his dwelling-place: he had lived during his lifetime in a house which was separated from the neighbouring house by an *anjong* (a kind of raised platform or erection between two houses), and from this circumstance he came to be universally called "Teungku Anjong." The same saint is named **Tunhar Anjung** in the spelling of the [[Three Sagis]]' letter to [[Penang]] of [[4 December]] [[1822]] — a Malay rendering that reflects the orthography of the chiefs' Malay scribe rather than Achehnese pronunciation.

## The Tomb and the Displacement of the Older Saints

Snouck Hurgronje writes: "The tomb at present most highly honoured in Acheh is that of Teungku Anjong in Gampong Jawa. It is the last resting-place of a certain Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih, and has completely outshone the more ancient tomb of [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Teungku di Kuala]] alias Abdora'oh (Abdurra'uf of Singkel) which enjoyed the highest reputation in former times." The displacement of the older saint by the newer is a notable fact in the religious topography of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] devotion: it shows how the focus of popular veneration has shifted, in the course of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, from the great saints of the classical period to those of more recent memory. The sayyid's reputation as the great saint of Acheh is now so universally accepted that vows may be made to him in the same manner as to the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]] himself or to the saint [[Mirah Sab]] (who lies buried at [[Nagore]] in [[British India]]).

A curious testimony to the rapid growth of his cult is the appearance of his tomb in the so-called "laws of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Meukuta Alam]]" — a body of Achehnese customary law traditionally ascribed to the great sultan of the early seventeenth century, but in fact containing much later material. Snouck Hurgronje remarks that "Teungku Anjong (†1782 A.D.) was not even born in the reign of Meukuta Alam (†1636 A.D.)," so that "how little reliance can be placed on Achehnese data in respect to the origin of such written laws" may be deduced from the very mention of his tomb in those laws.

## The Annual *Kanduris*

According to the Achehnese tradition, Teungku Anjong died on the 14th of Ramadhan 1196 ([[August]] [[1782]]). Three principal annual *kanduris* / *kanoeri*'s (religious feasts / meals) are held in the *deah* (prayer-house) at his tomb in [[Gampong Jawa|Gampöng Djawa]], on dates fixed by the Mohammedan calendar:

1. On the **12th of *Mo'lot*** (the 12th of the 3rd Muslim month) — the actual birthday of the [[Mohammad (the Prophet)|Prophet]], commemorating both Teungku Anjong's birth and his death. One or more buffaloes are slaughtered; besides those who assemble in the *deah* to partake of the feast, sundry *ulamas* have a share therein, and the festival is one of the great Achehnese observances of the year.
2. The **kanduri Teungku Anjong**, on the night before the **14th of *Puasa*** (Ramadhan) — his death date, held on the evening before the 14th — more especially dedicated to the saint himself, being the anniversary of his death.
3. On the **18th of *Rajab*** — held in honour of his consort **Aja Eseutiri** ("my lady the consort", also called **Radja Istëri**). Her real name was [[Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman 'Aidid|Fatimah bint Abdarrhaman ʿAidid]], a daughter of a sayyid of the famous clan of ʿAidid; she appears to have died on the 18th of Rajab 1235 (May [[1820]]). The *kanduri Aja Eseutiri* resembles exactly the two others celebrated at the sacred tomb.

The annual cycle of feasts at his *deah* — *Mo'lot* in spring, the night before the 14th of *Puasa* in the fasting month, and the 18th of *Rajab* in midsummer — gives the Achehnese ritual year three of its principal fixed points, and his cult is interwoven with the wider Achehnese observance of the Prophet's birthday and the fast of Ramadhan.

## The Cult in Achehnese Ritual Life

The rite of the *kaoy* / *meukaöj* (vow) is described by Snouck as one of the most characteristic Achehnese observances. It is a common custom for the father or mother to vow at the sick-bed of a child which has not yet been "brought down" — that is, has not yet made its first contact with the earth — that in the event of its recovery they will not merely bring it to the nearest holy tomb, but will take it on a pilgrimage to the resting-place of one of the great saints of Acheh, such as Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh or Sayyid Teungku Anjong. In such cases the child is escorted to the sacred spot by a great procession of men and women, and cattle are slaughtered and a feast given at the tomb. Where the vow includes the expression of an intention to head the procession with *geundrang*, the two drums or *geundrang* and the flute (*srune*) are marched in front.

The non-fulfilment of a vow is regarded by the Achehnese as apt to be attended by disastrous consequences. If a child suffers from persistent ulcers on its head, ears, or eyes, or is slow in recovering from an illness, people say: "That child has a *biinaran*" — which means either that a vow has been made in its behalf and remains unfulfilled. The pilgrimage to the tomb of Sayyid Teungku Anjong is one of the most solemn of the *biinaran* by which a parent can discharge such a debt of gratitude.

## Oath-swearing

The grave of Teungkoe Andjöng was also a traditional place for oath-swearing. Heads from outside Groot-Atjèh who offered submission to the Dutch Gouvernement had to swear loyalty oaths at this grave. Other oath-swearing sites in Atjèh included the graves of **Nè' Bati** (in gampöng Lamba'ét, VI Moekim's), **Nènè' Teukoe Tjhi' Langsa** (at Langsa), **Toean di Taloe'** (at [[Tapa' Toean|Tapa' Toean]]), and the now-discredited **Teungkoe Meuntröë**.

## The Oath of the Three Sagis, 4 December 1822

The most consequential political attestation of the saint's cult is the oath sworn at his tomb on [[4 December]] [[1822]] by the chiefs of the [[Three Sagis]]. In that year, three years after the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]] and the [[Treaty of Pedir]] had attempted to seat [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] firmly on the throne of Acheh, the Three Sagis addressed a letter to the Governor of Penang ([[W. E. Phillips|W. E. Phillips]]) declaring that they had deposed Jauhar Alum and would never receive him back. The relevant passage runs:

> "As to the old Rajah we acquaint our friend that we will never have him for our Rajah more, for we have deposed him and sworn to it on the tomb of Tunhar Anjung of Kampong Java, and we never can alter."

The oath at the tomb of Teungku Anjong was, in other words, the Achehnese chiefs' formal and religiously binding act of deposition. By invoking the saint's tomb, the Three Sagis declared that they could not now, even if they wished, take back Sultan Jauhar Alum, "the old Rajah." [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] cites the oath at Kampong Java in the broader context of the [[Panglima Polim's supremacy at Acheh|supremacy of Panglima Polim]] after the deposition of Jauhar Alum.

## Significance

Teungku Anjong is the only named Achehnese religious figure (other than the sultans themselves) to be invoked in the documents printed by Van der Kemp; his tomb at Kampong Java is the only specifically Islamic sacred site in Banda Acheh mentioned in the 1900 article. The oath sworn there by the Three Sagis was a binding religious-political act: it placed the chiefs' deposition of Jauhar Alum under the sanction of a saintly intercession, and made it impossible — in the chiefs' own account — for them to retract. The same tomb that anchored the Achehnese ritual year — *Mo'lot*, *Puasa*, *Rajab* — and drew the vows of parents on behalf of sick children, thus also anchored, in 1822, the Achehnese political rejection of an externally-imposed sovereign. Kreemer's account, in turn, supplies the additional detail of the oath-swearing function — heads from outside Groot-Atjèh who offered submission to the Dutch Gouvernement had to swear loyalty oaths at this grave — that places the saint's tomb at the centre not only of the ritual year but of the colonial-administrative order as well.
