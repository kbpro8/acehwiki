**Hendrikus Colijn** ([[1869]]–[[1944]]) was a Dutch military officer, colonial administrator, and later Prime Minister of the Netherlands ([[1925]]–[[1926]], [[1933]]–[[1939]]). During the [[Aceh War]] and the early years of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s Governor-Generalship, he served as a *kapitein* (captain) and then as a roving advisor for the outer islands, playing a key role in shaping the pacification policy in [[Aceh|Atjèh]] and other territories — including the reception of the surrender of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] (*Panglima Polem*), the Sultan's own army commander, which marked the political closure of the Acehnese Sultanate. In [[1908]] he was appointed the first [[Adviseur voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen|Adviseur voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen]], the institutional architect of the post-[[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]] colonial order in the outer possessions; his portrait, alongside those of [[A. W. F. Idenburg]] and [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], appears as one of the three frontispiece figures of the [[Encyclopaedisch Bureau]]'s volume *De Buitenbezittingen 1904 tot 1914*. He was a close collaborator of Van Heutsz and, like [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], warned against the appointment of [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen]] as Governor of Aceh — a warning vindicated by the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] of [[1907]]–[[1908]]. In Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]) he is the ninth-named in [[Musket]]'s list of the Marechaussee commanders — known to the Acehnese as *Toean Colijn*.

## Military and early career in Aceh

Colijn first saw action in Aceh in the [[1890s]] and rose to become a *civiel gezaghebber* (civil administrator) in [[Lho' Seumawe|Lho' Seumawé]], where he demonstrated exceptional administrative ability and a talent for working with local chiefs. Van Heutsz, then Governor of Aceh, valued him highly for this combination of military experience and civil tact. He served in Aceh as a young officer from [[1894]] onwards, was decorated with the [[Militaire Willemsorde|M.W.O.]] 4e klas (4th class) for his part in the final phases of the Aceh War, and participated in the [[1904]] [[Alaslanden|Gajo- en Alaslanden]] campaign and the subsequent civil integration of the region. Under Van Heutsz's command, Colijn led pursuit operations against Acehnese resistance forces; he was present at the capture of [[Batêê Iliê]] in [[1901]]. He was noted for his energy and persistence in pursuing guerrilla forces, and for his specialisation in the *marechaussee* tactics developed during the Aceh campaign.

## The reception of Panglima Prang Polim

"Panglima Prang Polim, de vroegere legeraanvoerder van de Sultan, die zich tenslotte had gemeld bij kapitein Colijn" — Panglima Prang Polim, the former army commander of the Sultan, who had finally reported to Captain Colijn. The book *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* places the surrender of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Prang Polim]] — the Sultan's own commander-in-chief — to *kapitein Colijn* as the moment of political closure of the Acehnese Sultanate. Dames does not give a precise date, but the context of the [[Colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]] of [[1910]]–[[1911]] suggests that Polim's surrender preceded the colonne, providing the political condition for [[H. N. A. Swart|Generaal Swart]]'s request that the three *voornaamste Atjehse hoofden* (principal Acehnese heads) write the Tiriō Teungkoes urging submission. Colijn's reception of Polim was therefore a precondition of the letter, and so of the last act of the Aceh War.

A separate account, drawn from A. and H. Algra's *Dispereert Niet* (Deel XI, [[1946]]), records the surrender as occurring at [[Lhokseumawe]] in [[1903]] under the name *Panglima Polem* — an alternative spelling of the same commander's name. This event, while underreported in the Dutch press at the time, marked the effective end of organised Acehnese resistance in the conventional phase of the war.

The identification of this *kapitein Colijn* with the later Dutch Prime Minister is not made explicit in either *Oom Ambon* or *Dispereert Niet* — both authors simply write "Colijn" or "kapitein Colijn" — but is overwhelmingly probable. Colijn ([[1869]]–[[1944]]) served in Aceh as a young officer from [[1894]] onwards, was decorated (M.W.O. 4e klas) for his part in the final phases of the Aceh War, and was widely known in the Netherlands of the [[1940s]] and [[1950s]] as the former Prime Minister who had been a young officer in Aceh. Both Dames's and the Algras' readers would have recognised the name without explanation.

## The list of Marechaussee commanders

In Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]) Colijn is the ninth-named in [[Musket]]'s list of the famous fighters and Marechaussee commanders of the Aceh War: "Gilles Graafland, Van Daalen, Campioni, Christoffel, Van der Maaten, Scheepens, Watrin, Webb, Colijn, Geertsema Beckering, Darlang and the sergeants Nussy, Salowono, Pongoh … a colourful row." The names of these *Marechaussee* commanders, the book records, "rang through the Indies like carillon music," and their "stories of brave deeds, adventures, and peculiarities went the rounds, as if they were legends." Colijn is therefore one of the book's fellows of the old time: the Marechaussee commander — the later statesman — whose name was carillon music.

## Opposition to Van Daalen's appointment

In [[1904]], when Van Heutsz proposed Van Daalen as his successor, Colijn privately advised against it. In a letter to [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in June [[1904]] he wrote:

> "Van Daalen handhaaft schitterend zijn roem als uitmuntend colonnecommandant… Toch geloof ik, dat hij eenige noodzakelijke kwaliteiten mist voor die civiele betrekking."

(Van Daalen brilliantly maintains his fame as an excellent column commander… Yet I believe that he lacks some necessary qualities for that civil position.)

He favoured [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]] as a more suitable candidate, noting that Swart was "goed verstand, vlot en krachtig werker" (good sense, fluent and powerful worker) with a talent for handling people. He conceded, however, that Van Daalen's seniority and experience made him the inevitable choice, and expressed concern that Van Daalen's authoritarian style would alienate the Acehnese chiefs and undermine the pacification policy. The subsequent Atjéh-enquête vindicated this assessment entirely.

## Advisor for the outer islands and first Adviseur

After his service on the Aceh front, Colijn served as Van Heutsz's roving advisor, visiting [[Tapanoeli|Tapanoeli]], [[Djambi|Djambi]], [[Sumatra's East Coast|Sumatra's East Coast]], and other regions to propose administrative reforms. His reports were praised for their conciseness and practical recommendations, and he contributed significantly to the expansion of Dutch control in the outer islands, laying the groundwork for the later decentralisation and consolidation of the [[Dutch East Indies]]. In [[1908]], when the [[Bureau voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen|Bureau voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen]] was formally established within the [[Departement van Binnenlandsch Bestuur|Department of the Interior]], Colijn was appointed its first [[Adviseur voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen|Adviseur]] — the inaugural holder of an office that would coordinate colonial administrative policy across all the outer possessions, with two Adjunct-Adviseurs under him and supervisory authority over the [[Encyclopaedisch Bureau|Encyclopaedisch Bureau]].

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Colijn as first Adviseur

In the Encyclopaedisch Bureau's account, Colijn is identified as "the first Adviseur for the Administrative Affairs of the Buitenbezittingen" — the inaugural holder of the office created in [[1908]] to provide institutional guidance for the post-[[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]] administration of the outer possessions. The Bureau notes that all three frontispiece figures — [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], [[A. W. F. Idenburg|Idenburg]], and Colijn — "granted permission for the inclusion of their likenesses" in the volume, which the Bureau frames as a tribute to the three men "whose names are so closely bound up with the preparation and execution of the measures that have led to the enormous progress of the Buitenbezittingen" in the [[1904]]–[[1914]] decade.

The portrait caption identifies Colijn at the time of publication ([[1917]]) as "De eerste Adviseur voor de Bestuurszaken der Buitenbezittingen; Oud-Minister van Oorlog; Lid van de Eerste Kamer der Staten-Generaal" — that is, as having already ascended from the colonial advisory role to the highest political offices in the Netherlands. His career thus illustrates the direct pipeline between the Atjeh campaign and Dutch metropolitan politics in the early twentieth century.

## Later political career

Colijn left the colonial service in [[1909]] to enter Dutch politics. He became a member of parliament, Minister of Colonies ([[1911]]–[[1912]]), and eventually Prime Minister, serving multiple terms in the [[1920s]] and [[1930s]]. He remained a defender of Van Heutsz's legacy and, in his later writings, credited Van Heutsz with transforming Dutch colonial policy from exploitation to ethical governance. His political career ended when the Netherlands was occupied during the Second World War; he died in German captivity in [[1944]].

## Significance

Colijn's combination of military experience, administrative skill, and political acumen made him one of the most influential figures in the transition from the Aceh War to the era of consolidation and ethical governance. His early warnings about Van Daalen were vindicated by the Atjéh-enquête, and his subsequent political career ensured that the principles of the "ethical policy" were carried forward into the twentieth century. In the narrative of *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.*, his reception of the Sultan's own army commander is the moment at which the formal Acehnese political establishment passed under Dutch authority — the commander of the Sultan's own army came in to a young Dutch *kapitein* who would, decades later, become the Prime Minister of the colonial power. In the wider frame of Merac's *Oost-Indisch Journael*, the same man is one of the Marechaussee commanders whose name "rang through the Indies like carillon music" — the only one of Musket's roll-call whose colonial career carried him all the way to the Prime Ministership of the Netherlands.
