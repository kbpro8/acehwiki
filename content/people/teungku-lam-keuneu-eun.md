**Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun**, popularly known as "the pepper saint," is an Achehnese saint (Teungku) whose tomb in the gampong of the same name in the [[XXII Mukims of Panglima Poleem|IX Mukims]] has long been revered as sacred. According to Achehnese tradition the pepper-plant is supposed to have originated in Acheh through his miraculous power, and the offerings made at his grave form one of the more striking examples of the way in which popular hagiology in Acheh clusters around local worthies whose historic personality is no longer recoverable.

## Association with Teungku Kutakarang

The cult of Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun received a notable stimulus from [[Teungku Kutakarang]], the active and eccentric rival of the great ulama [[Teungku Tiro]] during the early years of the [[Acheh War]]. Teungku Kutakarang, whose own residence lay in the neighbourhood of the saint's tomb, delighted to call himself the servant (glangwang) of that shrine. By attaching himself in this way to a long-established kramat, he at once legitimised his own presence in the district and placed himself in a position to receive the numerous gifts which pious visitors dedicated to the saint. Snouck Hurgronje explicitly notes that this was one of the pains to which Kutakarang went in order to enhance the repute of the "pepper saint."

## The Pepper Legend

The legend of the pepper-plant's origin is treated more fully in connection with another saint, [[Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun]], from whose kapok seed the first pepper-vines are also said to have sprung. The two traditions evidently point to a single body of folklore attached to the IX Mukims, in which the miraculous introduction of so profitable a crop was attributed to one or more local holy men. Such eponymous saints, named from the gampong in which their tombs stand, are a common feature of the Achehnese religious landscape.

## Veneration

The kanduri bungong lada ("pepper-blossom feast"), held annually on the East and West Coasts when the pepper blossoms, is the most tangible surviving memorial of this cycle of legend. Although the feast is conducted privately in each planter's house rather than as a public gathering at the tomb, the underlying belief — that the crop exists by virtue of a saint's barakah — keeps the memory of Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun alive in the religious consciousness of the pepper-growing districts.
