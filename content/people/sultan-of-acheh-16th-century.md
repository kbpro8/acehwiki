The **Sultan of Acheh in the sixteenth century** is the unnamed Achehnese ruler to whom the popular legend of the cannon **Lada Sichupa'** ("a *chupa'* of pepper") attributes the opening of relations between [[Acheh|Acheh]] and the [[Sultan of Turkey (16th century)|Sultan of Turkey]] at [[Stambul|Stambul]]. The story is preserved in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the popular explanation of the Achehnese custom of universally observing the feast of the Prophet's birth (the Mo'lot, or Maulud), which was regarded by the people of Acheh as specially binding upon their country.

According to the tradition, in the course of the sixteenth century, when Acheh began to grow powerful, one of the Sultans — "we know not which," Snouck Hurgronje carefully notes — thought that the time had come to bring his kingdom to the notice of the lord of all believers, the *Raja Rom*, i.e. the Sultan of Turkey. He caused one of his biggest ships to be laden with pepper, the principal product of the country, as an offering expressive of homage to the supreme lord. Some say that he made the journey himself; according to others he sent an embassy of wealthy chiefs.

On their arrival at Stambul the Achehnese ambassadors found that no one had ever heard of the existence of Acheh. The officials of the Ottoman court refused for a year or two to ask an audience for them, and the envoys were gradually compelled to sell their cargo of pepper to supply themselves with the necessaries of life. At last the Sultan, returning one Friday from the mosque to his palace, espied the Achehnese among the crowd of respectful spectators; struck by their peculiar dress, he enquired whence they came and what had brought them to Constantinople.

The episode gave rise to two enduring monuments of the legendary connexion between Acheh and the Sublime Porte. The first was the great cannon **Lada Sichupa'**, so named from the single *chupa'* (a small measure) of pepper which was all that remained of the original cargo when the ambassadors were at last admitted to the Sultan's presence. The cannon, before the coming of the Dutch, formed part of the defences of the [[Dalam]]. The second was the colony of skilled artisans — said to have been drawn from Syria — whom the Sultan sent to instruct the Achehnese in various arts hitherto unknown to them, and who, settling in a gampong close to the Dalam, gave their new home the name of [[Bitay|Bitay]], the Achehnese pronunciation of *Betal*, an abbreviation of *Betal-makdis*, Jerusalem.

The Sultan of Turkey, considering the great length of the journey, declared it unbefitting to bind his new vassal to the regular despatch of embassies or tribute. In place of yearly payments, he enjoined the universal observance of the feast of Mohammad's birth: "Let there be no village in Acheh where the inhabitants do not publicly hold a Mo'lot feast — that shall be your tribute to the lord of the Faithful." This legend was long received by the Achehnese as the historical explanation of the strictness with which the Mo'lot was observed throughout their country, the headman of a gampong who failed to make provision for the feast being fined by the uleebalang for neglect of duty. The story, though legendary in detail, has a historical core: the Achehnese sultanate of the sixteenth century did in fact exchange embassies with the Ottoman Porte and did in fact receive from Turkey both cannon and instructors in their use.
