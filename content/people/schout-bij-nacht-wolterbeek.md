**Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek** was a Dutch naval officer holding the rank of *schout-bij-nacht* (counter-admiral / rear-admiral) who served as one of the Netherlands Indies' principal commissioners in the Malay Archipelago in the years immediately following the restoration of Dutch rule after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)]]. He is best known in the present context for having negotiated the Dutch treaty with the sultan of [[Riouw]] in [[1818]], a diplomatic stroke that the British at [[Penang]] and in [[Bengal|Bengal]] believed had closed off further English expansion in the archipelago — only for [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] to demonstrate the contrary by founding [[Singapore]] a few months later.

## The Riouw Treaty and the Farquhar Rebuff

The story of Wolterbeek's role in the events of 1818–1819 is told at length by P. H. van der Kemp in his article of 1900. Writing from [[Bencoolen]] and Penang, Raffles had become convinced that the Dutch were consolidating their hold over the entire archipelago and that British interests — above all the unimpeded China trade through the [[Straits of Malacca]] — were being threatened. Two Dutch moves in particular inflamed his suspicions: the rejection of [[Major William Farquhar|Major Farquhar]] from [[Pontianak]] on [[Borneo]]'s west coast by the Dutch commissioner [[Van Boekholz|Van Boeckholz]], and the destruction by Wolterbeek of Farquhar's freshly-concluded treaty with the sultan of Riouw. As Van der Kemp puts it, Raffles received word that "Farquhar's Riouw treaty was cancelled by Wolterbeek." The Bengal government at [[Calcutta]] thereupon wrote to London on 14 January [[1819]] expressing the hope that "we may be in time to establish our political influence at Acheh to the exclusion of that of the Dutch or other foreign powers."

## Thyssen's Confidential Reports

Wolterbeek was stationed at [[Batavia]] and from there corresponded with Dutch officials throughout the archipelago. The most important of these correspondents was [[Timmerman Thyssen|Governor Thyssen]] of [[Malacca]], who addressed him on 16 March 1819 in a long private letter (printed as Bijlage 4 of Van der Kemp's article) detailing Raffles' movements and warning of suspected British designs on the [[Karimon Islands]] and on the kingdom of [[Acheh]] itself. Thyssen's letter reported disquieting rumours — that two ships with three hundred troops had left Penang for the Karimons, that Raffles intended to sail for Acheh with three more ships and four hundred men, and that an attack on [[Lingga|Lingen]] might follow. Wolterbeek replied on 17 September 1819 sympathising with the loss of Lieutenant [[Lieutenant Marx Bouwens|Bouwens]] of the Wilhelmina, whose murder he lamented "beside the loss of men and vessel, had he 50,000 Spanish dollars on board and an interesting commission to Calcutta."

## Earlier Ruffles and the Governor-General

Wolterbeek had been corresponding with Thyssen on Raffles' activities since at least the previous December: on 5 December 1818 Thyssen had sent him a confidential letter communicating rumours of British plans for an attack on Malacca, which he nonetheless believed could be withstood thanks also to some 4,000 men under the commies [[Kraal]]. On 9 January 1819, [[Godert Alexander Gerard Philip van der Capellen|Governor-General Van der Capellen]] wrote privately from Buitenzorg to Wolterbeek at Batavia, expressing displeasure at the conduct of certain Malacca officials and adding the firm injunction that "T[hissen] must stand firm. Every Government in India in general must do so." Van der Kemp pointedly asks whether Van der Capellen himself always put this maxim into practice.

## Assessment

Wolterbeek's significance in Van der Kemp's narrative is that of the senior Dutch naval officer whose commission at Riouw in 1818 was widely regarded on the Dutch side as the seal upon their supremacy in the archipelago — and who therefore had the painful task of receiving the news, less than a year later, that Raffles had nonetheless managed to plant a British flag at Singapore, that the [[Wilhelmina mutiny and murder of Bouwens|Wilhelmina]] had been lost with all hands to a Malay mutiny, and that the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé]] was harbouring the mutineers. His correspondence with Thyssen and with the Governor-General remains one of the principal Dutch primary sources for these events.
