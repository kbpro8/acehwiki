**Tuanku Muhamat Dawot** — in Kreemer's spelling **Moehamat Dawöt** (also **Mohammad Daud** / **Muhammad Dawud**), in full **Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan**, also written **Sultan Muhamat Dawot Shah** — was the last pretender to the sultanate of [[Acheh|Atjèh]]. Proclaimed sultan as a child in [[1878]] (1296 H, as inscribed on his [[Sarakata|ninefold seal]]) on the death of [[Sultan Mahmut Shah|Sultan Mahmut Shah]] shortly after the [[Loss of the Dalam|loss of the Dalam]] / [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|fall of the kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]], he was raised under the guardianship of [[Tuanku Asem|Tuanku Asem]] (Hashim) and maintained for more than two decades a wandering "court" at [[Keumala|Keumala]] and elsewhere in Pidië territory. He finally submitted to the Dutch Government in [[1903]] — effectively ending the Atjèh sultanate — and was banished in [[1907]]. For his formal style and letters patent, see [[Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan]].

## Earlier career as Pakèh of Pidië

Before becoming pretender Sultan, Moehamat Dawöt bore the title **T. Radja Pakèh Moehamat Dawöt** and served as Pakèh (head) of [[Pidië|Pidië]] from [[1895]] to [[1896]]. He was the sixth in the line of Pakèh's of Pidië, succeeding T. Radja Pakèh Soelöjman ([[1879]]–[[1895]]). His tenure as Pakèh was brief; he was succeeded by T. Radja Pakèh Moehamat Ali, who came under the voogdij (guardianship) of his uncle in [[1896]] and became self-governing after [[1914]].

## Election as a child

When [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]] died shortly after the loss of the Dalam, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] observes that "it was not thought necessary even in so perilous a crisis to select from among the candidates for the succession to the throne a man before all things. They contented themselves with a child, Tuanku Muhamat Dawot." His guardian Tuanku Asem (Hashim), though "a man of royal blood most bitterly hostile to the Dutch Government," preferred to watch the contest with interest from a distance rather than himself to take a serious share in it, much less to enter the lists as a leader. The "court," once it had found a safe place of refuge in [[Pidie|Pidie]] territory, far removed from the theatre of the conflict, resumed the condition it had been in before the war — only the scene being changed.

After the [[Kraton Atjeh|kraton]] fell to Dutch forces under [[Jan van Swieten|generaal Van Swieten]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]], Moehamat Dawöt established himself at [[Keumala|Keumala]] in Pidië as the pretender Sultan. From there he issued *sarakata*'s (royal charters) in [[1887]]–[[1888]] to Gajö *moefi*'s (religious scholars) who paid him homage, attempting to extend his religious-political authority into the [[Gajöland|Gajöland]].

## Character and conduct

The young sultan, gradually emerging from childhood, soon showed "that he aspired to something very different from sharing the weal or woe of his country as leader of the contest against the Gompeuni." True to the traditions of his house, he sought and still seeks diversion, writes Snouck, "in lawful and unlawful love, drink, fiddling, fights of animals, gambling and the pursuit of elephant and deer." His letters to the Achehnese chiefs still always commence with the solemn admonition continually to remember "Allah, his Messenger, the departed sovereigns and ourselves"; and this last reminder takes tangible form in the latter portion of these letters in a request to forward a certain sum of money or some costly merchandise. In the Introduction, Snouck sums him up as "a nonentity in a political sense … in a position neither to do the Dutch much harm nor to help his adherents," however much he enjoyed "the homage mingled with fear that natives are apt to give to the descendants of their tyrants." Kreemer adds that he was known as a great patron of cricket-fighting.

## Letters patent and the chab sikureu'eng

The sultan's edicts continued to be issued in the old form. The [[Letters patent with the ninefold seal to Panglima Meuseugit Raya|letters patent with the ninefold seal]] granted to the [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] on 4 Rabīʿ al-awwal 1307 H (28 October [[1889]]) is the example translated in full in Vol. I, and is reproduced as the type of its class. Other edicts confirmed [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] in his religious authority over Great Acheh — though the "court" did so "cautiously enough," with a deed of appointment which in appearance made no inroad on the rights of the adat-chiefs — and confirmed [[Teuku Uma]] as *amīr al-baḥr* ("chief of the sea"), with a mental reservation that the title "absolutely excluded the highest authority on land." Of all these documents Snouck remarks that they are "just as solemn and pious in form as before, but of equally little consequence in practice, as they simply imprint the *chab sikureu'eng* on what has been already won by inheritance or by the *chab limong*" — the five-fold seal, i.e. the hand as the symbol of actual power. Even [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri|Teungku Tiro]] paid visits to his court at Keumala.

## Family connections

Moehamat Dawöt was connected through his daughter **Teungkoe Djam** to **Pö Tjoet Meurah** (Tjout Nja' Meurah), a prominent woman raised at the kraton court whose aunt had married Sultan [[Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah|Mantsoer Sjah]] ([[1838]]–[[1870]]). Pö Tjoet Meurah first married Sultan [[Sultan Ala ad-dïn Mahmoed Sjah|Mahmoed Sjah]], then Toeankoe Mahmoed (brother of [[Tuanku Asem|Toeankoe Asem]]). Pö Tjoet Meurah was the daughter of Teukoe Pakèh Dalam (former Oelèëbalang of XII Moekim's Pidië). The family network centred on Keumala preserved a vestige of royal authority even after the sultanate's formal end.

## Submission and banishment

The pretender to the sultanate was compelled, owing to a quarrel with the local chiefs, to break up his "court" at Keumala and to settle first in one place and then in another. For the last three years ([[1896]]–[[1899]]) — that is, since the Dutch began to deal seriously with the government of Great Acheh and the [[Subjugation of Keumala|subjugation]] of its dependencies — the pretender was obliged to lead a wandering life, seeking safety by constantly shifting his abode and finding his asylum as a rule in the North-coast districts. In the end, as Snouck records in the Introduction, "the chiefs of dependent states, the members of the Sultan's family, and finally their head himself who in [[1878]] as a child had been proclaimed Pretender-Sultan, submitted almost without exception."

Moehamat Dawöt submitted unconditionally to Dutch authority in January [[1903]], an event Kreemer treats as the effective end of the Atjèh sultanate. He was subsequently banished in [[1907]].

## Significance

Tuanku Muhamat Dawot is significant in the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Atjèh]] history as the last pretender to the sultanate — the figure under whom the shadow court of Keumala finally submitted to the Dutch in [[1903]] and was banished in [[1907]]. Snouck Hurgronje's portrait of the man — "a nonentity in a political sense … in a position neither to do the Dutch much harm nor to help his adherents" — and of his court — which "as soon as it had found a safe place of refuge in Pidie territory, far removed from the theatre of the conflict, resumed the condition it had been in before the war, the scene alone being changed" — together give the measure of the late sultanate's abdication of responsibility. Kreemer's account, in turn, supplies the institutional detail of Moehamat Dawöt's earlier career as Pakèh of Pidië ([[1895]]–[[1896]]), the *sarakata*'s he issued to Gajö *moefi*'s in [[1887]]–[[1888]], and the family network — through his daughter Teungkoe Djam, Pö Tjoet Meurah, and the lines of Sultan Mantsoer Sjah and Sultan Mahmoed Sjah — that bound the Keumala court to the last ruling Sultan and the older royal house.
