**Tadjoel-Alam Safijatoeddin** (Tadj al-alam Safïat ad-dïn Sjah, reigned [[1641]]–[[1675]]) was the first of the four female rulers (*vrouwenregeering*) of [[Aceh|Atjèh]], whose accession in [[1641]] Kreemer identifies as "the first step of Atjèh's decline" from its zenith under [[Sultan Iskandar Moeda|Iskandar Moeda]]. Her ninefold seal (*[[Tjab sikoereuëng|tjab sikoereuëng]]*) is reproduced on the cover of Kreemer's *Atjèh* (1922–23).

## Account from *Atjèh* by J. Kreemer (Deel II)

Tadjoel-Alam Safijatoeddin was the widow of Sultan [[Iskandar Thani|Iskandar Thanï]] ([[1636]]–[[1641]]) and daughter of [[Sultan Iskandar Moeda|Iskandar Moeda]]. Upon the death of Iskandar Thanï in [[1641]], she acceded to the throne as the first female ruler — the beginning of the *vrouwenregeering* (female rule) that would last until [[1699]].

## The vrouwenregeering and the end of the havenkoningen regime

The beginning of female rule in [[1641]] marked, for Kreemer, the end of the *havenkoningen* ("harbour-kings") regime and the loss of the Sultan's trade monopoly (*alleenhandel*). Under the female rulers the Sultan no longer possessed the sole right of trade but "moest zich tevreden stellen met de opbrengst van bij in- en uitvoer geheven rechten" — "had to be content with the proceeds of import/export duties." Since wealth determined the Sultan's internal influence as well, the position of the female rulers could no longer equal that of their glorious predecessors.

The numismatic evidence for the *vrouwenregeering* comes from the *deureuham* coins of the period: coins from her reign and that of her successors show no sultan's name but only the kalima and the year in Arabic.

## Relations with the VOC

The relations between the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] and Atjèh during this period were marked by Dutch commercial advantage. The VOC obtained so many privileges on the Malay peninsula and on Sumatra's West Coast that Tadjoel-Alam Safijatoeddin once complained to a Dutch envoy: "ick en weet niet, wat ik nu de Hollanders voortaen al meer zal geven" — "I do not know what more I can give the Dutch hereafter." The Dutch believed they had Atjèh "genoeghsaem t' onser devotie" — "virtually at our devotion" — but in reality received "in plaetse van thin en peper slechts schone woorden en een vriendelijck gelaet" — "in place of tin and pepper only fair words and a friendly face."

In [[1648]] the Governor-General and Raden decided to close the VOC offices in Atjèh and Pera, and around [[1660]], when the Dutch settled on Sumatra's Westkust, the post in Atjèh was permanently closed.

## The ninefold seal

The *tjab sikoereuëng* (ninefold seal) of Tadjoel-Alam Safijatoeddin — the Sultan's ninefold seal (*negen-stempel*) — is reproduced on the cover of Kreemer's work. The seal's connection to the Mughol emperor Djahangir, who is said to have given it to Sultan Iskandar Moeda, made it a symbol of Atjèh's golden age; its use by the first queen demonstrates the continuity of royal legitimacy through the female succession.
