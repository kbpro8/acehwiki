## Lead

J. de Groot was the *Hoofdambtenaar voor de reorganisatie van het Bestuurswezen* (Chief Official for the Reorganization of the Governance System), serving directly under *Regeeringscommissaris* [[S. de Graaff]] in the Netherlands East Indies. In this capacity, De Groot participated in the series of *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) across Sumatra in [[1913]] and [[1914]] that formed the basis for the *hervorming van het bestuurswezen* (reform of the administrative system), contributing observations and assessments that drew on both his prior research into the territories under discussion and his experience of the earlier Java meetings. As De Graaff's principal subordinate in the reorganization effort, De Groot served as a critical intermediary between the *Regeeringscommissaris*'s broad reform agenda and the specific local knowledge required to adapt that agenda to the diverse conditions of Sumatra's various residencies and governorates.

## Role in the Reorganization

As *Hoofdambtenaar*, De Groot was responsible for supporting De Graaff in the extensive preparatory work that undergirded the *bestuursreorganisatie*, including the collection and analysis of information about the administrative conditions in each territory to be reorganized. His presence at the *bestuursvergaderingen* was not merely that of a recorder or assistant; De Groot actively participated in the discussions, offering informed observations that frequently drew on research he had conducted before the meetings or on his comparative experience of the reform process as it had unfolded in other parts of the archipelago. His contributions at the Palembang, Medan, and [[Koeta-Radja]] meetings reflected a working familiarity with the governance challenges of the territories under discussion, as well as an awareness of how the reform proposals were being received by local officials elsewhere—a perspective that proved valuable in contextualizing the concerns and suggestions raised during the Sumatran deliberations. De Groot's dual role as both a support to De Graaff and an independent contributor to the discussions made him an important figure in the practical execution of the reorganization, bridging the gap between the strategic direction set by the *Regeeringscommissaris* and the operational details that would determine whether the reform could be successfully implemented.

## Views on Atjeh-Oostkust Merger

At the Palembang meeting on [[12 August]] [[1913]], De Groot revealed that he had previously considered the *samenvoeging* (combination) of [[Atjeh]] with Sumatra's Oostkust as a potential difficulty, but after discussion with an *Assistent-Resident* (Assistant Resident) familiar with the area, he was told that no objections would exist and that the [[Gouverneur van Atjeh]] would not oppose the merger. This exchange was significant because it addressed one of the key concerns raised by Controleur [[J. Tideman]] about the feasibility of combining territories with fundamentally different governance structures and characteristics. De Groot's willingness to revise his initial assessment on the basis of local consultation reflected the empirical approach that characterized the reorganization process, in which the theoretical difficulties of administrative combinations were weighed against the practical judgments of officials with direct experience in the affected territories. However, the reliability of the *Assistent-Resident*'s assurance that the *Gouverneur van Atjeh* would not object to the merger would later be tested by the more nuanced and cautious position articulated by Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meetings, where Swart made clear that Atjeh's incorporation into a larger administrative unit would require significant modifications to the standard reform model.

## Atjeh and Native Official Training

At the Medan meeting on [[14 January]] [[1914]], De Groot noted that the future *Inlandsche ambtenaren* (Native officials) in the *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* (directly ruled territory) in Atjeh and Tapanoeli would also fall under the proposed training plans, expanding the scope of the *opleiding* (training) provisions beyond what had been initially contemplated for the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]]. This observation was relevant to the broader discussion about the development of *Inlandsche bestuurskrachten* (Native administrative forces), as it ensured that the training component of the reorganization would extend to those parts of Atjeh that were under direct Dutch rule rather than being governed through *Inlandsche Zelfbesturen* (Native Self-Governments). De Groot also offered a broader comparative observation drawn from the earlier Java meetings, noting that *ambtenaren* (officials) at those sessions had often wished to go further in reorganization than intended, sometimes seeing no issue with changing almost everything. This observation provided important context for the Sumatran discussions, serving as a cautionary note about the tendency of local officials to embrace sweeping changes that might exceed the carefully calibrated scope of the reform proposals—a tendency that De Graaff would need to manage as the reorganization proceeded.

## Significance

J. de Groot's contributions to the *bestuursvergaderingen* illustrate the important but often understated role of subordinate officials in the colonial administrative reform process. While the *Regeeringscommissaris* set the strategic direction and the local governors and residents provided the detailed knowledge of their territories, officials like De Groot served as the connective tissue of the reorganization, bringing comparative perspectives, prior research, and practical experience to bear on the specific questions at hand. His observations about the [[Atjeh-Sumatra's Oostkust merger proposal]] and the training of native officials in Atjeh's *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* contributed directly to the deliberations that shaped the final administrative structure of the proposed [[Gouvernement Noord-Sumatra]], ensuring that the reform plan took account of Atjeh's distinctive conditions while still incorporating the territory into the broader reorganization framework. De Groot's comparative observation about the Java meetings also provides a valuable insight into the dynamics of the reform process itself, revealing the tension between local enthusiasm for change and the central government's more cautious and incremental approach to administrative restructuring.
