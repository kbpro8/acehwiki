**G. F. V. Gosenson** — known from his rank as **Colonel Gosenson** (Dutch: *Kolonel G.F.V. Gosenson*) — was a colonel of the [[K.N.I.L.|Netherlands Indies Army]] infantry who served as **Territoriaal Commandant van Atjeh** (Territorial Commander of Aceh) from [[31 May]] [[1936]] until his capture by the Japanese on [[28 March]] [[1942]], and in that capacity was the senior Dutch military commander in [[Aceh|Aceh]] throughout the run-up to and the opening phase of the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of North Sumatra]]. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* characterises Gosenson as "the type of the Atjèh officer of the old stamp, a master in the personal political game which characterized old Atjèh," who "spoke the country's language fluently"; in the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account of the Atjeh campaign (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), he is the central command figure of the pre-invasion and invasion phases — the officer under whom the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussee]] divisions and the four garrison battalions were steadily reduced in [[1940]]–[[1941]], and under whom the Dutch response to the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] sabotage and the Japanese landings of [[12 March]] [[1942]] was mounted.

## Appointment and Character

Gosenson was appointed Territoriaal Commandant of Aceh and Sumatra's East Coast on [[31 May]] [[1936]]. He was, Piekaar records, "just as vigorous and impulsive as Resident [[Pauw|Pauw]] was contemplative" — the two together being the highest Dutch authorities in the residency, whose contrasting characters shaped the response to the Japanese threat. His "thorough linguistic knowledge, as well as his great joviality, enabled him to maintain excellent personal relations with the native chiefs" — the personal political game that characterised "old Atjèh." He was Knight of the [[Order of Orange-Nassau|Order of Orange-Nassau]] 4th class and bearer of the honorary insignia for important military operations in Atjèh in the years [[1911]]–[[1914]] and on Atjèh's West Coast in [[1925]]–[[1927]] — that is, during the [[Bakongan uprising|Bakóngan uprising]]; he served for "a long series of years" as chairman of the examination committee for the Atjèh language.

## Pre-war Command in Aceh

Gosenson had held the *Territoriaal Commandant* post since [[1936]], and devoted particular attention to the readiness and training of his troops. His command embraced not only Aceh but, beyond the Atjeh boundary, the troops of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra's Oostkust]] ([[Pangkalanbrandan|Pangkalanbrandan]], [[Pangkalansoesoe|Pangkalansoesoe]] and [[Medan|Medan]]) — a footprint that placed him astride the entire northern approach to Sumatra. The staff history credits him with continuing the intensive patrolling that had maintained Dutch authority since the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]]: all brigades were required to carry out intensive multi-day patrols of eight to ten days each month, principally as a show of force.

## The Hollowing-out of the Atjeh Garrison

When the [[4de Garnizoensbataljon|4de Garnizoensbataljon (the "Mobiele Reserve")]] — the formation raised in [[1928]] after the [[Bakongan uprising|great Bakongan uprising]] of [[1925]]–[[1927]] — was dissolved for reinforcement of Java and Malakka in [[1940]], and the detachments of the 1ste, 2de and 3de Garnizoensbataljons at [[Bireuën|Bireuën]], [[Langsa|Langsa]], [[Kotatjane|Kotatjane]], [[Tapatoean|Tapatoean]], [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]], [[Tjalang|Tjalang]], [[Lam No|Lam No]], [[Blang Pidië|Blang Pidië]], [[Padang Tiji|Padang Tiji]], [[Meureudoe|Meureudoe]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] and [[Boeloe Blang Ara|Boeloe Blang Ara]] were progressively run down through [[1940]]–[[1941]], Gosenson protested but complied. The staff history notes the warning from the military side that, when the perpetrators could not be seized immediately, they quickly gathered followers around them — a warning vindicated by the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] rising of February [[1942]].

## Limits of Understanding

The essence of the new political-religious movements that emerged in Atjèh in the late 1930s — the [[Poesa|Poesa]] and the related currents around [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]] — "however, remained foreign to him." Piekaar records that "the nature of the forces which would come into action in 1942 was unknown to him." This limitation — the strength of the old Atjèh officer in his understanding of the personal political game, set against his blindness to the religious-political mass movements that would underlie the insurgency of early [[1942]] — shaped the Dutch military response.

## December 1941 – February 1942

On [[8 December]] [[1941]] Gosenson had all Atjeh militia and *landstorm* conscripts (~140 men) called up at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]], with the Sabang contingent (~20 men) called up locally. On [[17 December]] [[1941]] he moved his *Territoriale Staf* (Territorial Staff) from Kotaradja to [[Kabandjahe|Kabandjahe]] in the Karo highlands — a step that physically distanced him from the seat of the disturbances that would break out two months later. In January–February [[1942]] he oversaw the dispatch of the *Westkustcolonne* (West Coast Column) of nine brigades to [[Lawe Sigalagala|Lawe Sigalagala]] for the guard of the German internee camp, and the reorganisation of the remaining troops for a planned guerrilla in central Atjeh (Tangse–Geumpang–Takengon–Blangkedjeren).

## The February 1942 Reorganisation

When the Japanese attack on Sumatra became imminent, the territorial command of Sumatra's East Coast was separated from that of Atjèh. Colonel [[Overakker|R. T. Overakker]], shortly thereafter promoted to major-general, was directed from Java to Sumatra and assumed command of the newly established territorial command Middle-Sumatra on [[9 February]] [[1942]]. General Overakker was also entrusted with the central leadership of the defense of all of Central and North Sumatra; the command of Colonel Gosenson was hereby limited to the residency Atjèh and Dependencies — the area where he was preeminently qualified to operate. When General Overakker established the new *commando Midden-Sumatra* (Command Middle-Sumatra) in early 1942, Gosenson's command was formally restricted to the *gewest Atjeh* (region of Aceh); he accordingly moved his command post from Kabandjahe to [[Takengon|Takengon]]. The reorganisation is treated by the staff history as a reduction of his authority.

## The F-beweging Rising and the Arrest of the Eight Chiefs

The [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] sabotage wave broke on the night of [[19 February|19]]–[[20 February]] [[1942]] in the [[Seulimeum|Seulimeumse]] (the *sagi der XXII moekims*, the *sagi* of the XXII Mukims), culminating in the [[murder of Tiggelman at Seulimeum|murder of controleur J. C. Tiggelman]] on [[24 February]] [[1942]] and the [[murder of Von Sperling|killing of Rittermeister Von Sperling]] the same morning. Gosenson returned from Takengon to Kotaradja on [[23 February]] [[1942]] and ordered in Maréchaussee brigades from the IVde divisie and the ten-brigade column of [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Major Palmer van den Broek]].

On [[11 March]] [[1942]], at the resident's yard at Kotaradja, Gosenson — over the objections of the [[Resident J. Pauw|resident]] — ordered the [[arrest of the eight Atjeh chiefs|arrest of the eight foremost Atjeh chiefs]] then in attendance at the resident's conference. The chiefs were shipped via [[Sigli|Sigli]] to [[Takengon|Takengon]] under armed escort. The staff history describes the arrest as an impulsive act and doubts its wisdom: "whether it was wise of the Territoriaal Commandant, under these circumstances, and without positive proof of malicious intent, to order the arrest of the chiefs, must be seriously doubted." The act is presented as having worsened rather than calmed the unrest.

## The Defense of Aceh and Capitulation

After the fall of Java, General Overakker decided to withdraw the troops elsewhere on Sumatra via [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] to Central Atjèh in order to try to hold out in the Atjèh highlands for as long as possible. The focus of the defense thus came to lie in the [[Alasvalley|Alasvalley]] and the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]], where General Overakker would personally lead the defense. [[Takéngón|Takéngón]], where Colonel Gosenson would remain in command, became the main center covering the rear.

The Japanese landings of [[12 March]] [[1942]] at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]], [[Sabang|Sabang]], [[Ujong Batëë|Ujong Batëë]], [[Kuala Bughah|Kuala Bughah]] and [[Tandjong Tiram|Tandjong Tiram]] cut the Dutch northern ([[Lt-Col C. J. Bekkers|Bekkers]]) and central (Palmer van den Broek) groups off from Gosenson at Takengon. With the *3de hoofdgroep* (third main group) around [[Blangkedjeren|Blangkedjeren]] under [[Lt-Col Blogg|Lt-Col Blogg]] and the survivors of the [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]] capitulation converging on the same area, Gosenson's position became untenable. He was captured with the [[Sumatra capitulation (28 March 1942)|general capitulation of 28 March 1942]] at Blangkedjeren and interned by the Japanese.

## Significance

Gosenson's command is the hinge on which the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|staff history's]] Atjeh narrative turns. He is presented as a competent, training-focused officer whose command was hollowed out by troop reductions he could not prevent, and whose February–March [[1942]] decision to arrest the eight chiefs — taken under the State of War and Siege but without positive proof of malicious intent — is presented as the impulsive act that finalised the Dutch loss of Atjeh. The staff history's postscript attributes the defeat to the under-strength force, the pre-invasion Atjehnese rising, and the demoralisation of the indigenous soldiers; but the command responsibility is fixed, by the structure of the narrative, on Gosenson. Piekaar's portrait of the "Atjèh officer of the old stamp" — strong in the personal political game, blind to the religious-political mass movements of the late 1930s — supplies the human frame for that command responsibility.
