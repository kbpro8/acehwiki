**G. F. V. Gosenson** was a colonel of the [[K.N.I.L.|Netherlands Indies Army]] infantry who served as **Territoriaal Commandant van Atjeh** from [[1936]] until his capture by the Japanese in [[March 1942]], and in that capacity was the senior Dutch military commander in [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] throughout the run-up to and the opening phase of the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of North Sumatra]]. In the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account of the Atjeh campaign (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), Gosenson is the central command figure of the pre-invasion and invasion phases — the officer under whom the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussee]] divisions and the four garrison battalions were steadily reduced in [[1940]]–[[1941]], and under whom the Dutch response to the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] sabotage and the Japanese landings of [[12 March]] [[1942]] was mounted.

## Pre-war command in Atjeh

Gosenson had held the Territoriaal Commandant post since [[1936]], and devoted particular attention to the readiness and training of his troops. His command embraced not only Atjeh but, beyond the Atjeh boundary, the troops of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra's Oostkust]] ([[Pangkalanbrandan|Pangkalanbrandan]], [[Pangkalansoesoe|Pangkalansoesoe]] and [[Medan|Medan]]) — a footprint that placed him astride the entire northern approach to Sumatra. The staff history credits him with continuing the intensive patrolling that had maintained Dutch authority since the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]]: all brigades were required to carry out intensive multi-day patrols of eight to ten days each month, principally as a show of force.

When the [[4de Garnizoensbataljon|4de Garnizoensbataljon (the "Mobiele Reserve")]] — the formation raised in [[1928]] after the [[Bakongan uprising|great Bakongan uprising]] of [[1925]]–[[1927]] — was dissolved for reinforcement of Java and Malakka in [[1940]], and the detachments of the 1ste, 2de and 3de Garnizoensbataljons at [[Bireuën|Bireuën]], [[Langsa|Langsa]], [[Kotatjane|Kotatjane]], [[Tapatoean|Tapatoean]], [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]], [[Tjalang|Tjalang]], [[Lam No|Lam No]], [[Blang Pidië|Blang Pidië]], [[Padang Tidji|Padang Tidji]], [[Meureudoe|Meureudoe]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] and [[Boeloe Blang Ara|Boeloe Blang Ara]] were progressively run down through [[1940]]–[[1941]], Gosenson protested but complied. The staff history notes the warning from the military side that, when the perpetrators could not be seized immediately, they quickly gathered followers around them — a warning vindicated by the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] rising of February [[1942]].

## December 1941 – February 1942

On [[8 December]] [[1941]] Gosenson had all Atjeh militia and landstorm conscripts (~140 men) called up at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]], with the Sabang contingent (~20 men) called up locally. On [[17 December]] [[1941]] he moved his Territoriale Staf from Kotaradja to [[Kabandjahe|Kabandjahe]] in the Karo highlands — a step that physically distanced him from the seat of the disturbances that would break out two months later. In January–February [[1942]] he oversaw the dispatch of the *Westkustcolonne* of nine brigades to [[Lawe Sigalagala|Lawe Sigalagala]] for the guard of the German internee camp, and the reorganisation of the remaining troops for a planned guerrilla in central Atjeh (Tangse–Geumpang–Takengon–Blangkedjeren).

When General [[Overakker|Overakker]] established the new *commando Midden-Sumatra* in early 1942, Gosenson's command was formally restricted to the *gewest Atjeh*; he accordingly moved his command post from Kabandjahe to [[Takengon|Takengon]]. The reorganisation is treated by the staff history as a reduction of his authority.

## The February–March 1942 rising and the arrest of the eight chiefs

The [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] sabotage wave broke on the night of [[19 February|19]]–[[20 February]] [[1942]] in the [[Seulimeum|Seulimeumse]] (the *sagi der XXII moekims*), culminating in the [[murder of Tiggelman at Seulimeum|murder of controleur J. C. Tiggelman]] on [[24 February]] [[24 February 1942|24 February]] and the [[murder of Von Sperling|killing of Rittermeister Von Sperling]] the same morning. Gosenson returned from Takengon to Kotaradja on [[23 February]] [[1942]] and ordered in Maréchaussee brigades from the IVde divisie and the ten-brigade column of [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Major Palmer van den Broek]].

On [[11 March]] [[1942]], at the resident's yard at Kotaradja, Gosenson — over the objections of the [[Resident J. Pauw|resident]] — ordered the [[arrest of the eight Atjeh chiefs|arrest of the eight foremost Atjeh chiefs]] then in attendance at the resident's conference. The chiefs were shipped via [[Sigli|Sigli]] to [[Takengon|Takengon]] under armed escort. The staff history describes the arrest as an impulsive act and doubts its wisdom: whether it was wise of the Territoriaal Commandant, under these circumstances, and without positive proof of malicious intent, to order the arrest of the chiefs, must be seriously doubted. The act is presented as having worsened rather than calmed the unrest.

## Capitulation

The Japanese landings of [[12 March]] [[1942]] at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]], [[Sabang|Sabang]], [[Ujong Batëë|Ujong Batëë]], [[Kuala Bughah|Kuala Bughah]] and [[Tandjong Tiram|Tandjong Tiram]] cut the Dutch northern (Bekkers) and central (Palmer van den Broek) groups off from Gosenson at Takengon. With the *3de hoofdgroep* around [[Blangkedjeren|Blangkedjeren]] under Lt-Col [[Blogg|Blogg]] and the survivors of the [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]] capitulation converging on the same area, Gosenson's position became untenable. He was captured with the [[Sumatra capitulation (28 March 1942)|general capitulation of 28 March 1942]] at Blangkedjeren and interned by the Japanese.

## Significance

Gosenson's command is the hinge on which the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|staff history's]] Atjeh narrative turns. He is presented as a competent, training-focused officer whose command was hollowed out by troop reductions he could not prevent, and whose February–March [[1942]] decision to arrest the eight chiefs — taken under the State of War and Siege but without positive proof of malicious intent — is presented as the impulsive act that finalised the Dutch loss of Atjeh. The staff history's postscript attributes the defeat to the under-strength force, the pre-invasion Atjehnese rising, and the demoralisation of the indigenous soldiers; but the command responsibility is fixed, by the structure of the narrative, on Gosenson.
