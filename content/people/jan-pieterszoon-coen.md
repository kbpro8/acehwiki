**Jan Pieterszoon Coen** (1587-1629) — the fourth Governor-General of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie]] and the principal architect of its territorial and monopolistic policy in the Indonesian archipelago — is the central figure of Johan van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]], which traces his career from his first voyage under [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff]] in [[1607]] to his directorship of the Bantam-Jacatra factories in [[1614]] and his ultimate Governor-Generalship. Although Coen's most famous acts — the conquest of [[Banda]], the founding of [[Batavia]], the [[Ambonese murder]] affair of [[1623]] — lie just outside the period Van der Woude covers, the book's central argument is that the seeds of Coen's later policy were sown in his Atjeh-era apprenticeship, when he witnessed at first hand the failures of free competition on the Sumatran and Banda coasts and concluded that monopoly, backed by force, was the only path to profit.

## Early life and first voyage (1607-1610)

Coen sailed from Texel on [[22 December]] [[1607]] as an onderkoopman on the fleet of nine ships and four jachts under [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] — a fleet "bestaande uit 9 schepen en vier jachten" with 1942 souls aboard. The fleet's secret instructions, dispatched from Holland on [[11 April]] [[1608]] and reaching Verhoeff at Bantam on [[26 December]] [[1608]], committed the Dutch to the new monopolistic policy: in the words of the bewindhebbers, "de eylanden van Banda op 't allerhoochste gerecommandeert, als sijnde dese landen der Compagnie daer aen op 't alderhoochste gelegen". Coen was present at the disastrous negotiations at Banda-Neira in [[1609]] in which Verhoeff and forty-six of his men were murdered by the Bandanese on [[22 May]] [[1609]] — the event known as "op syn Admiraels Verhoeven leeren bitcharen". Van der Woude treats this as a formative experience: Coen had "het begin van het treurspel van Banda meegemaakt", and the lesson he drew was that trade without coercion was a doomed enterprise.

The fleet called at [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] on the outward voyage and again at Malaka on [[23 November]] [[1608]], where Verhoeff — seeking to fulfil his instruction to attack Malaka — found the sultan of [[Djohor]] unwilling to help and the Portuguese garrison reinforced after [[Cornelis Matelieff|Cornelis Matelieff]]'s [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|siege of 1606]]. The Twelve Years' Truce had just been signed in Europe; the secret instruction of [[4 May]] [[1609]] directed that "men nu sien, dat wy daer mede ons proffyt doen" — that the truce be exploited to entrench the Company's position before Spain could regain its strength. Coen's experience of the Verhoeff voyage taught him both the limits of naval power in the archipelago and the diplomatic complications of trying to fight the Portuguese while at peace with them in Europe.

## Director-general of the Bantam-Jacatra factories (1614)

By [[1 January]] [[1614]], Coen — still only twenty-six — had risen to be director-general of the Bantam and Jacatra factories, the post in which all the threads of the Company's Asian business converged in his hands. Van der Woude marks this as the decisive moment: "voor het eerst liepen daar alle draden uit Indië en patria in diens handen samen en onder zijn toezicht werden alle gegevens in cijfers in het grootboek uitgedrukt." Coen's own letters of the period display the same conviction: "Ick ben doen by gebreck van anderen gedestineert geworden (...) omme 't genneraal comptoir als hoeft te becleeden, den handel te dirigeeren, ende de genneraele boecken te formeeren ende houden."

The [[Discours of 1 January 1614|Discours of 1 January 1614]] — the long memorandum in which Coen first set out his views to the [[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]] — was written from this position. Its central complaint was that the bewindhebbers were sending "slechte, onkloeckmoedige menschen" to staff the Indies, and that no amount of commercial skill could compensate for the absence of "deuchtsaem van leven, cloeckmoedich ende groot van verstande". The Discours explicitly cited Atjeh and Banda as the two theatres in which the policy of free competition had demonstrably failed: at Banda "moet 't ondergebracht worden ende moeten aldaer in 't velt domineren", and at Atjeh the sultan's balancing policy had kept Dutch pepper prices volatile and the factory precarious.

## The Atjeh inheritance

Coen's Atjeh inheritance was the picture Van der Woude draws in Chapter V. By [[1614]] the Atjeh trade had lost most of its significance for the Dutch: the sultanate's pepper was no longer the prime attraction it had been in the [[1599]]-[[1602]] period, when [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]], [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]], [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] and [[Joris Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]] had all competed for it. The Dutch had learned to draw their pepper from the west coast of Sumatra — Priaman, Tikoe, Pasaman — and from the Bantam-Jacatra region. But the strategic lesson of Atjeh remained: that a sultan who could play Portuguese, Dutch, English and French against each other would never grant a monopoly, and that no factorij on his soil was safe without a fort to back it. The Atjeh experience thus fed directly into the Banda policy that Coen would execute after [[1618]]: trade must be monopolised, forts must be built, and resistance must be crushed.

Van der Woude is careful to note that Coen's policy was not an innovation but the working-out of the bewindhebbers' instructions of [[11 April]] [[1608]] and [[4 May]] [[1609]], which had already committed the Company to monopoly and to the use of force. Coen's contribution was to apply the policy with consistency and ruthlessness: "Coen's conclusie lag voor de hand. 'Van Banda connen ons meester maken, dezelvige eylanden met macht aantasten; sulcx moeten nootelijcken doen ende de plaetse populeren, ofte het landt verlaten, want 't en sal anders met Banda nyet zijn'." The Discours's argument that "Indië onafhankelijk van Holland" must be — that the Asian trade must generate its own capital, run its own books, and feed its own reserve fund — was the administrative counterpart of the strategic argument: the Company in Asia could not be run as a branch of an Amsterdam export house.

## The 1614 inheritance at Banda

When Coen took up the director-generalship, the Banda inheritance was the immediate problem. The two Dutch forts on Neira — Fort Nassau and the higher Fort Bellevue built by [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] in [[1610]] — were virtually isolated in hostile territory; the Bandanese refused to deliver spices except at prices they set themselves; the English under [[William Keeling|William Keeling]] and [[David Middleton|David Middleton]] had footholds on Ai and Run; and Javanese and Makassarese middlemen continued to bleed the trade past the Dutch blockade. The forts "richtten niets uit" and the last year-and-a-half had seen no trade done at all. Coen's recommendation — "de plaetse populeren", populate the place with imported settlers, and "met macht aantasten", attack with force — was the policy he would execute four years later.

## Significance

Coen's significance in Van der Woude's narrative is as the heir of the [[1599]]-[[1614]] decade of commercial frustration and the executor of the monopoly-by-force policy that the bewindhebbers had already authorised. His Atjeh-era apprenticeship taught him that free competition among European nations on Achehnese and Bandanese soil would always drive prices up and profits down, and that the only solution was the exclusion of competitors and the coercion of suppliers. The Atjeh chapter is thus the prologue to the Banda chapter, and the Banda chapter is the prologue to the founding of [[Batavia]] in [[1619]] and the conquests of the [[1620s]]. Van der Woude's Coen is not a villain but an administrator who drew the logical conclusions from fifteen years of Company experience and applied them with consistency — the "baanbreker in ons Indië" of the older Dutch colonial historiography, but also the architect of a system whose "smadelijke ondergang" the book's final pages foreshadow.
