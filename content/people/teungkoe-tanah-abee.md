**Teungkoe Tanah Abée** — also written *T. Tanah Abée*, modern *Teungkoe Tanah Abee* — was an [[Achehnese]] Islamic scholar whom Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* identifies as "the greatest oelama" of [[Groot-Atjeh]] after the death of [[Teungkoe Koeta Karang]] on [[8 December]] [[1895]], and whose succession to Koeta Karang's authority transformed the religious politics of the [[XXII Moekims]] in the decisive months before and after the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]]. Where Koeta Karang had sanctioned Umar's pre-defection operations through the doctrine that "dying in battle against Oemar's legion did NOT guarantee admission to the Islamic heaven, while dying against the Dutch did", Tanah Abée reversed this doctrine and actively branded Umar a unbeliever. His letters, sent "in various directions" in the spring of [[1896]], labelled Umar and his bands as *kafrs* and explicitly called Umar's murder "a work pleasing to God" — a theological intervention that materially shaped the religious climate in which Umar sought to build his post-defection coalition.

## Succession to Koeta Karang and Reversal of Doctrine

Tanah Abée inherited the spiritual authority of the [[XXII Moekims]] *oelama* network at a moment of acute crisis. Koeta Karang's death on [[8 December]] [[1895]] had removed the religious figure whose sanction had made Umar's legion religiously tolerable to the XXII Moekims population, and the question of his succession was simultaneously a question of the future relationship between the *oelama* and Umar's Dutch-backed military project. Kruisheer reports that Tanah Abée "gained much influence" upon Koeta Karang's death, and that he used this influence to overturn Koeta Karang's pro-Umar doctrine. The reversal was total: where Koeta Karang had made Umar's *legioen* religiously untouchable, Tanah Abée made Umar himself a legitimate target of religious violence. Kruisheer also notes that Tanah Abée's "name [is] connected with Oemar's defection (afval) from the Dutch" — a phrase which suggests that the very reversal which made Umar a *kafir* in Tanah Abée's eyes was also, paradoxically, what pushed Umar toward defection, since the loss of religious cover for the Dutch alliance made it necessary for Umar to seek religious legitimacy on the Achehnese side.

## Letters against Umar and the Spring 1896 Crisis

The concrete form of Tanah Abée's intervention was a campaign of letters. Kruisheer records that Tanah Abée "sent letters in various directions" labelling Umar and his bands as *kafrs* and calling the murder of Umar himself "a work pleasing to God". These letters, circulated in the spring of [[1896]] among the *oelama* and imams of [[Groot-Atjeh]], constituted a religious verdict (*fatwa* in substance, though Kruisheer does not use that term) on Umar and his followers, and they materially complicated Umar's efforts to build a post-defection coalition with the [[XXII Moekims]] chiefs, including [[Panglima Polim]]. The letters help explain why Polim demanded that Umar compensate the XXII Moekims population for the damage he had caused while serving the *kafi* Dutch — the demand reflected a population that had absorbed Tanah Abée's verdict and viewed Umar with deep suspicion even after his defection. Tanah Abée's intervention thus emerges, in Kruisheer's account, as one of the key religious forces that shaped the contested, transactional character of the Achehnese coalition which confronted the [[Dutch]] in the spring and summer of [[1896]].

## Significance

Tanah Abée's significance lies in the fact that his succession to Koeta Karang was simultaneously the moment at which the *oelama* establishment of the XXII Moekims turned against Umar and the moment at which Umar was forced to seek legitimacy as a resistance leader rather than as a Dutch auxiliary. The theological verdict against Umar was not, however, unconditional support for the Dutch — Tanah Abée labelled Umar a *kafir* not because Umar was resisting the Dutch but because Umar had previously served them — and it left the door open to continued Achehnese resistance under different, religiously-sanctioned leadership. In this sense Tanah Abée's authority, like Koeta Karang's before him, defined the religiously permissible space within which the Achehnese political and military actors of [[1896]] had to operate.
