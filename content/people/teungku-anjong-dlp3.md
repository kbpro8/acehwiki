**Teungku Anjong** (also rendered **Tunhar Anjung**, in the spelling of the [[Three Sagis]]' letter to [[Penang]] of 4 December [[1822]]) was an Achehnese saint (*teungku*) whose tomb, located at [[Kampong Java]] in [[Banda Acheh]], served as the site of an oath sworn by the chiefs of the [[Three Sagis]] in their refusal to receive the deposed [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] back as their ruler. He is mentioned once in [[P. H. van der Kemp]] (1900), in Appendix 7, which reproduces the [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|Three Sagis' letter to Penang]] of 4 December 1822.

## The oath at Kampong Java

The relevant passage in the letter, addressed to the Governor of Penang ([[W. E. Phillips]]), runs:

> "As to the old Rajah we acquaint our friend that we will never have him for our Rajah more, for we have deposed him and sworn to it on the tomb of Tunhar Anjung of Kampong Java, and we never can alter. Moreover we acquaint our friend the Honble the Governor of Penang that we request his kindness to us not to examine strictly into the respectfulness of our address, for we are peasants and not well acquainted with Malay languages, but let our friend consider only the meaning of our letter."

The oath at the tomb of Teungku Anjong was, in other words, the Achehnese chiefs' formal and religiously binding act of deposition. By invoking the saint's tomb, the [[Three Sagis]] — the chiefs of the [[XXII Mukims]], [[XXV Mukims]] and [[XXVI Mukims]] who together ruled the capital — declared that they could not now, even if they wished, take back Sultan Jauhar Alum, "the old Rajah." The plea that they were "peasants" unversed in the polished Malay of formal correspondence was an apology for the roughness of their address, but the substance of the message was uncompromising.

## The religious and political context

Van der Kemp cites the oath at Kampong Java in the broader context of the [[Panglima Polim's supremacy at Acheh|supremacy of Panglima Polim]] after the deposition of Jauhar Alum, and of the failure of the British attempt — undertaken in the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] and the [[Treaty of Pedir]] — to seat Jauhar Alum firmly on the throne. The Sagis asked Phillips to order back their sovereign [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syful Allum]] (i.e., [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum]]) to Acheh, "so that we may be happy and our friend's vessels go to and from Acheen with safety whether on commerce or for amusement." In other words, the oath at the tomb of Teungku Anjong was the public-religious act by which the Sagis committed themselves to the rejection of Jauhar Alum and to the recognition of Saif ul-Alum as the new sultan.

A footnote in Van der Kemp adds, by way of parallel, that the Achehnese had also told [[Pické]] — referring to [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]'s interlocutor — that, when the English had asked who was king in Acheh, the answer was: "what is that to you?" The same robustly independent attitude underlies the invocation of Teungku Anjong's tomb.

## Significance

Teungku Anjong is the only named Achehnese religious figure (other than the sultans themselves) to be invoked in the documents printed by Van der Kemp. His tomb at Kampong Java is the only specifically Islamic sacred site in Banda Acheh mentioned in the article. The oath sworn there by the Three Sagis was a binding religious-political act: it placed the chiefs' deposition of Jauhar Alum under the sanction of a saintly intercession, and made it impossible — in the chiefs' own account — for them to retract. The episode demonstrates the autonomy of the Three Sagis from the British-imposed settlement of [[1819]], and the depth of the Achehnese political reorganisation that had overtaken the arrangements of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]] within three years of the Treaty of Pedir.
