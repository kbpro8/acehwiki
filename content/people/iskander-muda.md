**Sultan Iskander Muda** (also written *Iskandar Muda*, "Iskander the Younger") was one of the most powerful Sultans of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], whose court — in the tradition preserved by seventeenth-century Dutch observers — traced its descent to Alexander the Great. In the account of [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]] he appears chiefly as the ancestor-figure of [[Iskander Tsani]]'s court: when the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy]] of [[1641]] was entertained at the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]]'s pleasure-garden, songs in his honour were sung before the assembled Atjeh nobles, who were so moved that "hun de tranen uit de oogen barstten" — the tears burst from their eyes.

## Name and descent

The very name *Iskander* is the Malay/Atjeh rendering of *Alexander*; *Muda* means "young" or "the younger." As Godee Molsbergen records, the Atjeh court believed Iskander Muda to be "den afstammeling, naar men geloofde, van Alexander den Grooten" — the descendant, so it was believed, of Alexander the Great. This Alexander-connection was an old and widely diffused legitimation legend in Indo-Persian kingship, and the Atjeh sultans — like the Macedonian kings of Asia — adopted the title to assert a place in a trans-Asian royal genealogy.

## The song of Iskander Muda, July 1641

The principal Atjeh-related mention of Iskander Muda in *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie* comes from the festivities of [[14 July]] [[1641]], when the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]] invited the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Dutch legation]] to her *lusthof* (pleasure-garden). The garden lay against the mountainside, where water streaming from two caves formed a great pond stocked with thousands of carp; the banks were spread with costly carpets. Seven meals were served in golden dishes in the Atjeh manner. The Sultana commanded her musicians to perform songs in honour of Iskander Muda, her late royal predecessor. The Atjeh hearers were so moved by the recitation that, in the words of the report, "hun de tranen uit de oogen barstten" — the tears burst from their eyes.

Strikingly, this encomium of Iskander Muda was paired with complete silence over the recently-deceased Sultan [[Iskander Tsani]], the Sultana's husband: "Over den pas overleden Sultan, een Pahangschen prins, sprak men nooit en deed alsof hij nooit aan de regeering was geweest" — Of the recently-deceased Sultan, a Pahang prince, one never spoke; one acted as if he had never reigned. The contrast — tears for Iskander Muda, silence for Iskander Tsani — is itself a political statement about which sultans the Atjeh court considered legitimate and which it did not.

## Repertoire of the court musicians

After the song of Iskander Muda, the Sultana's entertainments continued with Javanese, Siamese and Moorish (*Javaansche, Siameesche en Moorsche*) musicians and dancing-girls, "die het gezelschap vermaakten" — who entertained the company — during the further meals. The international composition of the court's musical establishment reflects the cosmopolitan reach of Atjeh at the height of its seventeenth-century power, when its envoys reached [[Constantinople]] and its trade extended from [[Goezeratte|Gujarat]] to the Red Sea. To this international court the song of Iskander Muda — a memory of Atjeh's imperial moment — was offered as the musical centrepiece of the audience.

## Significance

Iskander Muda is the central figure of Atjeh's seventeenth-century imperial memory. In the Soury embassy account his name functions as the touchstone of legitimate sovereignty — a sovereignty that the Pahang-born [[Iskander Tsani]] could not inherit, and that the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]] invoked by having his praises sung in the hearing of her court and of the Dutch legation. The tears of the Atjeh nobles are a measure of how recently and how powerfully Iskander Muda's reign had stamped itself on the political imagination of the sultanate.
