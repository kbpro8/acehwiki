**Sultan Iskander Muda** (also written *Iskandar Muda*, "Iskander the Younger") was the [[Sultan of Atjeh|sultan of Atjeh]] from [[1607]] to [[1636]], one of the most powerful Sultans of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], remembered in the Dutch colonial record as the most militantly anti-Portuguese ruler the Acehnese throne produced and the most powerful sultan Atjeh ever knew. His court — in the tradition preserved by seventeenth-century Dutch observers — traced its descent to Alexander the Great. In Dr. F. W. Stapel's *Geschiedkundige Atlas van Nederland* he is the "declared enemy of the Portuguese" whose sympathies lay with the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Compagnie]] in its long contest for [[Malakka]], even though the Compagnie itself "did not venture to accept his eventual support" on account of his "very militant inclinations."

## Name and descent

The very name *Iskander* is the Malay/Atjeh rendering of *Alexander*; *Muda* means "young" or "the younger." As Godee Molsbergen records, the Atjeh court believed Iskander Muda to be "den afstammeling, naar men geloofde, van Alexander den Grooten" — the descendant, so it was believed, of Alexander the Great. This Alexander-connection was an old and widely diffused legitimation legend in Indo-Persian kingship, and the Atjeh sultans — like the Macedonian kings of Asia — adopted the title to assert a place in a trans-Asian royal genealogy.

## Anti-Portuguese alignment and the Compagnie's caution

Stapel situates Iskandar Muda in the centre of the rivalry over [[Malakka|Portuguese Malakka]] that defined the [[Straits of Malacca]] in the early seventeenth century. The Compagnie, having failed to take [[Malakka]] by assault — both [[Joris van Spilbergen|Van der Haghen]] in [[1615]] and [[Cornelis Matelieff de Jonge|Matelieff]] in [[1606]] had had to raise their sieges — contented itself with maintaining a squadron of six to eight ships regularly cruising the Malaccan waterway to intercept Portuguese commerce. In this work the Compagnie "could count on the sympathy of Iskandar Muda, sultan of Atjeh from 1607 to 1636, and declared enemy of the Portuguese" (*verklaard vijand van de Portugeezen*).

Yet the Compagnie stopped short of formal alliance. Two reasons are given. First, the sultan's "very militant inclinations" — *de zeer militante neigingen van dezen sultan* — "also interfered more than once with the Compagnie's pepper trade" (*die ook 's Compagnies peperhandel nog al eens bemoeilijkte*). Second, the Compagnie was caught in a tight triangular diplomacy: [[Djohor]] was "mostly in strife with Atjeh, with which the Compagnie preferred not to come to blows" (*Pric Djohor meest in strijd met Atjeh, waar de Compagnie het liever niet mee aan den stok kreeg*), while the Compagnie also maintained "important and friendly relations with [[Siam]], which claimed suzerainty over Djohor." Accepting open Acehnese military aid against Portuguese Malakka would have upset both balances.

## The 1629 attack on Malakka

The most consequential Acehnese operation of Iskandar Muda's reign recorded by Stapel is the great fleet he sent against [[Malakka]] in [[1629]]. The Compagnie watched with undisguised satisfaction: "The Compagnie saw with much pleasure that Iskandar Muda in 1629 sent a great fleet against Malakka; nothing could be so welcome to her as that Atjeh and the Portuguese should exhaust each other in a mutual struggle" (*Met veel genoegen zag de Compagnie ook, dat Iskander Moeda in 1629 een groote vloot op Malakka afzond; niets kon haar zoo welkom zijn, dan dat Atjeh en de Portugeezen elkaar in een onderlingen strijd uitputten*).

The outcome disappointed that calculation. "The Atjehers were however totally defeated by the Portuguese admiral [[Admiral Botelho|Botelho]]" (*De Atjehers werden echter door den Portugeeschen admiraal Botelho totaal verslagen*). This defeat broke the back of Acehnese naval power in the straits and left the Compagnie to inherit the anti-Portuguese role alone. See [[Atjeh's attack on Malakka (1629)]].

## Relations with the Compagnie in Sumatra

Within Sumatra, Iskandar Muda's reign is the period in which the Compagnie first established and then repeatedly lost its Atjeh factory. Stapel records that the Compagnie's factorij (*factorij*) on Atjeh, set up in [[1616]], was withdrawn in [[1620]], restored, and then "again lifted in 1623; thenceforth only a single assistant of the Compagnie remained resident on Atjeh." The strain arose from Acehnese claims over [[Indragiri]], whose pepper was being drawn to the Compagnie's lodge in [[Djambi]] over the sultan's sovereignty claims. Even so, Iskandar Muda would not openly confront the Compagnie, since both shared a common enemy in the Portuguese of [[Malakka]].

Stapel also records Iskandar Muda's [[Acehnese conquest of Djohor (1613)|conquest of Djohor in 1613]], in which the Acehnese sacked [[Batu Sawar]] — an episode covered separately in this wiki.

## The song of Iskander Muda, July 1641

The principal Atjeh-related mention of Iskander Muda in *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie* comes from the festivities of [[14 July]] [[1641]], when the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]] — Iskandar Muda's own daughter [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|Taj ul-Alam]] — invited the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Dutch legation]] to her *lusthof* (pleasure-garden). The garden lay against the mountainside, where water streaming from two caves formed a great pond stocked with thousands of carp; the banks were spread with costly carpets. Seven meals were served in golden dishes in the Atjeh manner. The Sultana commanded her musicians to perform songs in honour of Iskander Muda, her late royal predecessor. The Atjeh hearers were so moved by the recitation that, in the words of the report, "hun de tranen uit de oogen barstten" — the tears burst from their eyes.

Strikingly, this encomium of Iskander Muda was paired with complete silence over the recently-deceased Sultan [[Iskander Tsani]], the Sultana's husband: "Over den pas overleden Sultan, een Pahangschen prins, sprak men nooit en deed alsof hij nooit aan de regeering was geweest" — Of the recently-deceased Sultan, a Pahang prince, one never spoke; one acted as if he had never reigned. The contrast — tears for Iskander Muda, silence for Iskander Tsani — is itself a political statement about which sultans the Atjeh court considered legitimate and which it did not.

## Repertoire of the court musicians

After the song of Iskander Muda, the Sultana's entertainments continued with Javanese, Siamese and Moorish (*Javaansche, Siameesche en Moorsche*) musicians and dancing-girls, "die het gezelschap vermaakten" — who entertained the company — during the further meals. The international composition of the court's musical establishment reflects the cosmopolitan reach of Atjeh at the height of its seventeenth-century power, when its envoys reached [[Constantinople]] and its trade extended from [[Goezeratte|Gujarat]] to the Red Sea. To this international court the song of Iskander Muda — a memory of Atjeh's imperial moment — was offered as the musical centrepiece of the audience.

## Succession

Iskandar Muda died in [[1636]]. His successor, says Stapel without naming him, "was hesitant of the Compagnie's increasing power and withheld support" when [[Antonio van Diemen|G.G. Van Diemen]] sought Acehnese aid for the [[1640]]–[[1641]] siege of [[Malakka]]. By [[1641]] the throne had passed to Iskandar Muda's daughter, the [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|sultane of Atjeh]], whose long reign transformed Acehnese-Dutch relations on the [[Westkust van Sumatra|West Coast of Sumatra]].

## Significance

In the structure of Stapel's narrative, Iskandar Muda marks the high tide of independent Acehnese power. He is the last Atjeh sultan strong enough to threaten Portuguese Malakka directly, the last whose favour the Compagnie courted but dared not accept, and the ruler under whom Atjeh's authority over the Sumatran West Coast — [[Tikoe]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]], [[Indrapoera]] — was firmest. His death opens the period in which the Compagnie, no longer checked by a powerful sultan, would extend its own control over the West Coast at the sultana's expense.

In Godee Molsbergen's account, Iskandar Muda is the central figure of Atjeh's seventeenth-century imperial memory. In the Soury embassy account his name functions as the touchstone of legitimate sovereignty — a sovereignty that the Pahang-born [[Iskander Tsani]] could not inherit, and that the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]] invoked by having his praises sung in the hearing of her court and of the Dutch legation. The tears of the Atjeh nobles are a measure of how recently and how powerfully Iskander Muda's reign had stamped itself on the political imagination of the sultanate.
