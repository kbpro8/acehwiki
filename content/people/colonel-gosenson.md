**Colonel G. F. V. Gosenson** was the Dutch territorial commander of Atjèh and Sumatra's East Coast from [[31 May|31 May]] [[1936]] until the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]], the senior military officer in Atjèh at the outbreak of the Pacific War, and — together with Resident [[Pauw|Pauw]] — one of the two highest Dutch authorities in the residency whose contrasting characters shaped the response to the Japanese threat. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* characterises Gosenson as "the type of the Atjèh officer of the old stamp, a master in the personal political game which characterized old Atjèh," who "spoke the country's language fluently" and served for "a long series of years" as chairman of the examination committee for the Atjèh language.

## Appointment and character

Gosenson was appointed territorial commander of Atjèh and Sumatra's East Coast on [[31 May]] [[1936]]. He was, Piekaar records, "just as vigorous and impulsive as Resident Pauw was contemplative." His "thorough linguistic knowledge, as well as his great joviality, enabled him to maintain excellent personal relations with the native chiefs" — the personal political game that characterised "old Atjèh." He was Knight of the Order of Orange-Nassau 4th class and bearer of the honorary insignia for important military operations in Atjèh in the years [[1911]]–[[1914]] and on Atjèh's West Coast in [[1925]]–[[1927]] — that is, during the [[Bakóngan uprising|Bakóngan uprising]].

## Limits of his understanding

The essence of the new political-religious movements that emerged in Atjèh in the late 1930s — the [[Poesa|Poesa]] and the related currents around [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]] — "however, remained foreign to him." Piekaar records that "the nature of the forces which would come into action in 1942 was unknown to him." This limitation — the strength of the old Atjèh officer in his understanding of the personal political game, set against his blindness to the religious-political mass movements that would underlie the insurgency of early [[1942]] — shaped the Dutch military response.

## The February 1942 reorganisation

When the Japanese attack on Sumatra became imminent, the territorial command of Sumatra's East Coast was separated from that of Atjèh. Colonel [[Overakker|R. T. Overakker]], shortly thereafter promoted to major-general, was directed from Java to Sumatra and assumed command of the newly established territorial command Middle-Sumatra on [[9 February]] [[1942]]. General Overakker was also entrusted with the central leadership of the defense of all of Central and North Sumatra; the command of Colonel Gosenson was hereby limited to the residency Atjèh and Dependencies — the area where he was preeminently qualified to operate.

## The defense of Atjèh

After the fall of Java, General Overakker decided to withdraw the troops elsewhere on Sumatra via [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] to Central Atjèh in order to try to hold out in the Atjèh highlands for as long as possible. The focus of the defense thus came to lie in the [[Alasvalley|Alasvalley]] and the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]], where General Overakker would personally lead the defense. [[Takéngón|Takéngón]], where Colonel Gosenson would remain in command, became the main center covering the rear.
