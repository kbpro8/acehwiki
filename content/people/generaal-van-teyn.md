**Generaal Van Teyn** (also spelled [[Van Teijn]]) served as civil and military governor of [[Atjeh]] during the critical transition period of the late [[1880]]s and early [[1890]]s. His administration—characterised by attempts to restore the sultanate through negotiation, combined with shipping regulations and coastal blockades—illustrates the strategic confusion that preceded the active subjugation policy of [[1898]].

## Appointment and strategic approach

[[Van Teyn]] assumed command after the death of [[Generaal Demmeni]] in [[December 1886]]. He inherited the concentrated defensive line established in [[1886]]—the system of forts around [[Koeta Radja]] that had been intended to make Dutch forces invulnerable but which had only worsened the strategic situation by creating a fixed target for resistance.

[[Van Teyn]]'s approach combined negotiation with pressure. He attempted to approach the sultan—by then the pretender-sultan proclaimed at [[Indrapoeri]] in [[1886]]—with the goal of restoring the sultanate under Dutch sovereignty. This was to be combined with a shipping regulation (*scheepvaartregeling*) that would control all sea trade and thus exert economic pressure on the resistance.

## The Keumala negotiations and Snouck Hurgronje's mission

A defining feature of [[Van Teyn]]'s administration was his attempt to deal with the [[Keumala]] party—the spiritual and political centre of resistance in the interior. Before the arrival of [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]] in [[July 1891]], [[Van Teyn]] had pursued negotiations with the leaders of the [[Keumala]] party, sending money and gifts in an effort to win their cooperation. These efforts produced only growing opposition among leading [[Atjeh]] figures, who gradually recognised that accepting government money was a mockery of any serious administrative policy.

In [[1891]] [[Van Teyn]] requested the return of [[Snouck Hurgronje]] to investigate the religious-political situation in [[Atjeh]], specifically "how, after the death of [[Teungkoe Tiro]], the disposition of the spiritual party was in general, and in which direction it would seek to exert influence over [[Keumala]]." This request—made shortly before [[Van Teyn]]'s departure—marked a significant, if belated, recognition that the religious dimension of resistance could not be ignored.

However, [[Van Teyn]] himself made objections to the arrival of a new adviser, arguing that this could damage the expected submission of the so-called [[Keumala]] party. This hesitation reflected his continued commitment to the negotiation approach, even as the evidence mounted that negotiation was failing.

## The failure of the negotiation policy

By [[1890]] the Dutch government had begun to recognise that the strategy of seeking contact and rapprochement with the sultanate party—encouraged by the sending of money and gifts—was a mistake. The leading [[Atjeh]] figures began to see through the government's efforts: they held out the prospect of submission when it served their purposes, then withdrew once their goals were achieved. During [[Van Teyn]]'s administration, the enemy employed spies extensively and struck with ambush and surprise wherever Dutch plans were detected—particularly the bands of [[Panglima Polim]] and [[Teungkoe di Tiro]], which even penetrated within the Dutch defensive line, leading to fierce battles in [[1887]].

Although the shipping regulations and naval blockades were not without effect, they failed to bring the political party with [[Panglima Polim]] and the priestly party under [[Teungkoe di Tiro]] to repentance. Meanwhile, the sultan of [[Keumala]] exerted increasing influence—perhaps precisely because of the greater attention the government had begun to pay to his personality. Neither [[Van Teyn]] nor his successor [[Kolonel Pompe van Meerdervoort]] ([[April 1891]]–[[January 1892]]) could find the means to bend [[Panglima Polim]]'s neck.

## Relationship to the broader strategic debate

[[Van Teyn]]'s administration represents the final phase of the pre-[[1898]] strategic confusion. His commitment to the restoration of the sultanate, combined with his reluctance to fully embrace [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s recommendations, exemplified the tension between military and political approaches. Only after his departure—following the [[1896]] defection of [[T. Oema]] and the subsequent appointment of [[Van Heutsz]] in [[March 1898]]—did the Dutch adopt the consistent active policy that [[Van Teyn]]'s negotiations had failed to achieve.

In the strategic analysis of [[Dr. J. M. Somer]], the shift from [[Van Teyn]]'s negotiation-oriented approach to [[Van Heutsz]]'s active subjugation represents the critical turning point in the [[Atjeh]] problem. The backgrounding of the sultanate restoration—first hinted at during [[Van Teyn]]'s command—became the definitive policy after [[1898]].
