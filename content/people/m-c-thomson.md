**M. C. Thomson** was a lieutenant of the [[K.N.I.L.|Netherlands Indies Army]] Maréchaussee who served in the IIde divisie on the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust van Atjeh]] during the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of March 1942]], fought at [[Lam No|Lam No]] under Captain [[L. H. J. Kloprogge|Kloprogge]], refused to surrender at the [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]] capitulation of [[29 March]] [[29 March 1942|29 March 1942]], and after a six-week guerrilla march reached the column of Captain [[Van Dormolen|Van Dormolen]] in the [[Medan|Medanse]] — only to be captured there on [[26 April]] [[26 April 1942|26 April 1942]]. In the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), Thomson's six-week march is the longest individual escape-and-evasion episode of the Atjeh campaign.

## The Lam No crossing (March 1942)

When Captain [[L. H. J. Kloprogge|Kloprogge]] crossed the river at [[Lam No|Lam No]] by *vlerkprauwtje* with a single brigade on [[13 March]] [[13 March 1942|13 March 1942]], Thomson was left with the remainder of the troops and the transport materieel on the opposite bank, with orders to repair the *pont* that had been set adrift by *verzetslieden* and had washed up on the beach. From soldiers of the [[Lho'Nga|Lho'Nga]] airfield garrison who appeared on his side of the river, Thomson learned of the Japanese landings at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]] on [[12 March]] [[12 March 1942|12 March]] and of the Japanese march down the Westkust.

Thomson subsequently brought his brigade south to join Kloprogge at [[Tapatoean|Tapatoean]], arriving on [[18 March]] [[18 March 1942|18 March 1942]] with the women and children evacuated from [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]]. He continued with the column south to [[Bakongan|Bakongan]] and on to [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]].

## Refusal to surrender at Lae Boetar

At the [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]] capitulation of [[29 March]] [[29 March 1942|29 March 1942]], Thomson — together with Captain [[F. Steinhorst|Steinhorst]], Lt [[E. F. K. R. Mathus|Mathus]] and the *officier van gezondheid* V. A. C. L. Dingemans — refused to lay down arms. Steinhorst was captured and killed by *opstandelingen*; Mathus and Dingemans were captured by the Japanese and "enige tijd daarna terechtgesteld." Thomson alone escaped.

## The Van Dormolen march (March – April 1942)

Thomson received permission from Kloprogge to attempt to break through to the still-fighting Dutch troops around [[Blangkedjeren|Blangkedjeren]]. On [[23 March]] [[23 March 1942|23 March 1942]] he was detached with three Maréchaussee brigades (about 60 men, with two machine guns but no motor vehicles) and ordered to delay the Japanese advance south of [[Bakongan|Bakongan]]. He was to withdraw to the [[Singkel|Singkelse]] and, at [[Koeala Kepeng|Koeala Kepeng]] (on the Simpang-rivier halfway between Bakongan and Singkel), to find large *prauwen* that had been promised him; with these he was to drop down the river and join the rest of the troops.

On [[24 March|24]]–[[25 March]] [[25 March 1942|25 March 1942]] he successfully laid several ambushes in the sparsely inhabited country, killing dozens of Japanese. At Koeala Kepeng, however, the promised *prauwen* were missing — "later bleek dat zij door de bevolking waren verstopt." A small prauw was eventually found that could carry only eleven men; the rest had to march. By desertion Thomson's strength fell to 33 men, and finally to 18 (three officers and fifteen *onderofficieren en soldaten*).

Around [[30 March]] [[30 March 1942|30 March 1942]] Thomson made contact with a detachment under Captain [[Van Dormolen|Van Dormolen]], who had executed a *tegenstoot* on the orders of major Van Aarsen from the [[Koeta Boeloebenteng-stelling|Koeta Boeloebenteng-stelling]] (see Hoofdstuk IV of the staff history) and was still in the *terrein*. Thomson briefed Van Dormolen on the Westkust situation, and the two detachments were combined. From the local population the combined force heard that General [[Overakker|Overakker]] had capitulated some days before — news that "een slechte invloed op het moreel van de inheemse troepen" had, producing further desertions.

## Capture at the "Merijke" onderneming (26 April 1942)

The combined Van Dormolen–Thomson column reached a *kampong* on the *cultuuronderneming „Merijke"* in the [[Medan|Medanse]] on [[8 April]] [[8 April 1942|8 April 1942]]. Van Dormolen contacted the still-serving Dutch *administrateur*, who agreed to supply food and shoes for the next day. On the morning of [[9 April]] [[9 April 1942|9 April 1942]] a patrol reported Japanese in a *ladang* opposite the kampong. Van Dormolen ordered the kampong evacuated; it was already surrounded, but the small group succeeded in slipping away. Van Dormolen then noticed that he had left his *actetas* containing *f 2000,—* in the house; one soldier returned for it but was captured by the Japanese, who used him — under pistol threat — to call the group back. Van Dormolen, hearing the soldier's tone, suspected the coercion and refused to return; Thomson (later, in captivity) confirmed the soldier's account of the coercion.

The group — by now reduced to six men — moved on, foraging by night. Near [[Pematangsiantar|Pematangsiantar]] the group met a *bevolkingspatrouille* with a Dutch-speaking Indonesian, who informed them that the *zelfbestuurder* of the streek had an urgent message and begged Van Dormolen to come with him. The *zelfbestuurder*, "zeer hulpvaardig," explained that the Japanese had ordered every kampong to report the presence of Europeans, on pain of severe punishment. On [[24 April]] [[24 April 1942|24 April 1942]] Van Dormolen decided to report to the Japanese at Pematangsiantar; on [[26 April]] [[26 April 1942|26 April 1942]] the group — now six men — was taken to a prisoner-of-war camp at [[Medan|Medan]].

## Significance

Thomson's six-week march from [[Lae Boetar|Lae Boetar]] to [[Medan|Medan]] — through Bakongan, the Singkelse, the Koeta Boeloebenteng-stelling, the Merijke onderneming and Pematangsiantar — is, in the staff history's Atjeh chapter, the longest sustained evasion narrative of the campaign. The staff history's emphasis on Thomson's small-unit leadership, on the steady attrition by desertion of the indigenous soldaten, and on the *bevolking*'s reporting of the group to the Japanese, frames the march as a case study of the impossibility of sustained Dutch guerrilla in the face of a hostile population.
