**Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje** ([[1857]]–[[1936]]) was a Dutch orientalist and colonial adviser, the author of *The Achehnese* — the great ethnographic and historical study of which the present Wiki is, in effect, an index. As **Adviser for Native Affairs, Netherlands India**, he was sent to [[Acheh]] in [[July 1891|July 1891]] under instructions from the Netherlands-India Government "to make a special study of the religious element in the political conditions of that country". The two volumes of *The Achehnese* (originally issued in Dutch at [[Batavia]] in [[1893]]–[[1894]], and reissued in English translation at [[Leyden|Leyden]] in [[1906]]) were the principal fruit of that mission, and remain even now the standard work on the country and people with which they deal.

Snouck's preparation for the Achehnese mission had begun years earlier in Arabia. "During a residence in Arabia (1884–85) I had been in a position — especially at [[Mecca|Mecca]] — to obtain an intimate knowledge of the influence of Mohammedan fanaticism upon the obstinate resistance of the Achehnese to Dutch rule". He had already, in 1884, become one of the first Western scholars to enter Mecca in disguise and observe the holy city at first hand; his experience there gave him an unrivalled insight into the pan-Islamic networks that linked the far-flung Muslim lands to the Hejaz, and through which the Achehnese pilgrims and exiles carried home the religious ideas that animated their resistance to Dutch rule.

In Acheh Snouck soon saw that "the available data regarding the language, country and people fell far short of what was wanted", and accordingly "extended my enquiry beyond the limits of my commission". "In order to get at the very foundations of a knowledge of the influence of Islam upon the political, social and domestic life of the Achehnese, I took (so far as local conditions allowed me) that life in its entire range as the subject of my research". By [[February 1892|February 1892]] he had gathered enough preliminary matter to begin composing the work; he wrote up his materials at [[Batavia|Batavia]], and the treatise first appeared in 1893–94. When the English translation was undertaken by [[A. S. O'Sullivan|A. S. O'Sullivan]] in [[1899]], Snouck was again in Acheh, "spending a great deal of my time in that country" while the real conquest was being commenced, and was therefore "in an incomparably better position to investigate that old pirate-state than I had been in 1891 and 1892".

The English translation was undertaken by the late **[[A. S. O'Sullivan|A. S. O'Sullivan]]**, Assistant Colonial Secretary of the [[Straits Settlements|Straits Settlements]], and indexed by **[[R. J. Wilkinson|R. J. Wilkinson]]**, Inspector of Schools in the [[Federated Malay States|Federated Malay States]]; it was published in two volumes by **[[E. J. Brill|E. J. Brill]]** of Leyden in [[1906]]. The translator's footnotes, which frequently correct or supplement the text from the standpoint of the Malay Peninsula, are themselves a valuable commentary on Snouck's material.

Snouck's influence on the Dutch conduct of the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]] was considerable. His insistence that the resistance was religious as much as political, and that no mere treaty with a Sultan could pacify a country divided among countless independent chiefs, helped to shape the policies pursued by [[Governor Van Heutsz|Governor Van Heutsz]] and [[General van der Heijden|General van der Heijden]] during the years of the final subjugation. *The Achehnese* is at once a monument of Dutch scholarship and the practical handbook of the colonial administration that ended the sultanate.
