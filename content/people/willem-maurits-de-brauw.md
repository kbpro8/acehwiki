**Willem Maurits de Brauw** (Jhr. Mr. W. M. de Brauw; not to be confused with the army officer [[Colonel C. A. de Brauw|C. A. de Brauw]]) was the Dutch Minister of Colonies who, continuing the line of his predecessor [[W. van Goltstein|van Goltstein]], defended before the States General the policy of the civil administration at Atjeh and the [[Normale bestuursinrichting (Atjeh, 1881-1884)|normal administrative organisation]] there. [[J. J. W. E. Verstege|Verstege]] (1886) accused him of political misleading of the natio when he declared on [[16 November]] [[1882]], when the Trooperschap at Atjeh was at its most miserable, "that there could be no question of a problematic financial state ... that we were lord and master on Sumatra" — words with which the paper *Het Dagblad van Zuid-Holland en 's Gravenhage* had rendered his reply; in the pamphlet's accounting Verstege acknowledged that, by the former minister's own letter of [[27 January]] [[1886]], de Brauw's words of [[16 November]] had in fact been: "De onverdeelde heerschappij over dat eiland hebben wij ons in deze tijden veroverd; het onbetwist en vreedzaam bezit van dat eiland zijn wij bezig ten volle te verzekeren" ("The undivided sovereignty over that island we have conquered for ourselves in these times; the undisputed and peaceful possession of that island we are on the way to secure fully"), and that by this undivided sovereignty he had meant only what the [[Sumatra Treaty (1871)|treaty with England of 2 November 1871]] gave the Netherlands: the right to rule the whole of Sumatra including Atjeh, to the exclusion of every other European nation.

## Statements on the Atjeh condition

From the same minister Verstege quotes the declaration of December [[1882]] that after the conquest of the [[XXVI Moekims]] everything had to be set in train "om de landstreek weder bevolkt te krijgen" ("to get the district peopled again"); the announcement, on [[23 December]] [[1882]], that governor [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]] had asked for his pension, with the addition that this meritorious official had performed excellent services in Atjeh — "the Minister might comfortably have added: to the Atjehers", Verstege commented dryly; and the counter-memorandum in which de Brauw denied the demoralisation signs of the Indies army which the member [[A. de Casembroot|de Casembroot]] had laid before the government in his Nota at the end of [[1882]], a denial which — Verstege predicted and parliamentary tradition confirmed — would, three years further, be maintained still.
