**The Sajarah Banten** — in modern spelling **Sejarah Banten**; in Dutch scholarship also **Sadjarah Banten** — is a chronicle of the sultanate of [[Banten]] on Java, one of the principal historical texts of the Javanese-Malay tradition. The chronicle is cited by [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] in the introduction to the [[1955]] edition of the two Malay treatises of [[Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] as the source of the hypothesis — advanced by Voorhoeve in his earlier notes in *BKI* 107 (1951), p. 357 ff., and subsequently withdrawn — that Raniri sojourned in [[Mecca]] before his departure for [[Acheh]] in [[1637]], and that he is the scholar referred to as "Ibn ʿAlī" or "Ibn ʿAlam" who refused to return with the Banten envoys to their country.

## The Ibn ʿAlam hypothesis

In his earlier notes in *BKI* 107 (1951), p. 357 ff., Voorhoeve had advanced the hypothesis — on the strength of a passage in the Sajarah Banten — that Raniri sojourned in Mecca before his departure for Acheh, and that he is the scholar referred to as "Ibn ʿAlī" or "Ibn ʿAlam" who refused to return with the Banten envoys to their country. The hypothesis was an attempt to explain how Raniri could have come to Acheh already familiar with the *[[Bahasa Djawi]]* and with two Malay works already in his luggage.

In the [[1955]] edition, Voorhoeve withdrew the hypothesis. On further reflection, he concluded that it is much more probable that the "Ibn ʿAlam" of the Sajarah Banten is in fact **Ibn ʿAlān** — that is, **Muḥammad ʿAlī Ibn ʿAlān as-Siddīqī** (d. 1057 H / [[1648]]) — of whom it is known for certain that he was repeatedly consulted from Banten: by Sultan Abū ʾl-Mafāḥir ʿAbd al-Qādir on the *Naṣīḥat al-mulūk* of al-Ghazālī (on which Ibn ʿAlān had written a commentary), and by Abū ʾl-Maʿālī Aḥmad on the *Tuḥfa al-mursala* of [[Muhammad b. Fadl Allah|Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh]]. The Banten envoys, in other words, consulted not Raniri but Ibn ʿAlān; and Raniri's Malay education is to be sought not in Mecca but in Gujarat.

## Significance

The Sajarah Banten is the source of a hypothesis about Raniri's Meccan sojourn that was advanced by Voorhoeve in 1951 and withdrawn in 1955. The chronicle is itself a document of the networks by which the sultanate of Banten — one of the principal Muslim states of Java in the seventeenth century — communicated with the religious scholarship of Mecca in this period. The fact that the Banten envoys consulted Ibn ʿAlān in Mecca — on the *Naṣīḥat al-mulūk* of al-Ghazālī and the *Tuḥfa al-mursala* of Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh — is itself a document of the Banten sultanate's engagement with the scholastic Sufism of the wider Muslim world, and of the channels by which that engagement was carried on.
