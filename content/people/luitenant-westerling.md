**Luitenant Westerling** — later captain, later still the controversial commander of the *Special Troepen Corps* — was a Dutch officer of the [[K.N.I.L.]] of partly Turkish descent who served in [[Atjeh|Aceh]] during the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Aceh War]]'s counter-insurgency phase and is mentioned in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* in connection with the late-colonial cycle of unrest on [[Celebes]] in [[1946]]-[[1947]], where his name became associated with the *Menado opstand (February 1946)|Menado opstand]] and the broader South Celebes pacification that would later make him notorious.

## The Aceh apprenticeship

Westerling served in Aceh as a young officer — the same theatre that produced the *colonne Schmidt* method of counter-insurgency. The book mentions him in passing, but the connection is significant: the methods later associated with his name in [[Celebes]] in [[1946]]-[[1947]] were learned in the Aceh counter-insurgency, where the *colonne Schmidt* had established the model of small, highly trained patrouilles operating with local intelligence against a religiously motivated resistance.

## The Celebes and Menado period (1946-1947)

The book's reference to Westerling comes in the context of the cycle of unrest on [[Celebes]] and the *Menado opstand (February 1946)|Menado opstand]] of [[February 1946]]. In this period Westerling's *Special Troepen Corps* conducted the South Celebes pacification that would later become the subject of controversy. The book does not detail the controversy; it registers the figure as part of the landscape of the late-colonial army's last war.

## Significance

Westerling's significance in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* is small but pointed. He is the figure through whom the book connects the methods of the Aceh counter-insurgency — the *colonne Schmidt* method of small patrouilles, the *dardanellen|dardanel* system, the use of local intelligence — to their later application in the Indonesian revolution. The book's quiet point is that the methods that had finished the Aceh War in [[1911]] were the methods that would, in [[1946]]-[[1949]], be used in the last war of the colonial army — and that the soldiers who had learned them in Aceh were the soldiers who would, in [[1949]], be abandoned by the political settlement.
