**Tuan di Bitay** is the sainted cannon-caster of Achehnese tradition, whose tomb and the mosque beside it, in the gampong of [[Bitay]] in the VI Mukims of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]], are revered as sacred up to the present day. He belongs to the legendary cycle that connects the [[Sultan of Acheh]] of the sixteenth century with the [[Sultan of Turkey]], from whom, according to the tradition, artisans were sent to instruct the Achehnese in various crafts. Among these artisans Tuan di Bitay is the most celebrated: he is credited with having taught the Achehnese, among other things, the art of casting cannon.

## The Legend of the Turkish Artisans

The gampong of Bitay had already been introduced in Snouck Hurgronje's account of Achehnese history, in connection with "the somewhat legendary story of the relations opened by a Sultan of Acheh in the 16th century with the Sultan of Turkey, and of the artisans lent by the latter to his Achehnese vassal to instruct his people." The legend reflects the real diplomatic and religious connections between Acheh and the Ottoman Porte in the sixteenth century, when Achehnese sultans sought — and reportedly received — military assistance from the Turks in their struggle against the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean. Whether the cannon-caster Tuan di Bitay ever existed as a historical personage is, in the nature of the case, impossible to determine; what is certain is that the Achehnese of the late nineteenth century revered his tomb as that of the man who had first taught them the art of casting the great guns that defended their coast.

## Bitay as a Place of Sacrifice

The sacred character of the tomb of Tuan di Bitay gave to the gampong of Bitay a special role in the Achehnese ritual calendar. Before the coming of the Dutch to Acheh, great — though in many respects profane — *kurubeu'en* feasts used to be held there. People assembled in crowds from the 10th to the 13th of the month Haji, and even for a couple of days longer; they came from the capital and the whole of the surrounding district, nay, all the sagis lent their contributions to this noisy gathering. Gambling, cockfighting and *sadati*-games were the chief pastimes indulged in by the people who crowded thither, and the fights inseparable from such pastimes were not wanting. Thus the word *kurubeu'en* acquired and still retains in the Achehnese vernacular the meaning of a heathenish tumult. Sacrificial cattle were also slaughtered here in large numbers. The custom in Bitay required that all beasts brought for sacrifice should be slaughtered by a descendant of the local saint, who acted as the keeper of the tomb. At the sacrifice of each animal a number of articles were presented to the slaughterer on a tray (*dalong*): two raw eggs, husked and unhusked rice mixed together, the various things which are usually employed for the cooling (*peusijue*) of a newly built house or one in which a wedding has just taken place, a flask of perfumed oil, a little *seureuma* (the well-known black powder), and other articles. A portion of the things on the tray formed part of the emoluments of the descendant of the sainted founder of cannon.

## The Survival of the Cult

It is difficult, Snouck Hurgronje observes, to conjecture why this tomb in particular is esteemed the proper place for offering sacrifices in the month of Haji; all that can be said is that Bitay came to be regarded as the place for these sacrifices, and that the feasts celebrated there assumed an entirely worldly character and became an offence to all devout persons. In Java, almost precisely the same objects as those contained in the tray are added to a sacrifice by those who adhere to old fashions; the teaching of Islam contains nothing of the kind, and there must without doubt be some pre-Mohammedan ideas at the bottom of these curious preparations for a sacrifice. The cult of Tuan di Bitay, in short, is one of the clearest cases in which an older stratum of religious practice — perhaps connected with the veneration of skilled craftsmen as bringers of new technology — survives under a thin Islamic veneer, attached to the name of a sainted cannon-caster whose legendary arrival from Turkey both legitimated and explained it.
