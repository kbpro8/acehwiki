**Jacob Wilckens** was a Dutch vice-admiraal who, in command of four ships of the fleet of [[Jacob van Neck|Jacob van Neck]]'s first voyage to the Indies, anchored off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[21 December]] [[1599]] — the same day the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush]] had taken place three months earlier in September — and who, after his refusal to land at the sultan's invitation, dispatched the chain of events that would lead to the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of 1601]] and the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|Achehnese embassy to Prince Maurits of 1601]]. Wilckens is, after the [[Cornelis de Houtman|Houtman brothers]], the principal Dutch commander at Atjeh in the earliest Dutch-Indies period.

## The Atjeh visit (December 1599)

Wilckens had sailed from the Netherlands on [[21 December]] [[1599]] as vice-admiraal of four ships detached from the [[Jacob van Neck|Van Neck]] fleet — the rest of the fleet under Van Neck himself following in June [[1600]]. He arrived off Atjeh on [[31 July]] [[1600]], where [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] — still in captivity, serving on the sultan's fleet — heard of his arrival. **Hans Decker**, one of the surviving [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman captives]], managed to get a letter aboard one of Wilckens's ships detailing what had befallen the *Leeuw* and the *Leeuwin*. The letter, and "andere gebeurtenissen" that had "het wantrouwen van de Hollanders opgewekt", led Wilckens to decline the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]]'s invitation to come ashore for a visit.

Wilckens's refusal was costly. Hans Decker and the boy who had carried the letter aboard "moesten het ontgelden" — suffered the sultan's displeasure. The Portuguese at Atjeh seized the occasion to strengthen their position, pressing the sultan to cede them his strongest fort in compensation for promised help against [[Djohor|Djohor]] — a demand that, Van der Woude notes, "bij den vorst wantrouwen wekte". Wilckens sailed for [[Bantam|Bantam]] without concluding any commercial agreement.

## The episode in context

Van der Woude treats Wilckens's Atjeh visit as the connecting link between the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush of 1599]] and the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit of 1600-1601]]. The Houtman brothers' misadventure had established the sultan's willingness to use force against Dutch visitors; Wilckens's refusal to land confirmed the sultan's suspicion that the Dutch were a treacherous power; and the sultan's continued detention of the surviving Houtman captives — despite Wilckens's arrival — confirmed the Dutch conviction that the sultan was "onbetrouwbaar". The deadlock could only be broken by a stronger Dutch mission with gifts and a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]] — the mission that [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] would undertake in September [[1600]].

## Significance

Jacob Wilckens is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal Dutch commander at Atjeh in the immediate aftermath of the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush]] and the principal instance of the Dutch caution at the Achehnese court in the earliest Dutch-Indies period. His refusal to land at the sultan's invitation — though understandable in the circumstances — confirmed the sultan's hostility to the Dutch and helped prolong [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]]'s captivity. The episode also marks the first Dutch diplomatic failure at Atjeh: where the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman visit]] had produced an ambush and the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit]] would produce a contract, Wilckens's visit produced neither. The visit thus frames the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship at its lowest point and shows the cost of operating at the Achehnese court without either gifts or force.
