**The Sultana of Atjeh** who received [[Pieter Soury|Pieter Soury]]'s [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|embassy]] in [[1641]] was the widow of Sultan [[Iskander Tsani]], chosen by the *Rijksgrooten* (great officers of the realm) to succeed her husband on the Atjeh throne. Her accession opened the long era of female rule in seventeenth-century [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. Godee Molsbergen, careful with the terminology, notes that her title was *Sultan* in her own right: "Sultan was haar titel, Sultane beteekent slechts eerste vrouw van een Sultan" — Sultan was her title; *Sultana* merely means first wife of a Sultan.

## Accession and the prince-consort proposal

When Sultan [[Iskander Tsani]] died in [[1641]], the *Rijksgrooten* chose his widow as his successor — "dezelfde, die op ongeveer veertigjarigen leeftijd een Nederlander tot Prinsgemaal wilde verheffen" — the same one who, at about forty years of age, wished to elevate a Dutchman to the rank of *prinsgemaal* (prince-consort). Both the *Rijksgrooten* and the *Hooge Regeering* (High Government) at [[Batavia]] vetoed the proposal. The widow became Sultana anyway, ruling in her own right under the title *Sultan*.

The princess-consort proposal is the first recorded Dutch-Atjeh diplomatic incident of her reign, and a revealing one: the new Sultana was reaching outside the Atjeh nobility for a spouse, and looking specifically at a Dutchman — a striking gesture at a moment when the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] and Atjeh had just cooperated to drive the Portuguese from [[Malakka]]. The Company's refusal shaped the rest of her long reign.

## Soury's embassy of July 1641

The full ceremonial of her reception of *Commissaris* [[Pieter Soury|Soury]] is described under [[Atjehsche ambassade ceremony (1 July 1641)]]. Among the unprecedented honours she paid the embassy:

- She came outside the palace to greet the ambassador "in spite of the rainy weather" — "een buitengewone eer, nooit tevoren aan iemand bewezen" — an extraordinary honour, never before paid to anyone.
- She sent the legation an *[[Atjehsche feestjas|Atjehsch gewaad]]* with three golden rings on the collar, "zooals nog nimmer tevoren aan een ambassade was gezonden" — such as had never before been sent to any embassy. (The senior merchant [[Jacob Compostel|Compostel]] did not receive one, presumably because she did not class him as part of the legation.)
- She offered Soury her own golden *beteldoos* (betel-box) to prepare a *sirih*-quid, while the other gentlemen used a silver box — an honour reserved for the head of the mission.

The Sultana, the report notes, sat shoeless in Atjeh dress chewing *sirih* with the Dutchmen — "Lands wijs, 's lands eer" — country's way, country's honour — they all surely thought, as they sat to the honour and profit of the Honourable Company.

## Diplomatic skill: the "translation" audience

The Sultana was no passive recipient of ceremony. After the formal audience she sent the chief eunuch to request that the *Secretaris* [[Jan van Riebeeck|Van Riebeeck]] come the following day to the house of the *Opperbisschop* (high priest) to "translate" the *missive* — although it had already been rendered into Malay. The silent purpose was to extract clarification on points obscure to her council: how Portugal in [[1640]] had been freed from Spain, why Portugal was no longer the Republic's mortal enemy, and why the Republic had concluded a ten-year truce with Portugal the following year. The Sultana's council also had before it the question of [[Francisco de Souza de Castro|Francisco de Souza de Castro]], a Portuguese nobleman imprisoned in Atjeh.

## The "maidens as ransom" request

At an audience attended by the *[[Panglima|Panglima's]]* of [[Pedir]] and [[Deli]] and many other Atjeh nobles, the Sultana's chiefs asked whether the Dutch had brought "Portugeesche jonge dochterkens" — Portuguese maidens — to be delivered as *losprijs* (ransom), as they had previously requested. Soury answered that there were none to be had at Batavia; that the ships had lain seven days at Malakka but could obtain none there either. His instructions had recommended him to add that the sending of maidens was *tegen onze wet en religie* — against our law and religion — but when the two Atjeh ambassadors who had travelled back with him confirmed what he had said, he omitted that declaration. See [[Maagden als losprijs]].

## The jewel negotiations

The most protracted business of the embassy was the disposal of the late Sultan's outstanding jewel-order. The Sultana already had many jewels; female vanity could not be played upon, since jewels were not women's wear in Atjeh. She was not liable for the order placed by her late husband; the treasury would not authorise the 15,000 *[[Tayl|tayl]]* (= 5,000 guilders) it would have cost. After long lobbying of the *[[Orangkaja|Orangkaja's]]* by *Secretaris* [[Jan van Riebeeck|Van Riebeeck]] — many a gift delivered at their houses — a price was agreed for the greater part of the parcel, but for the future the import of jewels by the Company was forbidden in Atjeh.

## The pleasure-garden feast of 14 July 1641

On [[14 July]] [[1641]] the Sultana invited the legation to her *lusthof* (pleasure-garden), magnificently situated against the mountainside, where water from two caves formed a great pond stocked with thousands of carp; the banks were spread with costly carpets. Seven meals were served in golden dishes in the Atjeh manner. The Sultana commanded songs in honour of [[Iskander Muda]], and the Atjeh nobles marvelled at her special friendliness to the Dutch. The song moved her hearers to tears. After the meals, Javanese, Siamese and Moorish musicians and dancers entertained the company.

The Sultana's conduct throughout the visit is striking for its deliberate contrast with her treatment of her late husband: she lavished honour on the Dutch, sang the praises of [[Iskander Muda]], and at the same time orchestrated complete silence over the recently-deceased [[Iskander Tsani]] — "men deed alsof hij nooit aan de regeering was geweest" — one acted as if he had never reigned.

## Significance

The Sultana of [[1641]] is the political hinge of seventeenth-century Atjeh. She opened the era of female rule that would dominate the sultanate for the next half-century. The Dutch embassy of that year — recorded by [[Jan van Riebeeck|Jan van Riebeeck]] — preserves the most detailed European portrait of her court, her ceremonial style, and her diplomatic agility. Her proposal to take a Dutch prince-consort, her refusal to honour her late husband's jewel debt, her "translation" audience to extract political clarification, and her silence over [[Iskander Tsani]] all show a ruler carefully re-founding the legitimacy of her reign on the memory of [[Iskander Muda]] and on a pragmatic, distanced partnership with the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]].
