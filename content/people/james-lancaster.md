**Sir James Lancaster** (c. 1554–1618) was an English navigator and commander who, as admiral of the [[English East India Company|English East India Company]]'s first voyage with four ships, anchored off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]] and conducted the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]]. He was received with ceremonial honours by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|Sultan of Atjeh]] [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]], praised Queen Elizabeth as "a princesse of great noblenesse, for fame speaketh so much of her", and on [[3 October]] [[1602]] went on — in cooperation with the Dutch fleet under [[Joris van Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]] — to capture the annual Portuguese *kraak* (carrack) from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]] in the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]]. Lancaster is the principal English figure of the early Atjeh chapter, and his visit marks the formal entry of the English East India Company into the Atjeh pepper trade.

## Background and earlier voyages

Lancaster had already made one voyage to the East Indies, on behalf of the Levant Company merchants, in [[1591]]–[[1594]] — the first English voyage to the East Indies, in which he had lost two of his three ships but had reached [[Penang|Penang]] and the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]]. His experience of the Portuguese trade and his reputation as a navigator made him the natural choice to command the first [[English East India Company|English East India Company]] fleet, outfitted after the Company's charter of [[31 December]] [[1600]]. The fleet of four ships — *Hector*, *Susan*, *Ascension* and *Red Dragon* (Lancaster's flagship) — left [[Woolwich|Woolwich]] on [[13 February]] [[1601]] and reached the [[Cape of Good Hope|Cape]] in April. From there Lancaster sailed to the [[Nicobaren|Nicobaren]] and on to [[Acheh|Atjeh]], arriving on [[5 June]] [[1602]].

## The Atjeh visit of June 1602

The full account of Lancaster's Atjeh visit is given in the entry on the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|visit itself]]. In summary: he was received with great ceremony by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|Sultan]], praised Queen Elizabeth, exchanged gifts transported by caparisoned elephants, and obtained leave to trade; he found the pepper prices higher than expected and his capital insufficient for a full cargo; he conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese *kraak* from the Koromandel coast. Lancaster's account of the visit, preserved in the Hakluyt Society edition by [[Clements R. Markham|Clements R. Markham]], is the principal English source for the early Atjeh chapter. His description of the Sultan's reception — the elephants, the banquet, the dances, the praise of Queen Elizabeth — became a stock passage of early English East India Company literature.

## The Dutch encounter and the hire of the *Lam*

When [[Joris van Spilbergh|Joris van Spilbergh]] reached Atjeh later in [[1602]], he found the *Lam* — a yacht from his own fleet that had strayed — already lying there, hired by the English Company under Lancaster, with the famous English pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] under Lancaster's command. The hire of a Dutch ship by the English Company at Atjeh is a striking instance of the practical cooperation — and competition — that characterised the early Dutch-English encounter in the Archipelago. Lancaster's choice of Atjeh as his principal port of call was strategic: the sultanate was the dominant pepper-power of northern Sumatra, and the Sultan was at that moment seeking to balance the Dutch, the English and the Portuguese against each other.

## The joint Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese *kraak*, 3 October 1602

In cooperation with the English, Spilbergh took a richly-laden Portuguese *kraak* (merchantman). After unloading the goods, the *kraak* was returned to the Portuguese "met hun vrouwen en kinderen" (with their wives and children) to sail to [[Malakka|Malakka]]. This act of clemency — keeping the cargo, returning the people — was a deliberate signal of Dutch-English good faith in Atjeh waters, and a practical way of disposing of prisoners without becoming responsible for them. The joint operation of [[3 October]] [[1602]] is the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]].

## The arrival of the first VOC fleet at Atjeh

At Atjeh the first [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] fleet arrived during Lancaster's and Spilbergh's stay — sent out by the newly-founded Company (charter of [[20 March]] [[1602]]). Spilbergh sold one of his ships to the new foundation and reached the fatherland with his well-laden ship. Lancaster, having dispatched the *Ascension* under **Christophor Colthurst** to the [[Banda-eilanden|Banda islands]] to load spices, sent the *Susan* home with her cargo, and returned himself to England with the *Red Dragon* and the *Hector* in [[1603]]. The voyage returned a substantial profit to the Company and established the English East India Company's commercial position in the East Indies. Lancaster was knighted by King James I on his return.

## Significance

James Lancaster is the principal English figure of the early Atjeh chapter and the principal instance of the early phase of Anglo-Dutch commercial co-operation against the Portuguese in the East Indies. The [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|visit of 1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|capture of the Portuguese *kraak* on 3 October 1602]] are the high-water mark of the brief moment in which the English and Dutch companies co-operated against the Portuguese in the East Indies, before the rivalry between the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] and the [[English East India Company|EIC]] hardened into the conflicts of the [[1610s]] and the [[Ambonese murder|Ambonese murder of 1623]]. Lancaster's ceremonial reception at Atjeh — the elephants, the banquet, the praise of Queen Elizabeth — also marks the beginning of English diplomacy at the Achehnese court, which would continue through the [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh|Captain Canning mission of 1814]] and the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission|Raffles-Coombs commission of 1819]] down to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog of 1873-1903]].

The hire of the Dutch *Lam* by the English at Atjeh, and the joint taking of the Portuguese *kraak*, are also the visible traces of how the Atjeh trade was actually conducted in the early 1600s: not by national monopolies but by opportunistic partnerships between Europeans far from home. Godee Molsbergen's framing of the episode places Lancaster's visit at the hinge between the pre-VOC "wilde vaart" and the chartered Company's monopoly — Lancaster, Spilbergh, and the first VOC fleet were all at Atjeh in the same year, and the Sultan had three European powers to play off against each other and against the Portuguese.
