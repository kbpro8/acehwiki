**Sir James Lancaster** (c. 1554-1618) was an English navigator and commander who, as admiral of the [[English East India Company|English East India Company]]'s first voyage with four ships, anchored off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]] and conducted the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]], where he was received with ceremonial honours by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]], praised Queen Elizabeth as "a princesse of great noblenesse, for fame speaketh so much of her", and went on to capture the annual Portuguese carrack from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]] on [[3 October]] [[1602]] in the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]]. Lancaster is the principal English figure of the early Atjeh chapter.

## Background and earlier voyages

Lancaster had already made one voyage to the East Indies, on behalf of the Levant Company merchants, in [[1591]]-[[1594]] — the first English voyage to the East Indies, in which he had lost two of his three ships but had reached [[Penang|Penang]] and the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]]. His experience of the Portuguese trade and his reputation as a navigator made him the natural choice to command the first [[English East India Company|English East India Company]] fleet, outfitted after the Company's charter of [[31 December]] [[1600]]. The fleet of four ships — *Hector*, *Susan*, *Ascension* and *Red Dragon* (Lancaster's flagship) — left [[Woolwich|Woolwich]] on [[13 February]] [[1601]] and reached the [[Cape of Good Hope|Cape]] in April. From there Lancaster sailed to the [[Nicobaren|Nicobaren]] and on to [[Acheh|Atjeh]], arriving on [[5 June]] [[1602]].

## The Atjeh visit and the carrack capture

The full account of Lancaster's Atjeh visit is given in the entry on the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|visit itself]]. In summary: he was received with great ceremony by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]], praised Queen Elizabeth, exchanged gifts transported by caparisoned elephants, and obtained leave to trade; he found the pepper prices higher than expected and his capital insufficient for a full cargo; he conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese carrack from the Koromandel coast; he was joined by [[Joris Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]]'s Dutch fleet and the separated yacht *'t Lam under [[Cornelis Spex|Cornelis Spex]]; and on [[3 October]] [[1602]] the combined Anglo-Dutch squadron captured the Portuguese carrack in the Strait of Malaka, taking 950 bales of calicoes and pintados. The 600 Portuguese crew were released unhindered to Malaka.

Lancaster's account of the visit, preserved in the Hakluyt Society edition by [[Clements R. Markham|Clements R. Markham]], is the principal English source for the early Atjeh chapter. His description of the sultan's reception — the elephants, the banquet, the dances, the praise of Queen Elizabeth — became a stock passage of early English East India Company literature.

## The return voyage and the cargo

After the capture of the carrack, Lancaster dispatched the *Ascension* under **Christophor Colthurst** to the [[Banda-eilanden|Banda islands]] to load spices, sent the *Susan* home with her cargo, and returned himself to England with the *Red Dragon* and the *Hector* in [[1603]]. The voyage returned a substantial profit to the Company and established the English East India Company's commercial position in the East Indies. Lancaster was knighted by King James I on his return.

## Significance

James Lancaster is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal English figure of the early Atjeh chapter and the principal instance of the early phase of Anglo-Dutch commercial co-operation against the Portuguese in the East Indies. The [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|visit of 1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|capture of the Portuguese carrack on 3 October 1602]] are the high-water mark of the brief moment in which the English and Dutch companies co-operated against the Portuguese in the East Indies, before the rivalry between the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] and the [[English East India Company|EIC]] hardened into the conflicts of the [[1610s]] and the [[Ambonese murder|Ambonese murder of 1623]]. Lancaster's ceremonial reception at Atjeh — the elephants, the banquet, the praise of Queen Elizabeth — also marks the beginning of English diplomacy at the Achehnese court, which would continue through the [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh|Captain Canning mission of 1814]] and the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission|Raffles-Coombs commission of 1819]] down to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog of 1873-1903]].
