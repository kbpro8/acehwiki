**Sultan Alauddin Muhammad Shah Jauhar** (in [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Veth]]'s spelling *Alaoe'd-din Mahmoed Shah Djohan*; in Van der Kemp's text *Alaëddin Mohammed Shah*; in the 1786 letter to Bengal styled, probably by corruption, *sultan Munson*) was [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Acheh]] from [[1781]] until his death in [[1795]]. He is chiefly known to history through a celebrated insolent letter of [[7 November]] [[1786]] to [[Warren Hastings]], Governor-General of Bengal, which opens Van der Kemp's narrative as a token of the "loose or strained" relations between Europeans and Acheh down to the eighteenth century.

## The 1786 letter to Warren Hastings

Van der Kemp introduces the letter as a vivid specimen of Achehnese hauteur. Hastings, "the mighty Warren Hastings", had hoped for a token of goodwill; instead the sultan, by sultan's letter of [[7 November]] [[1786]], returned him the brutal message that, if he thought to send more worthless articles, he had better leave it alone altogether:

> "Although I requested you to send me 300 small muskets conformably to the model sent to you, those which I have received, and which I return to you herewith, prove to be worth nothing. I pray you, if you should think to send me anything further, that it be something of value, or else that you leave it altogether undone."

The letter (printed in full by Van der Kemp as Appendix 1) was addressed to Hastings by the hands of Captain Steven, and acknowledged the receipt of Hastings's letter together with the accompanying articles, which the Sultan returned as worthless. It mentions earlier communications entrusted to [[Captain Hobbs]] — who, after two days' sailing from [[Tringano]], was wounded by a "classy" (a Malay sailor) from Java and died five or six days later — and refers to [[Captain Glass]], [[Captain Thomas]] and [[John Bird]] as Englishmen "acquainted with my affairs". The Sultan's letter thus also serves as a small window onto the loose English commerce with Acheh and the Malay world before [[Penang]] was founded.

## Identification and chronology

In a footnote to the appendix, Van der Kemp identifies "sultan Munson, son of sultan Ziemul Abudeen" — the signatory of the letter — with the Sultan Alaeddin Mohammed Shah Jauhar who, according to [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Veth's]] *Atchin* (1873, p. 66), came to the throne in [[1781]] and died in [[1795]]. The identity is established through the patronymic: the father named in the letter, *Ziemul Abudeen*, is presumably the same as [[Sultan Ziemul Abudeen]] who appears elsewhere in the Achehnese regnal lists. Van der Kemp notes only that this Sultan "must be the same" as Veth's *Alaoe'd-din Mahmoed Shah Djohan*. The corruption "Munson" of the Sultan's name in the English translation of the letter is characteristic of the OCR/translation peculiarities of the period.

## Place in Van der Kemp's narrative

Van der Kemp adduces the letter at the very opening of his article as evidence that, even under so powerful a governor-general as Hastings, the Achehnese court had felt itself able to insult Bengal with impunity. The incident thus frames the long-prehistory of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]]: it was because such a relationship had proven so unsatisfactory, and because [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light's]] proposal of [[1786]] to settle [[Penang]] rather than Acheh had been adopted, that the supervision of Achehnese affairs had devolved upon the Penang government. When, thirty-three years later, [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] arrived at Pedir to treat with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] (the descendant and successor of Alauddin), he inherited a relationship freighted with this earlier history.
