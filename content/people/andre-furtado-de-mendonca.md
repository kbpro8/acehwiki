**André Furtado de Mendonça** — also rendered *Andrea Furtado de Mendoça* or *André Furtado de Mendoza* — was a [[Portugal|Portuguese]] naval commander who, in [[1601]], led an armada from [[Goa|Goa]] to the East Indies whose appearance off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] sharpened Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]]'s hostility to the Portuguese and indirectly precipitated the release of the Dutch prisoner [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] and the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|1602 embassy to Prince Maurits]]. His subsequent defeat at [[Bantam|Bantam]] by a Dutch squadron confirmed that the Portuguese could no longer project significant naval force into the western archipelago. He is the principal Portuguese naval figure of the Atjeh chapter in the immediate aftermath of the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman affair]].

## The Armada of 1601

The scope of Furtado's fleet is reported differently by the two principal sources:

- **[[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]** describes an armada of substantial size sent from [[Goa|Goa]] with multiple objectives: "Van Goa uit zonden ze onder Andrea Furtado de Mendoça een armada tot bestrijding van den zeeroof, vervolgens tot bevestiging van de Portugeesche macht op Ceylon, waarbij ze Jaffanapatnam op Ceylon's Noordpunt bezetten, en ten slotte, wat het voornaamste moest gerekend worden, tot verdrijving der Nederlanders uit den Archipel" — From Goa they sent, under Andrea Furtado de Mendoça, an armada to combat piracy, then to confirm Portuguese power on [[Ceylon|Ceylon]] (in the course of which they occupied [[Jaffanapatnam|Jaffanapatnam]] on Ceylon's north point), and finally — what was to be considered the principal purpose — to drive the [[Netherlands|Netherlanders]] from the [[East Indian Archipelago|Archipelago]].

- **Johan van der Woude**, by contrast, describes the fleet sent against [[Bantam|Bantam]] more narrowly as "a galleon (*galjoen*) and a fust" sent from [[Goa|Goa]] by way of [[Malaka|Malaka]] to [[Bantam|Bantam]], with instructions to "getucht" — discipline — the natives for their reception of the Dutch. Van der Woude places this mission within the wider Portuguese effort to interrupt the Dutch pepper trade in the archipelago after the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|Dutch commercial challenge]] of the late [[1590s]].

The discrepancy reflects the two sources' different frames: Godee Molsbergen's narrative of the wider Indian Ocean strategic picture versus Van der Woude's focus on the western archipelago and the Bantam mission specifically. Whether the larger Goa armada was subdivided for the Bantam mission, or whether the two accounts describe two distinct Portuguese deployments in the same year, is not resolved by the sources available here. The Ceylon occupation of [[Jaffanapatnam|Jaffanapatnam]] — recorded only by Godee Molsbergen — was a permanent gain; the anti-piracy and Dutch-expulsion objectives would not be achieved.

## The Effect at Atjeh

The appearance of Furtado's fleet off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — placed by Van der Woude in June [[1601]], at the very moment when the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]] was weighing how to receive the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden]] visit and the expected Zeeland mission — altered Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]]'s calculations. "De Sultan van Atjeh vreesde dat Portugal met zijn vijand, den Sultan van Djohor, tegen hem samenspande. Ook het verzoek om op een der eilandjes een Spaansch-Portugeesch fort te bouwen, ontstemde hem" — The Sultan of Atjeh feared that Portugal was conspiring with his enemy the Sultan of [[Johor|Djohor]] against him. The Portuguese request to build a Spanish-Portuguese fort on one of the islands also displeased him.

The arrival was accompanied by the unguarded conduct of a [[Franciscan|Franciscan]] monk at the sultan's court, who had been pressing the sultan to cede his strongest fort to the Portuguese. The Atjehers suspected a connection between the Portuguese fleet's arrival and the simultaneous presence of a fleet of sixty warships of the sultan of [[Djohor|Djohor]] off [[Pasei|Pasei]], and "versterkten zich, bevreesd voor een aanval van beiden" — fortified themselves, fearing an attack from both. The sultan, unwilling to make the Portuguese his enemies but angered when they seized an Arab ship in defiance of his hospitality, had his shore batteries fire on the Portuguese ship; one of Furtado's ships was lost, and the crew was disarmed and imprisoned.

Van der Woude notes the strategic consequence: "de volgende Hollandsche vloot zou van deze gebeurtenissen profiteeren" — the next Dutch fleet would profit from these events. The Zeeland fleet of [[Hans Huybrecht Tonneman|Hans Huybrecht Tonneman]] that arrived off Atjeh on [[23 August]] [[1601]] bearing a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]] found the sultan already alienated from the Portuguese; the sultan released [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] "en zijn vrouw" without ransom, granted the Zeelanders a factory and a commercial treaty, and sent the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|three-ambassador embassy]] to Europe on the returning Zeeland ships. Furtado's failed mission thus paradoxically produced the diplomatic rapprochement that the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush of 1599]] had foreclosed — and, in Godee Molsbergen's framing, accelerated the very Dutch-Atjeh alliance the armada had been (in part) sent to prevent.

## The Bantam Battle

When the Portuguese armada had left Atjeh, "voer De Mendoça naar Bantam" — De Mendoça sailed to Bantam. The Dutch-Portuguese naval contest then shifted to the [[Sunda Strait|Sunda Strait]].

According to Godee Molsbergen, the last great Dutch expedition before the founding of the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] — thirteen ships under the Admirals [[Jacob van Heemskerck|Jacob van Heemskerck]] and [[Wolphert Harmensz|Wolphert Harmensz]] — encountered a strong enemy armada in Bantam bay on [[Christmas Day|Christmas Day]] [[1601]]: eight large galleons and twenty-two galleys. The *Breede Raad* (Broad Council) decided "dat men de Portugesche armade met courage zou bezoeken, vertrouwende op de hulpe des Allerhoogsten" — that the Portuguese armada should be visited with courage, trusting in the help of the Most High. Three Dutch ships and two yachts attacked thirty enemy vessels. The battle, begun on Christmas Day, ended in a complete Dutch victory; on [[New Year's Day|New Year's Day]] [[1602]] the heavily-reduced armada was in flight, Bantam was relieved, and the respect of the natives for the Dutch was greatly increased.

Van der Woude likewise reports Furtado's defeat at Bantam by a Dutch squadron under [[Wolfert Harmensz.|Wolfert Harmensz]] in a sea battle off the roadstead — a defeat that, in his narrative, completed the strategic defeat of the Portuguese attempt to interrupt the Dutch pepper trade in the archipelago. (Van der Woude's tag-list dates this to [[1603]], but his text does not give a specific date; the date accepted in the older Dutch historiography, as recorded by Godee Molsbergen, is [[25 December]] [[1601]]–[[1 January]] [[1602]].) The defeat confirmed that the Portuguese could no longer project significant naval force into the western archipelago after the destruction of their Malaka fleet by [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] in [[1606]] — though Furtado's defeat preceded Matelieff's victory by several years.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, Furtado de Mendonça is significant as the Portuguese admiral whose armada — by alarming Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] and displeasing him with the fort request — produced the diplomatic opening between Atjeh and the Dutch Republic that became the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|1602 embassy to Prince Maurits]]. He is the principal instance of the Portuguese attempt to interrupt the Dutch pepper trade at its Atjeh source, and his subsequent defeat at Bantam confirmed that the Portuguese could no longer project significant naval force into the western archipelago — a strategic fact that the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] would exploit in the [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign of 1606]] and after. The episode is a measure of how Portuguese strategic overreach in the Indian Ocean in [[1601]] opened the door to the Dutch-Atjeh relationship that would dominate the next four decades.
