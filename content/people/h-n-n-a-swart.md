## Lead

H. N. N. A. Swart was a *Luitenant-Generaal der Infanterie* (Lieutenant-General of the Infantry) in the Royal Netherlands East Indies Army and served as *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* (Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh and Dependencies). As the highest-ranking Dutch colonial official in [[Atjeh]] during the critical period of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]], Swart played a central role in articulating the unique governance challenges that the territory posed to the broader reform agenda. His detailed explanations of Atjeh's indigenous administrative structures and his candid assessments of the prospects for *zelfbestuur* (self-governance) in the residency made him an indispensable voice in the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) convened by Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] at [[Koeta-Radja]] in January 1914.

## Role in the Bestuursreorganisatie

Swart presided over the two *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) held at [[Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]], which were convened by Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] to discuss the *hervorming van het bestuurswezen* (reform of the governance system) as it pertained to Atjeh and the proposed new [[Gouvernement Noord-Sumatra]]. As *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur*, Swart was the senior colonial authority in Atjeh and thus the principal respondent to De Graaff's reform proposals for the territory. His contributions during these meetings were among the most extensive and substantive of any local official involved in the Sumatran phase of the reorganization, reflecting both his deep familiarity with Atjeh's complex administrative landscape and his pragmatic approach to the challenges of governance reform in a territory that was still only partially under direct Dutch control. Swart's participation ensured that the specific conditions of Atjeh were thoroughly represented in the deliberations that would shape the future administrative structure of northern Sumatra.

## Views on Atjeh's Governance

Swart declared that the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] could not have local *zelfbestuur* (self-governance) in the standard form because of the territory's distinctive administrative composition. He explained that only [[Groot-Atjeh]] and part of the *onderafdeeling* (sub-district) [[Singkel]] constituted *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* (directly ruled territory), while the remainder of the governorate consisted of *Inlandsche Zelfbesturen* (Native Self-Governments, also referred to as *landschappen*). This meant that the conventional model of decentralized governance being proposed for other parts of the Netherlands East Indies could not be applied uniformly in Atjeh without significant modifications. Swart's assessment carried considerable weight, as it came from the official most directly responsible for administering the territory and reflected the practical realities of governing a region where Dutch authority coexisted with, and in many areas depended upon, indigenous self-governing entities that maintained their own traditional structures of authority.

Regarding the appointment of indigenous officials, Swart stated that *erfelijkheid* (heredity) in Atjeh *hoofd* (head) appointments existed but was increasingly being set aside in favor of *bekwaamheden* (competence) as the decisive factor. He explained that while the local population was consulted and their recommendations were heard, these could be overridden if the *rechthebbende* (claimant) lacked the required qualifications for the position. This pragmatic approach to indigenous appointments reflected the evolving Dutch policy in Atjeh, where the demands of effective governance were gradually supplanting traditional hereditary succession—a tension that would continue to shape the relationship between colonial authorities and Atjehnese society throughout the early twentieth century.

Swart expressed confidence that with proper *opleiding* (training), within a small number of years the necessary *Inlandsche bestuurskrachten* (Native administrative forces) would be obtained in Groot-Atjeh. This optimism about the potential for developing indigenous administrative capacity was significant, as it suggested that Swart viewed the current shortage of qualified native officials as a temporary condition rather than a permanent obstacle to governance reform. However, he also raised probing questions about the proposed administrative structure, asking whether the *ambtelijk element* (bureaucratic element) in the proposed [[Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra]] (Regional Council of North Sumatra) would be overly dominant, and whether the rank of [[Civiel Gezaghebber]] (Civil Authority Holder) would be open to military officers with ambition—questions that reflected his concern about maintaining an appropriate balance between civilian and military authority in a territory where the two had long been intertwined.

## The Sagi System Explanation

One of Swart's most significant contributions to the *bestuursvergaderingen* was his detailed explanation of the indigenous administrative hierarchy of [[Groot-Atjeh]], prompted by De Graaff's inquiry about institutions comparable to Palembang's *marga*'s. Swart described a three-tiered system comprising three *[[sagi]]*'s, which he compared to Javanese *regentschappen* (regencies); *[[peleebalangschap]]pen*, which he compared to *districten* (districts); and *[[moekimschap]]pen*, which he compared to *onderdistricten* (sub-districts). This explanation was crucial for the reorganization discussions, as it provided De Graaff and other officials unfamiliar with Atjeh's internal structure with a framework for understanding how indigenous governance operated in the territory's *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied*. The comparison to Javanese administrative units was deliberate, as it allowed officials whose experience was primarily in Java to grasp the scale and function of Atjehnese institutions through analogy with the more widely understood *desa* and *regentschap* system of Java.

## Closing Statement

Swart closed the [[Koeta-Radja]] meetings by thanking Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] for the "*heldere, zakelijke uiteenzetting*" (clear, business-like explanation) of the reform plan. He expressed hope that this "*grootsche plan*" (grand plan) would soon be realized, signaling his general support for the reorganization effort despite the reservations he had articulated about its application to Atjeh's unique circumstances. This closing statement was notable for its diplomatic tone, balancing acknowledgment of the reform's ambition with the implicit recognition that Atjeh would require special treatment within any new administrative framework. Swart's expression of hope for the plan's realization suggested that he viewed the reorganization not as a threat to Atjeh's governance but as an opportunity that, if properly adapted to local conditions, could strengthen Dutch administration in the territory.

## Significance

H. N. N. A. Swart occupies an important place in the history of Dutch colonial governance in Atjeh during the early twentieth century. As the *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur* during the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]], he served as the primary intermediary between the reform agenda of the central colonial government and the practical realities of administering a territory with a unique blend of direct and indirect rule. His detailed explanations of Atjeh's *sagi* system and his candid assessments of the territory's readiness for *zelfbestuur* provided essential context for the decisions that would ultimately shape the administrative structure of the proposed [[Gouvernement Noord-Sumatra]]. Swart's contributions illustrate the tension between the universalizing impulse of colonial administrative reform and the particularistic demands of local governance conditions—a tension that was especially pronounced in Atjeh, where decades of military conflict had produced an administrative landscape unlike any other in the Netherlands East Indies.
