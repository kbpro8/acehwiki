**Sayyid Abdullah** — in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s history, "een rijk Arabisch koopman te [[Penang|Poelau Pinang]]" — "a rich Arab merchant at Penang" — was the father of [[Sharif Saif al-Alam|Sharif Saif al-Alam (Sayyid Abdullah)]] whom the Atjèhnese opposition party set up as rival sultan against [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Djauhar al-Alam Shah]] in [[1805]]. The elder Sayyid Abdullah's wealth and Penang base made his son a plausible candidate for the Atjèhnese throne in the eyes of the anti-Jauhar faction, and gave the succession dispute an immediate entanglement with British commercial interests in the Strait.

## Significance

Kreemer mentions Sayyid Abdullah only to identify his son — [[Sharif Saif al-Alam|Sharif Saif al-Alam]] — as the candidate of the Atjèhnese opposition. The choice of a Penang-based Arab merchant's son as rival sultan is, however, itself significant: it reflects the degree to which the Atjèhnese succession had become a matter of Strait-wide politics, in which the Arab trading networks of Penang, the British authorities at [[Penang|Pinang]], and the factions of the [[Three Sagis|three sagi's]] of [[Great Acheh|Groot-Atjèh]] were all intertwined. The elder Sayyid Abdullah's money and connections made his son's candidacy possible; the candidacy, in turn, produced the dual sultanate that the British would exploit in [[1818]]–[[1819]] and that the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 1824]] would nominally resolve.
