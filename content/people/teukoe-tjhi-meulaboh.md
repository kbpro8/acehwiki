**Teukoe Tjhï' Meulabôh** was the [[Keudjeuroeën|keudjeuroeën]] — originally the Sultan's customs-collector — of [[Meulabôh|Meulabôh]] on the West Coast of Atjeh, whose historical position as *belastinggaarder des Sultans* over the sixteen chieftains of the [[Kawaj XVI|Kawaj XVI]] was transformed by Dutch intervention into a territorial chieftaincy over a substantial portion of the lower [[Kr. Meureubôh|Kr. Meureubôh]] basin. *De Buitenbezittingen* describes him as one of the most consequential native rulers of the West Coast: a figure whose authority derived originally from a Sultan's appointment of [[1185]] Moh. (≈ [[1760]] AD) but whose effective power was the creation of Dutch recognition, which "begiftigden Teukoe Tjhï' van Meulabôh als loon voor zijne diensten met territoriaal gezag."

## Origins of the office

In the year 1185 of the Mohammedan era (approximately [[1760]] AD), sixteen chiefs from the Meulabôh region, led by a prominent merchant of that harbour town, made their appearance at the court of the [[Sultans of Atjeh|Sultan of Atjeh]] and received, in exchange for a promise of regular tribute, *[[Sarakata|sarakata]]'s* — letters of appointment confirming their authority. Their conductor was appointed the Sultan's customs-collector, with the title *Keudjeuroeën Tjhï'*, to whom these sixteen chiefs were henceforth required to pay the tribute. Among the sixteen were the [[Keudjeuroeën|keudjeuroeëns]] of Wojla, Boebôn, and Seunagan (with the *oelèëbalang-tjoet* of Seunagan), the three *dato's* of Meulabôh, and various heads from the Kr. Meureubôh basin; also included was the *Panghoeloe-Sidik*, charged with collecting the Sultan's dues on the island of [[Simeuloë|Simeuloë]], which thereby became tributary to the Tjhï' of Meulabôh.

## From collector to territorial chief

The Keudjeuroeën Tjhï' gradually attempted to extend his territorial authority over the sixteen tributary chiefs — succeeding with the three *dato's* of Meulabôh but failing with the others. Dutch intervention in the late nineteenth century transformed the office. As a reward for his services, the Dutch "begiftigden" — endowed — Teukoe Tjhï' of Meulabôh with territorial authority not only over the three *dato*-ships already under him but also over a portion of the Kr. Meureubôh basin (excluding Beutông) that had not previously been directly subordinate to him. The new territory thus created — still called, by historical resonance, the [[Kawaj XVI|Kawaj XVI]] — was, *De Buitenbezittingen* emphasises, "in werkelijkheid dus heel iets anders dan de Kawaj XVI, waarover hij vroeger belastinggaarder was": not a federation of sixteen chiefs, but a consolidated chieftaincy. The earlier suggestion that the Kawaj XVI was analogous to a Groot-Atjehsche [[Sagi|Sagi]] federation is explicitly rejected.

## The Teukoe Ali Tripa affair

The Tjhï' of Meulabôh's reach extended into the petty politics of the surrounding lands. The *landschap* of Teukoe Ali Tripa, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, was originally part of Tripa — itself once subordinate to Seunagan — but had been granted by the Keudjeuroeën of Seunagan to someone who had assisted him in war. That beneficiary's grandson pawned the land for 500 dollars to a Klingalese; from him it passed to T. Tjhï' Meulabôh, who in turn re-pawned it to another Klingalese. The Dutch eventually recognised the Klingalese as an independent *landschapshoofd*, severing the chain of pawning that had attached the territory to the Tjhï''s domain.
