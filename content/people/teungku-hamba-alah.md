**Teungku Hamba Alah** was a celebrated religious teacher of the [[XXVI Mukims]] of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], whose tomb in the mukim of Silang was, at the time Snouck Hurgronje wrote, still revered as a holy place. He is one of the clearest examples in Achehnese history of that process by which an ulama of exceptional spiritual authority was able, by the sheer weight of his piety and learning, to transform himself and his descendants into the hereditary rulers of a territorial chiefdom.

The panglima of the XXVI Mukims, whether from respect for his learning or in order to neutralize his influence, made over to Teungku Hamba Alah the government of thirteen of his mukims, and let him enforce Allah's law there to his heart's content. The grant appears to have been a deliberate political concession: a sagi-chief, by surrendering part of his territory to a man whose spiritual following he could not otherwise control, secured a measure of peace and a useful ally. In this single act we see, in compressed form, the recurring Achehnese pattern by which religious and territorial authority interpenetrated.

Circumstances favoured the efforts of Teungku Hamba Alah's descendants to retain this control. The grant of thirteen mukims thus became, in the course of generations, the hereditary uleebalangship of the XIII Mukims Tungkob — a territorial chiefdom of the same rank as the more ancient houses of the sagis, but distinguished by its religious origin. In time the distinctively religious character of the chiefdom faded: the later bearers of the title exchanged the religious dignity of *Teungku* for the worldly one of **Teuku Imeum Tungkob**, and so passed, by a single change of style, from the family of ulamas into the family of territorial chiefs.

Snouck's discussion of Teungku Hamba Alah is part of a broader inquiry into the manner in which offices in Acheh were created, transformed, and ultimately corrupted from their original purpose. The story of the XIII Mukims Tungkob is paired with that of [[Teungku Chot Putu]] of the III Mukims, who likewise succeeded in attaining to some degree of authority over a self-governing group of mukims through the personal force of his piety and severity, though in his case the authority did not crystallize into a permanent uleebalangship.

The tomb of Teungku Hamba Alah at Silang remains one of the *kubu kramat*, the revered graves that punctuate the religious geography of Great Acheh, a material monument to the founder of a lineage that turned sanctity into sovereignty.
