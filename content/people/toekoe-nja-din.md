**Toekoe Nja Din** was an [[Achehnese|Atjehnese]] female leader who, during the early [[1890s]], effectively governed the [[VI Moekims]] of [[Groot-Atjeh]] — the territory immediately adjacent to the western side of the Dutch [[geconcentreerde linie]] — while her husband [[Toekoe Oemar]] pursued his military and political career along the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust]] and in the upper valley of the [[Acheh River|Atjeh-rivier]]. She is one of the few women named in the Dutch popular account of the [[1896]] crisis preserved in the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of Eigen Haard]], where she appears as a political actor in her own right rather than as a consort.

## Background

The [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album]] introduces [[Toekoe Nja Din|Nja Din]] in the course of its narrative of [[Toekoe Oemar]]'s rise. Oemar had, by the late [[1870s]], begun to consolidate his position through the customary Acehnese means of marriage alliance: he took three wives from what the Dutch account calls *den deftigen stand* (the respectable class), and through these alliances — together with the less conventional methods of poisoning and *klewang* blow that the same account attributes to him — he established himself as *Panglima prang Besar Gouvernement* ("commander-in-chief of the great war for the government") and as *Oeloebalang* of [[Lepong]] on the Westkust. Nja Din, identified in the album as "one of his wives," was the effective ruler of the VI Moekims while Oemar was absent on his Westkust campaigns.

## The VI Moekims and the Dutch line

The [[VI Moekims]] formed the western segment of the territory enclosed by the Dutch *geconcentreerde linie* after the Dutch retreat from the interior of [[Groot-Atjeh]] in [[1884]]. To hold the line, the Dutch had constructed a perimeter of *bentings* (fortified posts) around [[Kota Radja]], with outposts — among them [[Lambaroe]], [[Lamreng]], [[Lamkrak]] — projecting into the surrounding mukim territory. Toekoe Nja Din's territory lay immediately to the west of this perimeter, and she thus controlled the principal line of communication between the Dutch-held coast and the upper valley where the religious resistance was organised.

## The Habib Long incursion

The album records that in the months before [[Toekoe Oemar]]'s open defection of [[March]] [[1896]], a certain *Habib Long* — described as a former Bengaloesche koewachter (cattle-keeper of Bengali origin) — led a bandit group into the territory of Nja Din, where he burned houses, murdered a village head and his wife, and killed several of Oemar's best fighters. The incursion was politically significant because it placed Oemar in a position where, as the album puts it, he was given *de gelegenheid … om orde en rust te handhaven en de Moslemin te verdrijven* ("the opportunity to maintain order and peace and to drive out the Moslemin"). Nja Din's territory was thus the immediate theatre of the *Moslemin* versus *Oeloebalang* conflict which the Dutch would later exploit by hiring Oemar as their auxiliary.

## The 1896 defection

When [[Toekoe Oemar]] defected from the Dutch alliance in [[March]] [[1896]], the territory of the VI Moekims — under Nja Din's day-to-day rule — became one of the principal theatres of the ensuing crisis. The album records that the *Moslemin* (religious-warrior bands) who had been driven out of Nja Din's territory by Oemar's earlier purges now returned, and that Oemar's defection placed the entire Dutch outer line in jeopardy. Nja Din's own political posture during the defection crisis is not specified in the album, but the fact that her territory is named in the album's account of the defection — and that she is identified as the wife who *bestuurde feitelijk de VI Moekims* ("actually governed the VI Moekims") — indicates that she was a political figure of the first rank in the immediate pre-defection period.

## Significance

Toekoe Nja Din is significant in the documentary record of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] as one of the few named female political actors in the Dutch popular historiography of the late *concentratie* period. Her effective governance of the [[VI Moekims]] during her husband's absences illustrates the way in which the *adat*-based structure of Atjehnese authority — in which an *[[Uleebalang|oeloebalang]]*'s household shared in the exercise of territorial power — could place a woman at the head of a strategically critical district. Her appearance in the [[Atjeh-Album (Eigen Haard, 1896)|Atjeh-Album of 1896]] is also a marker of the relative prominence of the VI Moekims as the western anchor of the Dutch perimeter and as the principal contact zone between the Dutch-held coast and the Atjehnese interior.
