**Ibn Battuta** — full name **Abu Abdullah Muhammad Ibn Battuta** (Morocco, [[1304]]–[[1369]]) — was the Arab traveller whose *Rihla* (Voyage) records his visit, in [[1345]]/[[1346]], to the sultanate of [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] on the north coast of [[Sumatra]], where he was received by [[Sultan Malik at-Tahir of Pasej|Sultan Malik at-Tahir]] for fifteen days. His is the principal Arabic-language account of the pre-[[Aceh Sultanate|Atjehnese]] north Sumatran sultanate, and the principal external witness to the [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]]–[[Moel Djawa|Moel Djawa]] complex that [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] reads as the older Hindu-Buddhist geography of the [[Aroe|Aru]]-[[Acheh|Atjeh]] sphere.

## Visit to Pasej (1345-1346)

Sailing from [[Bengal|Bengal]] to the "isle of Djaouah" (i.e. [[Sumatra]]), Ibn Battuta arrived at the port of [[Pasej-Samodra|Sarha]], which [[G. P. Rouffaer|Rouffaer]] thought "probably Telok Semawe", but which [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] decodes as *Sa-ri-ra* (i.e. *awak*), the city of the [[Djawaka|Dj-awak-a]]. Four miles inland lay the capital, [[Pasej-Samodra|Sjemoe-t or Samatra]] — "a city with wooden wall and wooden towers", probably, De Roo de la Faille suggests, [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] on the north coast. There Ibn Battuta was hospitably received by Sultan [[Sultan Malik at-Tahir of Pasej|Malik at-Tahir]], an zealous warrior of the holy war against the infidels of the interior, whose slaving raids brought him profitable spoil. Ibn Battuta stayed fifteen days, then sailed by junk to [[Moel Djawa]].

## Voyage to Moel Djawa

Ibn Battuta's next destination, [[Moel Djawa]], had its capital at ***Qakola*** — a name conventionally read as [[Angkola|Rouffaer's Bataksch Angkola]], but which [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] decodes as Arabic *Qaclola* / Sanskrit *Kakkola*, the camphor-name by which the Hindu-Javanese chronicles denoted the ancestral land of [[Taroema]]. The "city", Ibn Battuta wrote, was surrounded by heavy walls of natural stone — not baked brick, *bata*, i.e. not *Beta[-ksch]* but of the *Djawa-ge*. There were many elephants, used as draught and transport animals; the products of the land included cloves and nutmeg, both on the local market — evidence, De Roo de la Faille notes, of regular trade with the [[Moluccas|Moluccas]].

## Decoding: *Thaouglich* and the return voyage

On his voyage toward [[China|China]], Ibn Battuta came to or through a place he calls *Thaouglich* — which [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] reads as *tawa-li-si*, a contraction of *Ketawa-ng gi-li we-si*, a *bedekte taal* form for [[Sulawesi|Kataha, the island of Sulawesi]]. On his return he came into the sea of [[Champa|Tjempa]], where the dangerous bird *Roh* dwelt — which De Roo de la Faille decodes as *pek-si roh*, i.e. the *si-kep* (bird-catchers) of [[Aroe|A-ro]], the sea-rovers of [[Aroe-baai|the bay of Aroe]] in the *arnawa* of *Tjempa-ka(-mbodja)*.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, Ibn Battuta's importance is twofold. He is the only Arabic-language witness to the [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] sultanate of the pre-Atjehnese age, and so the principal external source for the [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] court in its golden age; and his *Rihla* is, in [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]]'s reading, pervaded by the *bedekte taal* of the [[Djawaka|Djawaka]]s, by which the older Hindu-Buddhist geography of the [[Aroe|Aru]]-[[Acheh|Atjeh]] sphere was concealed from the foreign listener. His account fixes the [[Moel Djawa|Moel Djawa]]-[[Qakola|Qakola]] complex as the *Taroema* of the chronicle tradition, and so the principal east-coast landmark of pre-Atjehnese Sumatra in Arabic record.
