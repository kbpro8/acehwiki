**Gerard le Roy** was a [[The Netherlands|Dutch]] Zeeland merchant of the [[Middelburgsche Compagnie|Middelburgsche Compagnie]] who, in August [[1601|1601]], arrived at [[Atjeh|Aceh]] as commander — together with his fellow merchant [[Laurens Bicker|Laurens Bicker]] — of the four ships dispatched in January [[1601|1601]] by the company founded by [[Adriaen Hendriksz ten Haeff|Adriaen Hendriksz ten Haeff]] and others, under the admiralship of [[Hans Huybrecht Tonneman|Hans Huybrecht Tonneman]]. His timely arrival at the Acehnese court secured the release of [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] and the surviving Dutch prisoners from Acehnese captivity, produced the first formal Dutch-Acehnese commercial settlement (the factory of 28 August 1601), the first direct shipment of Acehnese pepper to the Republic, and the first Acehnese embassy to the [[Staten-Generaal|Staten-Generaal]]. He is, in [[F. W. Stapel|Stapel]]'s and [[Johan van der Woude|Van der Woude]]'s narratives, the principal Dutch commercial figure of the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of 1601]].

## The 1601 Voyage

The fleet of four ships was substantially sized for a pre-[[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] venture. [[F. W. Stapel|Stapel]] records: "In August '01 Gerard Leroy (sent out in January '01 by the 'Middelburgsche Compagnie,' founded by Adriaen Hendriksz ten Haeff and others, with 4 ships) put in here" — Dutch original: *in Aug. '01 Gerard Leroy (door de 'Middelburgsche Compagnie,' opgericht door Adriaen Hendriksz ten Haeff c.a., in Jan. '01 met 4 schepen uitgezonden) hier aanliep*. The founding by [[Adriaen Hendriksz ten Haeff|Ten Haeff]] signals the rising Middelburg interest in the East India trade that would soon be folded into the unified VOC. [[Johan van der Woude|Van der Woude]] adds that the two chief merchants of the fleet, Le Roy and [[Laurens Bicker|Bicker]], arrived off Aceh on [[23 August|23 August]] [[1601|1601]] with gifts and a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]] — a diplomatic rapprochement that the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman ambush of 1599]] and the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden affair of 1600-1601]] had foreclosed.

## The Release of the Houtman Party

The release came swiftly and through no Dutch intercession. [[F. W. Stapel|Stapel]] records: "Meanwhile the Atjehers and Portuguese had fallen into enmity, so that when in August '01 Gerard Leroy … here put in, Frederik de H[outman] and his men were set at liberty, even before a corresponding letter from Prince [[Maurits van Oranje|Maurits]] to the prince had been presented" — Dutch original: *Intusschen geraakten de Atjehers en Portugeezen in vijandschap, zoodat toen in Aug. '01 Gerard Leroy … hier aanliep, Frederik de H. en de zijnen in vrijheid werden gesteld, nog vóór een desbetreffend schrijven van Prins Maurits aan den vorst was overhandigd*.

The decisive factor was the Aceh-Portuguese breach, not the Dutch embassy. The [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]], no longer aligned with the Portuguese, no longer needed the Dutch prisoners as Portuguese-pleasing hostages. The release preceded the formal delivery of Prince [[Maurits van Oranje|Maurits]]'s letter — a detail that suggests the sultan was already inclined to release the Dutch before the formal diplomatic cover arrived.

## The Van Caerden Indemnity

The chief remaining grievance between the Dutch and the sultan was [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]]'s seizure of pepper from Arab junks at anchor on [[12 January|12 January]] [[1601|1601]] — a seizure that the Arab claimants had demanded compensation for and that the sultan had used to justify the rearrest of [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] in chains. Le Roy and Bicker, in [[Johan van der Woude|Van der Woude]]'s account, "waren zoo verstandig, den sultan de verzekering te geven dat [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] zijn bevoegdheid te buiten was gegaan toen hij de peper uit de Arabische jonken lichtte, en dat de gedupeerde kooplieden zonder twijfel schadeloos zouden worden gesteld" — were so wise as to give the sultan the assurance that Paulus van Caerden had exceeded his authority when he lifted the pepper from the Arab junks, and that the wronged merchants would without doubt be compensated. The sultan accepted the assurance; Houtman was released; the commercial relationship was restored.

The indemnity was paid. Bicker, who would handle the Arab claimants' interests, reached an agreement with the [[Nieuwe Compagnie|Nieuwe Compagnie]] in [[1602|1602]] for the payment of ƒ 51,048 in damages — a sum that, according to [[Francisco Pyrard|Francisc Pyrard]], was still unpaid in [[1608|1608]] and, per [[P. A. Tiele|Tiele]], still pending in [[1617|1617]]. The long-running compensation claim was the principal commercial-legal consequence of the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden affair]] and the principal instance of the early Dutch companies' willingness to indemnify third-party losses caused by their commanders' unilateral actions.

## The First Factory and the First Pepper Cargo

Le Roy's visit also produced the first formal Dutch-Acehnese commercial settlement. [[F. W. Stapel|Stapel]] records: "On 28 [August 1601] they were permitted to establish a factory (*factorij*) here, and Atjehsche envoys, provided with gifts and letters to the Prince, were sent along with two of the ships, which with a full cargo of pepper departed directly from here to Holland" — Dutch original: *28 werd hen toegestaan hier een factorij te stichten en werden Atjehsche gezanten, voorzien van geschenken en brieven aan den Prins, met 2 der schepen, die met een volle lading peper rechtstreeks van hier naar Holland vertrokken, medegegeven*. [[Johan van der Woude|Van der Woude]] adds that the sultan granted the Zeelanders a factory called "Zeelant", and permitted two of the *commiezen* (factors) to travel with his letters of favour to the king of [[Cambay|Cambay]] to open trade with Gujarat.

Three elements of this settlement deserve notice. First, the Dutch factory at Aceh — the first formal Dutch commercial establishment on Sumatran soil — is Le Roy's achievement. The [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis]]–[[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik]]–[[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]] sequence had failed to obtain one; Le Roy obtained it in weeks. Second, the Atjehsche envoys sent with the two returning ships to the Republic constitute the first Acehnese embassy to the Netherlands — a diplomatic counterpart to the Dutch presence at Aceh. The gifts and letters to Prince [[Maurits van Oranje|Maurits]] are the visible tokens of the embassy. Third, the full pepper cargo shipped directly from Aceh to Holland is the commercial seed of the great Dutch pepper trade of the [[Westkust van Sumatra|Westkust]]. The [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Compagnie]] would, after its [[1602|1602]] founding, build its Aceh and Westkust policy on the foundation Le Roy had laid.

## Significance

Le Roy's visit is the pivot of the opening Dutch-Acehnese phase. Where [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]] had died, where [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]] had failed, Le Roy succeeded — and he did so because the Aceh-Portuguese breach had reset the court's politics in his favour. The [[28 August|28 August]] [[1601|1601]] factory is the first Dutch commercial establishment on Sumatran soil, the first Acehnese embassy to the Republic is the first diplomatic exchange between the two powers, and the pepper cargo is the first direct commercial fruit of the relationship.

The episode also marks the moment at which the Acehnese court, freed from the Portuguese alignment, becomes a potential Dutch partner. The [[1602|1602]] founding of the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] would build on this opening; the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (15 February 1641)|1641 Schouten treaty]] would systematise it. The indemnity Le Roy and Bicker undertook to pay — for Van Caerden's seizure of the Arab junks — was the principal commercial-legal consequence of the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden affair]] and the principal instance of the early Dutch companies' willingness to indemnify third-party losses caused by their commanders' unilateral actions. The diplomatic skill with which the indemnity was offered enabled the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission]] to obtain what the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman visit]] and the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit]] had failed to obtain: the unconditional release of the Dutch captives, a working factory, and a reciprocal embassy to the [[Staten-Generaal|Staten-Generaal]]. The mission's success also confirmed the *bewindhebbers*' (directors') view that Aceh pepper was worth pursuing despite the political risks, and that competition among the European nations — Zeelanders, English, French — drove up prices and made the case for a unified company more urgent.
