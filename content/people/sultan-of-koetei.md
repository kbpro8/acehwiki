**Sultan of Koetei** — the Muslim sultan of the *zelfbesturende landschap* (self-governing territory) of Koetei on the east coast of Borneo — is named in Ir. [[F. P. C. S. van der Ploeg|Van der Ploeg]]'s chapter *Mijnbouw* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as the contracting party in the [[1889]] petroleum agreement with [[J. H. Menten|J. H. Menten]] that opened the Borneo side of the Indonesian petroleum industry. The agreement, secured "in 1889" (Van der Ploeg's date), granted Menten a concession for petroleum extraction in a terrain on the Sanga-Sanga river, later extended by further concessions to cover a continuous strip from Koetei to Balikpapan.

## Significance for the Atjeh-adjacent petroleum industry

The Sultan of Koetei's concession to Menten is named in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* as the Borneo counterpart to the Langkat concession of *Zijlker* of [[1884]] on the Sumatra East Coast. The two concessions together — one in the *Landschap* of [[Langkat]] adjacent to [[Acheh|Atjeh]], the other in the *Landschap* of Koetei on Borneo — bracket the late-colonial petroleum industry of the Indies at its two principal points of origin. Menten's Koetei concession was, after his contact with the English capitalist Sir Marcus Samuel (whose Shell Transport and Trading Co. had a petroleum storage and sales business in the East), to become one of the two pillars of the eventual [[Royal Dutch Shell]] merger of [[1903]].

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the Sultan of Koetei's significance is therefore comparative: his concession to Menten is the Borneo parallel to the Sumatra East Coast concessions of the Sultans of [[Deli]], [[Langkat]] and [[Serdang]] — the sultans whose self-governing territories bordered Atjeh to the south-east and whose petroleum and tobacco industries shaped the late-colonial strategic significance of the Atjeh region. The volume's brief mention of the Sultan of Koetei is thus a reminder that the *zelfbesturende landschappen* of the Indies, including those of the Sumatra East Coast, were not isolated polities but participants in a wider late-colonial system of concession-granting to European and American capital.
