**Hans de Decker** (also *De Dekker*) was a Dutch sailor who, after beginning his career in the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] and spending time as a prisoner in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] alongside [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]], entered French service and rose to become "Generaal" of the [[St. Michel affair at Atjeh (1620-1621)|Saint-Malo expedition]] of [[1617]]–[[1618]] whose flagship the *St. Michel* lay at Atjeh in [[1617]]. His career — VOC officer, Atjeh captive, French "General," and at last a fugitive who died on an English ship — spans the entire Dutch-French-English-Atjeh quadrilateral of the early seventeenth century.

## Early VOC service: De Houtman's second expedition (1598)

De Decker had taken part in [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]]'s second expedition of [[1598]] and was, with [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]], taken prisoner at Atjeh in [[1599]]. See [[Death of Cornelis de Houtman at Atjeh (1599)]] and [[Frederik de Houtman]].

## Return to the fatherland and Zeeland service

After his release De Decker returned to the fatherland and took service with the Zeeland Company. He sailed out as *Opperkommies* (senior merchant) and Councillor under Vice-Admiral [[Sebald de Weert|Sebald de Weert]] — eleven days after the founding of the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]]. He touched at Ceylon, stayed on in Atjeh "tot den inkoop van peper" — for the purchase of pepper — and was back in Zeeland by [[1606]].

## French service and the Saint-Malo expedition

Grievances and unpleasantness drove him to France, where he was given the post of "General" in the Saint-Malo expedition — the same expedition that included the ships *St. Michel* and *St. Louis*, owned by Hollanders, sold to the French, fitted out by renegades including [[Lodewijk Heyns|Lodewijk Heyns]], Gerard Leroy, [[Jan Banninck Coeckebakker|Jan Banninck Coeckebakker]], Jan Jansz. Moll and presumably [[Jacob Le Maire|Lemaire]] — "het stel samenzweerders tegen de Nederlandsche Oost-Indische Compagnie" — the gang of conspirators against the Dutch East India Company. The two ships had been signalled already when they lay in Atjeh, where the Admiral's ship had already taken on a full cargo of pepper.

## Atjeh and the voyage to Jakatra

The two ships sailed through the Strait of Malakka, called at Malakka, and were scattered by storm and current. The Governor-General [[Laurens Reaal|Dr. Laurens Reaal]] — "meer jurist dan krachtig bewindsman" (more jurist than strong administrator) — met the Vice-Admiral's ship among the islands before Jakatra and with threats (he had two ships) lifted ten Dutchmen from it. Other Dutch ships waited for the Admiral's ship, which was reduced to a wreck five miles below Jakatra.

## The sloops and the escape

The "General" De Decker came in a sloop with the Skipper and two Hollanders to the ship *Bantam* to request steersmen. Reaal had promised, on handshake, that De Decker would be sent home to the fatherland — a promise that would cost the Company much trouble, hindrance and loss.

On [[17 January]] [[1618]] De Decker came under escort before Bantam. He expected to be put on a seaworthy ship bound for Holland. Learning that he would be transferred to another ship, and that Reaal's word would be overruled by the Councillors of the Indies, he fled with two of his servants to an English ship. From there he was brought to the court of Bantam, where he stayed three days without the Dutch knowing where he was hidden. The *Pangeran* was asked to extradite him, which he "plat uit" (flatly) refused, adding that he was not pleased that the Dutch would do in Bantam what they had done in the Moluccas. He pointed out that he had nothing to do with the jurisdiction of the States-General. "Gelijk had hij" — He was right.

## The English ship and death

To force De Decker out, the Indian Government arrested his ship, the *St. Michel*. The heated conversations of French and English at the Bantam court about the Dutch can be imagined. They worked on the *Pangeran* to act strongly. Meanwhile, he let De Decker escape to the English ship *Charles*. On that ship De Decker died on the homeward voyage. "Het laatste bericht over hem is dat de scheepskist van den overledene wordt opgevraagd" — The last news of him is that the deceased's sea-chest is being claimed. "Een moeitevol en mislukt leven!" — A laborious and failed life!

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, De Decker is significant as a Dutchman who knew the sultanate intimately — twice a prisoner and a pepper-trader there — and who carried that knowledge into French service. The *St. Michel*'s call at Atjeh in [[1617]] (see [[St. Michel affair at Atjeh (1620-1621)]]) and her full cargo of Atjeh pepper made De Decker the connecting figure between the VOC's early Atjeh trade and the French intrusion of the 1610s. His subsequent flight to the English *Charles* and his death at sea close a career that had touched all four European powers — Dutch, French, English, and (as prisoner) Atjehnese — active in the Sumatra pepper trade.
