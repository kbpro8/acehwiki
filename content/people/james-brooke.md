**James Brooke** ([[1803]]–[[1868]]) was a British adventurer who, in [[1841]], conquered North-West Borneo with his crew and had himself proclaimed Rajah — the first of the "White Rajahs of Sarawak." The episode is cited by [[J. Frijling|Ir. J. Frijling]] in his [[1946]] sketch *Een stukje geschiedenis der Indische Buitengewesten* as a parallel instance of British willingness to act unilaterally in the archipelago when the Dutch failed to do so, alongside the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]]. The Dutch government, "gehypnotiseerd en geparalyseerd door haar politick, bleef lijdelijk toezien" — hypnotised and paralysed by its policy, remained a passive spectator.

## The Conquest of 1841

In [[1841]] Brooke conquered North-West Borneo with his crew and had himself proclaimed Rajah. Frijling cites the episode as the most striking instance of the unwillingness of the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] to enforce its claims of sovereignty in the Buitengewesten under the [[Niet-inmengingspolitiek|niet-inmengingspolitiek]]. The Dutch government did nothing; Brooke's Sarawak became a recognised polity and remained under Brooke rule until the Japanese invasion of [[1941]].

## Significance for Atjeh

Brooke's conquest of [[1841]] is, in Frijling's argument, the most striking illustration of the consequences of the Dutch *niet-inmengingspolitiek* for the international standing of the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] in the archipelago. Together with the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]], it formed the international-pressure background to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] and the [[Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy (1872)|Dutch military measures of 1872]]. The episode also illustrates the contrast between the Dutch and British approaches to intervention in the archipelago: where the Dutch abstained, the British — whether officially as in [[1844]], or semi-officially as in Brooke's case — acted.
