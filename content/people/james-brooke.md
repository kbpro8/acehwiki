**James Brooke** ([[1803]]–[[1868]]) was a British adventurer who, in [[1841]], obtained a charter from the [[British Empire]] and, with his crew, conquered north‑west [[Borneo]] and proclaimed himself [[Rajah of Sarawak|Rajah]] of the newly‑named [[Sarawak]] (then called [[Serawak]]). His personal kingdom became the first of the “White Rajahs of Sarawak” and later clashed with the [[Sultanate of Aceh]], which claimed suzerainty over parts of Borneo’s western coast. The episode is cited by [[J. Frijling|Ir. J. Frijling]] in his [[1946]] sketch *Een stukje geschiedenis der Indische Buitengewesten* as a parallel instance of British willingness to act unilaterally in the archipelago when the Dutch failed to do so, alongside the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]]. The Dutch government, “gehypnotiseerd en geparalyseerd door haar politick, bleef lijdelijk toezien” — hypnotised and paralysed by its policy, remained a passive spectator.

## Charter and Establishment

In [[1841]] Brooke secured a royal charter from the [[British Empire]] that authorised him to administer the coastal region of [[Serawak]] (later known as [[Sarawak]]) as a personal fiefdom. The charter granted him the right to collect customs, maintain a militia, and negotiate treaties with local chieftains. Brooke’s appointment marked the first formal British‑backed creation of a quasi‑sovereign state on Borneo’s north‑west coast, setting a precedent for the later “White Rajah” dynasty that would rule until the Japanese invasion of [[1941]].

## The Conquest of [[1841]]

With a small force of Malay and European sailors, Brooke entered the region in early [[1841]] and swiftly subdued rival Malay chiefs who resisted his authority. After a series of naval engagements and negotiated surrenders, Brooke was proclaimed [[Rajah of Sarawak|Rajah]] by his supporters and by the local population that accepted his rule in exchange for protection against piracy and inter‑tribal warfare. The Dutch, adhering to their *[[Niet-inmengingspolitiek|niet‑inmengingspolitiek]]*, did not intervene, allowing Brooke’s nascent state to become a recognised polity under British protection.

## Relations with the [[Sultanate of Aceh]]

Brooke’s expansion threatened the [[Sultanate of Aceh]]’s claimed suzerainty over western Borneo. Diplomatic friction arose as Aceh protested Brooke’s annexations and the establishment of a British‑aligned enclave. The British acquisition of [[Labuan]] in [[1846]] provided a naval base that reinforced Brooke’s position and facilitated his campaigns against local chieftains, some of whom were allied with Aceh. The competition over the lucrative spice trade and smuggling routes linking [[Singapore]], [[Labuan]], and Aceh’s hinterland intensified the rivalry, although no full‑scale war erupted during Brooke’s lifetime.

## Significance for Atjeh and Colonial Policy

Brooke’s 1841 conquest is, in Frijling’s analysis, the most striking illustration of the consequences of the Dutch *niet‑inmengingspolitiek* for the international standing of the [[Netherlands]] in the archipelago. Together with the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]], it formed part of the diplomatic pressure that led to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra‑contract of 1871]] and the subsequent [[Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy (1872)|Dutch military measures of 1872]]. The episode also highlights the contrast between Dutch restraint and British assertiveness: where the Dutch abstained, the British—whether officially as in [[1844]] or semi‑officially as in Brooke’s charter—acted decisively, reshaping the balance of power in the eastern Indies.
