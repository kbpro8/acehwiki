**C. Deykerhoff** — also written *C. Deiykerhoff* — *Generaal-Majoor*, *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*, was the senior Dutch colonial official whom the [[Teuku Umar|Umar]] *bindingspolitiek* of [[1893]]–[[1896]] had been conducted under, and whose "optimistic views about [[Teuku Umar]] ([[T. Djohan]]) before the defection" made him, in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*, the principal casualty of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March]] [[1896]]. Deykerhoff had been visited by the the future [[J. A. Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]] in [[November]] [[1895]], held conferences at his home after the defection, and was "*eervol ontheven van zijne functien als Civiel en Militair Gouverneur*" (honourably relieved of his functions as Civil and Military Governor) upon Vetter's arrival on [[7 April]] [[1896]]. The *Koloniaal Verslag* quoted by Kruisheer offers the most candid colonial assessment of Deykerhoff's tenure: "*Waar de afval van Tockoe Djohan, aanstonds tot so ver strekkende gevolgen aanleiding gaf, dat al het sedert 1893 verkregene, was, as it were, set on loose screws, moest wel ernstige twijfel rijzen, of de Civiele en Militaire Gouverneur den toestand te voren wel inderdaad juist beoordeeld had*" (Where the defection of Toekoe Djohan gave rise to such far-reaching consequences that everything obtained since 1893 was, as it were, set on loose screws, serious doubt had to arise whether the Civil and Military Governor had in fact correctly judged the situation beforehand…).

## Pre-Defection Tenure and the Umar Bindingspolitiek

Deykerhoff's tenure as Civiel en Militair Gouverneur covered the period of the Dutch experiment with binding Umar to the colonial administration through salaries, arms, and titles. The [[Government Resolution (Gouvernementsbesluit) of 5 September 1893, No. 1 geheim|Government Resolution of 5 September 1893, No. 1 geheim]] had authorised Umar's appointment under the name *Toekoe Djohan Pahalawan en Panglima Prang besar*; the [[Oath of Loyalty and Confirmation Act of 30 September 1893|oath of loyalty of 30 September 1893]] had formalised the relationship; Umar had been given additional leadership of the [[XXVI Moekims]] on [[21 October]] [[1893]], the conquest of [[Ana' Galong]] on [[30 November]] [[1893]] had demonstrated the legion's military value, and the [[Government Resolution of 19 January 1896, No. 9|Government Resolution of 19 January 1896, No. 9]] had appointed Umar *hoeloebalang* of [[Lepong]]. Kruisheer's observation that Deykerhoff "held optimistic views about T. Djohan (Toekoe Oemar)" before the defection indicates that he had been the principal advocate of the binding policy and that he had continued to vouch for Umar's loyalty even as warning signs accumulated. The [[Controleur Gisolf|controleur Gisolf]] had "warned the general about Oemar's more than suspicious attitude" before the defection, and the [[Ketjihik Oemar]] informant had reported Umar and [[Tjoet Nja Din]]'s "busy meetings with the heads of the clergy" — but Deykerhoff had apparently discounted these warnings. The visit of the Vetter in [[November]] [[1895]] — four months before the defection — may itself have been an early indication that the colonial government was losing confidence in Deykerhoff's handling of the Umar relationship.

## The Defection Crisis and Honourable Relief

The defection of Umar on [[29 March]] [[1896]] was, in the colonial assessment which Kruisheer quotes, the comprehensive vindication of the warnings which Deykerhoff had discounted. The immediate military consequences — the sieges of [[Biloel]] and [[Lam-Koenjit]], the collapse of the eastern *blokhuizen*, the progressive evacuation of the buitenlinie — confirmed that the Umar policy had been built on sand, and Deykerhoff held "conferences after the defection at his home" in an attempt to manage the crisis. The colonial government's response was the dispatch of Vetter as Regeeringscommissaris — a senior officer with full authority whose arrival would inevitably entail Deykerhoff's removal. The relief was, however, honourable — Deykerhoff was not cashiered but relieved of his functions, a treatment which reflected both his personal standing and the political sensitivity of publicly acknowledging the failure of the Umar policy. The *Koloniaal Verslag*'s published verdict — that "*ernstige twijfel [moest] rijzen, of de Civiele en Militaire Gouverneur den toestand te voren wel inderdaad juist beoordeeld had*" — was as close to an official admission of failure as the colonial government would make, and the abolition of the Civiel en Militair Gouverneur office (replaced by the Regeeringscommissaris under Vetter, and then restored only on Vetter's departure under [[J. W. Stemfoort|Stemfoort]]) confirmed that Deykerhoff's institutional inheritance had been comprehensively reorganised. Umar himself would later recall, in his [[Teuku Umar's third letter to the Governor of Atjeh (23 April 1896)|third letter of 23 April 1896]], that Deykerhoff had promised him a "*kruis*" (Royal decoration) — a detail which both confirms Deykerhoff's personal involvement in the binding policy and explains the depth of Umar's grievance when the decoration was not forthcoming.

## Significance

Deykerhoff's significance in *ATJEH '96* lies in his embodiment of the colonial official whose personal optimism about Umar had underwritten the binding policy and whose honourable relief marked the institutional admission of that policy's failure. The *Koloniaal Verslag*'s verdict — quoted at length by Kruisheer — is the most candid published colonial assessment of the Deykerhoff era, and the dispatch of Vetter as Regeeringscommissaris in his place was the principal structural consequence of the defection crisis. His portrait, included among the four *portretten* of the balso, confirms his continuing prominence in the colonial memory of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] despite the failure of his signature policy.
