**The Familie Veenhuizen** — a father with three sons — settled a few years before Broersma's visit at kilometre 98 of the [[Gajo-weg|Gajo-weg]], between [[Lampahan|Lampahan]] and [[Takengon|Takengon]], on a *paja* of twenty-five *bouw* which they called *[[Paja Toempi|Paja Toempi]]*. The site lies at the foot of a mountain-slope, down which the washed-down soil comes as a welcome addition to the garden; but water is largely lacking, which is unfavourable for market-gardening.

## The Paja Toempi Enterprise
The family began in [[1921]] with the cultivation of potatoes alongside various vegetables. Disease appeared in the potatoes, but the Dutch seed-potatoes, imported with difficulty, held up well. The market for the produce is in Atjeh itself: the family brings its fruit and vegetables by lorry to [[Bireuen|Bireuen]] — a journey of five to six hours — and there loads them into the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh-tram]]. Lorry-transport takes five to six hours, the tram-journey usually another day, so that the deliveries cannot consist of leaf-greens, which would wilt, but of beets, turnips, cabbage, peas, carrots and aubergines. The market is thus restricted and lies at a considerable distance. Potato-cultivation might find a wider market, but the small enterprise is not in a position to undertake large deliveries.

The family expects soon to receive three hundred *bouw* of forest-ground at kilometres 95 and 96 of the Gajo-weg, and intends to plant coffee there. The Gajos in this region also grow vegetables and coffee, and at Takengon itself the inland administration has begun a garden under more favourable conditions than those of Paja Toempi, from which it tries to supply the needs of the bivouac and of the few Europeans there.

## Significance
The Veenhuizen family's Paja Toempi is one of the earliest European planting-enterprises in the Gajo interior, and is the type of the small highland garden — potatoes, vegetables, and a little coffee — which the colonial government hoped would multiply along the Gajo-weg. The enterprise demonstrates both the possibilities and the limits of highland cultivation in the Gajo country: the soil is good, but transport-costs are high and the market is small.
