**Aḥmad ar-Rifāʿī** was the founder of the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]] Sufi order, one of the principal ṭuruq of orthodox Sunni mysticism. His tomb at [[Mocha]] — the coffee port of Yemen on the Red Sea — is reverenced, and his order was the silsila into which [[Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] was initiated by his shaikh [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]]. The Rifāʿiyya was the spiritual genealogy by which Raniri, on his arrival in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]], claimed the authority of an orthodox mystic against the [[Wujudiyyah]] of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]].

## The Rifāʿiyya

The Rifāʿiyya — also called the *Baṭāʾiḥiyya* — was founded in the marshlands of southern Iraq in the twelfth century C.E. and spread rapidly through the Muslim world. By the sixteenth century it had reached [[Gudjarat|Gujarat]], where it was transmitted to [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]] by [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]] at [[Surat]]. From Bā Shaybān the silsila was transmitted to two of the principal Malay-Indonesian Sufis of the seventeenth century — [[Raniri]] and [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]] — and so reached the Malay-Indonesian archipelago.

## The tomb at Mocha

[[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] records, in the introduction to the [[1955]] edition of Raniri's two Malay treatises, that Raniri "was initiated into the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]], founded by the saint Aḥmad Rifāʿī, whose tomb at [[Mocha]] is reverenced." The association of the founder's tomb with Mocha — rather than with the marshlands of southern Iraq where the order was actually founded — is itself a document of the devotional geography of the Rifāʿiyya as it was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world: a geography in which the founder's sanctity was located at a Red Sea port frequented by Indian and Indonesian pilgrims and traders, and so made accessible to the Malay-Indonesian devotees of the order.

## Significance

Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī is, through his order, one of the principal spiritual ancestors of [[Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] and of [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]]. The Rifāʿiyya gave both Malay-Indonesian Sufis the authority of an orthodox silsila by which they were able to confront the [[Wujudiyyah]] mystics of the Malay-Indonesian courts — in [[Acheh]] in the case of Raniri, in [[Makassar]] in the case of Shaikh Yūsuf — and to claim for their own teaching the sanction of a ṭarīqa that had its origin in the heartlands of Sunni Islam. The transmission of the Rifāʿiyya from the marshlands of Iraq to the harbours of Gujarat and the courts of Acheh and Makassar is one of the principal channels by which the orthodox Sufi scholasticism of the Arab and Persian worlds was carried to the Malay-Indonesian archipelago in the seventeenth century.
