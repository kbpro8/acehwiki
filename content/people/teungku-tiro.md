In the Hikayat Prang Gompeuni, Teungku Tiro (identified with Cheh Saman) is portrayed as the great ulama-leader of the holy war against the Dutch. He arrived at Lam Panaih with followers chiefly from Pidie, required re-conversion of those from Dutch-controlled territory, erected forts, and organized great feasts. He was ultimately poisoned, and on his deathbed adjured his followers, especially his son Mat Amin, to be guided by the ulamas.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teungku Tiro

**Teungku Tiro** is the hereditary title borne by the most prominent *ulama* of [[Tiro]], a *gampong* of [[Pidie]] whose reputation as a seat of religious teaching and burial-place of distinguished scholars gave it a privileged position (*bibeuëh*) in Achehnese life. The most famous bearer of the title, also called **Teungku di Tiro** or **Teungku Chi'** ("the Old"), was the recognised head of the Achehnese religious party and commander-in-chief of the holy war against the Dutch from the time of the "concentration" until his death in January [[1891]]. The same title had earlier been borne by **Teungku Tiro Muhamat Amin**, who died in December [[1885]] and was succeeded by his collateral relative [[Cheh Saman (Shaikh Samman)|Cheh Saman (Shaikh Samman)]], the man under whom the office attained its greatest prominence. In what follows, "Teungku Tiro" refers to this second and more famous holder of the title, Cheh Saman, unless otherwise stated.

The village of [[Tiro]] owed its reputation partly to the teaching of sacred subjects of which it was the seat, and partly to the number of distinguished *ulamas* it had produced. These *ulamas*, if not drawn elsewhere by marriages with women of other *gampongs*, returned to their native place in their declining years, so that many of them lay buried there. To the sanctity of their tombs and the constant presence of influential *ulamas*, the place owed its peculiar privileged position. The neighbouring chiefs made over to the *ulamas* the maintenance of law and order, so that *adat* had in Tiro much less significance than elsewhere; they respected it as a place of refuge for those involved in internal feuds, and refrained from hostilities in the neighbourhood of its *meunasah*. Even members of hostile parties could meet there without fear of disturbance.

When Teungku Tiro Muhamat Amin (the then *Teungku Chi' di Tiro*) died in December [[1885]], he was succeeded by his collateral relative [[Cheh Saman (Shaikh Samman)|Cheh Saman (Shaikh Samman)]], who had long been his right-hand man. As the son of the deceased, though a good scholar, was still too young to take his father's place, the designation of Teungku Tiro passed to Teungku Saman. Favoured by the progress of the war, Cheh Saman gained for himself a unique position, in which the Sultan's *chab sikureung* (nine-fold seal) could do no more than set the stamp of officialdom on power he had already won.

Teungku Tiro worked himself up, by means described by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], to the position of commander-in-chief in the war against the Gompeuni (Dutch). He wished to arm himself to some extent against the jealousy of the *adat*-chiefs by an official authorisation of the Sultan, whereby he might be placed on a level with the *uleebalangs* — or, as he preferred to interpret it, over their heads. The "court" at [[Keumala]] gave him, cautiously enough, a deed of appointment which in appearance made no inroad on the rights of the *adat*-chiefs, since it limited the province of the *ulama* to religious matters. At the same time, all parties concerned were well aware that in the view of the *ulamas* everything came under the head of religion, especially antagonism to unrighteous *adats*, the waging of war against unbelievers, and the administration of justice. Even after this formal appointment Teungku Tiro submitted to no legal dictation from Keumala, and refused on more than one occasion to satisfy the Sultan's demands for money contributions.

In his letters to the Dutch Government Teungku Tiro always tried to show the superiority of the power of the *ulamas*. In one of his pamphlets he expressed astonishment that the Gompeuni had from the very commencement been so eager to obtain concessions from the Sultan. It should, he said, have been remembered that the Sultan could do nothing without consulting [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Kali]], [[Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa]], the [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] and the [[Imeum of Lueng Bata]]; that these four were in their turn dependent upon the decision of the three *panglimas* of the *sagis*; that the latter had no power without the acquiescence of the seven *kawoms* (the traditional expression for the Achehnese people); and that the people themselves could only act in accordance with the determination of the *ulamas*, "who derive their knowledge from Allah and his Apostle."

Teungku Tiro knew that money is the life and soul even of a holy war. He pushed on with the utmost zeal the collections of the *haqq sabil* — the portion of the *zakat* tax set apart for the holy war — and urged all the chiefs at least to give him money contributions towards the good cause, even if they would take no personal part in the conflict. Whoever appeared backward in this duty became exposed to the enmity of his followers. When the *adat* chiefs protested, his answer was that he was desirous of giving full weight to their *adat* qualifications — but these he never defined, and he always submitted any questions that arose to the test of the religious law. He denied that he wished to deprive them of a handsbreadth of their territory, but as the representative of religion he required of their subjects a strict obedience to the law of Allah. Thus the Teungku succeeded without difficulty in obtaining the control of a never-empty coffer for the purposes of the war — an achievement no *uleebalang* would ever have conceived.

His principal rivals among the *ulamas* were [[Habib Samalanga]], the sayyid who came to [[Great Acheh]] to direct the jihad especially in the [[XXVI Mukims]], and [[Teungku Kutakarang]], an active but peculiar man. The latter had, even before the coming of the Dutch, been banished from the capital in the Sultan's name for the strangeness of his decisions on points of law; in the war he worked at first side by side with — or even under — Teungku Tiro, but in the circle of his intimates spoke contemptuously of the great man as "Leube Saman." Teungku Kutakarang opposed Teungku Tiro's teaching that the prohibition against wearing gold or silk applied to combatants in the holy war, and classed all Teungku Tiro's good works — building of mosques, repair of chapels, great *kanduris* — as mere "louse-questions," arguing that the money should have been used to erect forts all along the *linie* and to fit out a fleet to harass the enemy by sea.

Teungku Tiro did not concern himself about the insignificant Sultan, nor, except in so far as was absolutely necessary, about the *uleebalangs*. As the representative of religious law he could assume full powers, and none dared show him open hostility; the few cases of apparent enmity on the part of some of the chiefs were really directed against some *panglima* (military leader) of the *ulama* on account of excessive interference with traditional privileges. He died in January [[1891]], still in the full vigour of life, after a sudden illness. He is said to have expressed the conviction that poison had been administered to him by some agent of his son [[Mat Amin]] — a *teungku* by title and a guerrilla chief by trade, who had lived openly with another man's wife and had been forbidden access to his father's presence. Notwithstanding this, Teungku Tiro had Mat Amin summoned to his bedside when dying, and solemnly warned him to forsake his evil ways and to seek the counsel of devout *ulamas*.

Teungku Tiro had no true successor. His son Mat Amin was killed in action in [[1896]] when the Dutch troops captured the fortress of [[Aneu Galong|Aneu' Galong]]. Two other sons — [[Teungku Beb]] (died [[1900]]) and [[Teungku Mahidin]] — together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died [[1901]]), much respected for his piety and learning, continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the *sabil* contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]; but Great Acheh no longer furnished a field for their labour.
