**Jacobus Pel** was a officer in the [[Dutch East Indies Army]] who served as a battalion commander during the [[Second Aceh Expedition]] and subsequently became the Civil and Military Governor of Aceh from [[1874]] until his death in [[1876]].

## Military Role in the Second Aceh Expedition

During the [[Second Aceh Expedition]] in [[1873]]–[[1874]], Lieutenant-Colonel Pel commanded the 14th Infantry Battalion. He led key tactical maneuvers, including the march through the nipa swamps that resulted in the [[Capture of Peunayong]] on [[18 December]] [[1873]] and the [[Battle of Ketapan Doewa]] on [[15 February]] [[1874]].

## Administration of Aceh (1874–1876)

When General [[Jan van Swieten]] departed Aceh in [[April]] [[1874]], he left Colonel Pel in command of the occupational garrison at [[Kraton of Aceh|Kota Raja]] with a force of 3,200 men. Faced with relentless guerrilla attacks from Acehnese fighters led by [[Tuanku Hashim]] and [[Panglima Polim]], Pel abandoned Van Swieten's purely passive policy.

Pel initiated an active defense system, building fortified outposts along the river trade routes and constructing a protective perimeter from [[Krueng Raya]] to [[Krueng Raba]] to isolate Great Aceh from coastal trade. He also oversaw the construction of the military railway connecting Kota Raja to the port of [[Ulee Lheue]].

## Death and Legacy

On [[25 February]] [[1876]], General Pel died suddenly from an aneurism in the bivouac at Lamjong while leading operations in the field. He was widely respected within the colonial military for his energetic leadership and tactical innovations. His defensive ring strategy established the foundation for the subsequent [[Concentrated Line]].

## Account from Verstege's *Een beroep op het Nederlandsche volk* (1886)

[[J. J. W. E. Verstege|Verstege]] defends Pel's memory against its most tenacious misuse: the claim of the champions of the [[Afwachtende houding|afwachtende houding]] that Pel in his letter of [[30 April]] [[1875]] had spoken for their system. The pamphlet prints large parts of the letter itself (see [[Pel's missive to the Governor-General (30 April 1875)]]), proves that it closed with the words "Consolidatie van onze actueele positie op Atjeh; voortzetting der vijandelijkheden zoo spoedig als onze strijdkrachten ons daartoe in staat stellen" ("Consolidation of our actual position at Atjeh; resumption of hostilities as soon as our fighting forces put us in a position to do so"), and shows that the declaration of general [[Jan van Swieten|van Swieten]] ([[1880]]), that Pel was only "omgepraat" (talked over) towards the offensive during a three months' leave at Batavia in mid-[[1875]], is disproved by the order of dates: the letter is of 30 April, while Pel departed for Batavia only on [[13 June]] [[1875]]. Also against the reproach — acknowledged even by some of Pel's warmest champions, after [[B. C. P. Kielstra|Kielstra]] — that Pel had fallen too often into the error of making too many posts (or letting them exist too long), Verstege opposes the testimony of the position at the end of April [[1875]] (see [[Verstege's folding map of Groot-Atjeh (1886)]]), when the posts of his guarded ring gave within it a complete safety that the concentrated line of [[1885]] never gave. From Pel's days the pamphlet further cites the day's developments after [[Departure of Jan van Swieten from Atjeh (April 1874)|van Swieten's departure]]: the same evening the enemy south and east of the Kraton; the [[Punishment of Marassa (27 April 1874)|punishment of Marassa]] of 27 April, van Swieten's letters from the Netherlands and the reinforcement demands of 1874; and from his last days the Dagorder of [[8 February]] [[1876]] addressed to the troops on the return from the conquest of the [[XXV Moekims]], and the testimonial of the member [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]], spoken in the Tweede Kamer on the arrival of the sad news of his death ([[24 February]] [[1876]]), "dat ons vaderland door zijn dood het grootste verlies had geleden, dat het door het sterven van één persoon lijden kon" ("that our fatherland had suffered by his death the greatest loss it could experience by the decease of one person"). It was the same Pel, Verstege reminds, who told the men, that the enemy, who "meer dan twee jaren" had kept the Dutch shut up inside a narrow ring of posts round the Kraton, was forced back only by waging war.
