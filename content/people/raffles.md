**Raffles** — Sir **Thomas Stamford Bingley Raffles** ([[1781]]–[[1826]]) — was the British East India Company official, founder of [[Singapore|Singapore]], and Lieutenant-Governor of the Dutch East Indies during the British interregnum ([[1811]]–[[1816]]), whose diplomatic intervention in the affairs of [[Acheh|Acheh]] forms one of the principal strands in the pre-history of the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]]. He is twice invoked by Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese*: once in the Introduction, for the [[Raffles Treaty with Acheh|treaty of 1819]] made with the pretender Sultan [[Johor Allum|Johor Allum]], and once in the body of the work for his celebrated dictum that "the sultan of Acheh was revered throughout his whole kingdom, but obeyed nowhere".

The treaty which Raffles concluded with Acheh in [[1819]] was, in Snouck's account, one of a long series of British attempts to establish friendly relations with the sultanate — attempts which "were overridden in the most insulting way". The pretender with whom Raffles negotiated was "Johor Allum as king of Acheen", concerning whom the Chairman of the East India Company wrote to Bengal on the [[4 August 1824|4th of August 1824]]: "that chief, so far as we can collect from your correspondence, not having possessed an established authority in the country which he assumed to represent, has never been in a situation to maintain the relations into which he entered". The treaty thus rested, from the outset, on a misapprehension of the nature of Achehnese sovereignty: a single treaty with a single "Sultan" could not bind the countless uleebalangs of the Three Sagis, still less the chiefs of the dependent coast-states.

Snouck draws the details of the Raffles treaty from a "recent essay upon the treaty made with Acheh by Raffles in 1819, in which long extracts have been quoted from the archives of the India Office" — namely, the essay of [[P. H. van der Kemp|P. H. van der Kemp]] in the *Bijdragen van het Koninklijk Instituut voor de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde van Nederlandsch Indië*, vol. LI, pp. 159–240 (The Hague, [[1900]]). The episode belonged to the wider story of Anglo-Dutch rivalry in the archipelago that was temporarily regulated by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]], under which the Netherlands guaranteed the security of trade and shipping in Acheh, and Great Britain undertook to respect the independence of the country. As Snouck observes, the Dutch Government "with great lack of foresight" thus guaranteed the security of trade with "its fanatical and treacherous population, turbulent and warlike to a degree unknown among the other races of the Archipelago", and made the further stipulation "which rendered its undertaking impossible to carry out" — namely, that it would respect the independence of the country.

Raffles's celebrated description of the sultan of Acheh as "revered throughout his whole kingdom, but obeyed nowhere" is quoted by Snouck in a footnote to the chapter on the Dalam, in illustration of the practical impotence of the later sultans. "This feeling of awe", Snouck writes, "has within the memory of man always been coupled with the conviction that the sole sovereign prince of the country exercised no perceptible influence on the conduct of affairs, and was in fact but an expensive luxury like the documents sealed with the *chab sikureuʾeng*". The dictum of Raffles is the more authoritative because Raffles had himself, during his lieutenancy of Java, been in a position to observe at first hand the practical working of native sovereignty in the archipelago; and his verdict on Acheh stands as the contemporary equivalent of the diagnosis of [[Captain Francis Light|Francis Light]] and [[James Price|James Price]] from a generation earlier.

## Account from *Dispereert Niet Vol. XI* (1946) — Raffles

**Raffles** (Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles) was a British colonial administrator and founder of **[[Singapore]]**. He played a pivotal role in the history of **[[Sumatra]]** and **[[Aceh]]** in the period after the Napoleonic Wars, often acting in direct competition with Dutch interests.

### Role in Sumatra

Raffles was appointed Lieutenant-Governor of **[[Benkoelen]]** (Bencoolen) in [[1817]], a British possession on the west coast of **[[Sumatra]]**. From this base, he attempted to expand British influence throughout the island, often in conflict with the re-established Dutch authority.

### The Singapore Coup

In **[[1819]]**, Raffles engineered the establishment of a British settlement at **[[Singapore]]**, which he had identified as a key strategic point for controlling the **[[Strait of Malacca]]**. The Netherlands protested, claiming Singapore fell within its sphere of influence, but Britain maintained control. The dispute was resolved by the **[[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]]**.

### The Treaty of 1819 with Aceh

During his time in Sumatra, Raffles had concluded a treaty with the Sultan of Aceh, granting Britain special rights in the sultanate. This treaty was abrogated by the Treaty of London (1824), but its existence illustrates Raffles's ambitions in the region.

### Legacy

Raffles is remembered as the founder of Singapore and a key figure in the expansion of British influence in Southeast Asia. His actions in Sumatra and the Strait of Malacca contributed to the division of the Malay archipelago into British and Dutch spheres.
