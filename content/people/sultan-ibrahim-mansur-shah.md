**Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah** — known in Achehnese pronunciation as **Manso Shah**, and in the formal genealogical style of his house as **Alaedin Mansur Shah** — was an [[Sultan|Achehnese sultan]] of the decadent period of the [[Sultanate of Acheh|sultanate]], who reigned in the middle decades of the nineteenth century (the sources differ as to the exact dates: one passage of *The Achehnese* gives [[1838]]–[[1870]], another [[1858]]–[[1870]]). He was the father of [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot|Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan]], the last sultan recognised by the Achehnese before the final establishment of Dutch rule over the kingdom. His name appears in the centre of the [[Sarakata|ninefold seal]] of the sultanate and his mediation (*afwah*) is invoked in the preamble of sultans' letters patent (*sarakata*); at his instance, the religious-political leader [[Teungku Kutakarang|Teungku Kuta Karang]] (Shaikh Abbas) wrote the *Taj-ul-mulk*, an important compendium of Islamic mystic arts, prognostications, and native medicine.

## The Reign and the Date Discrepancy

Snouck Hurgronje gives two different dates for the reign of Mansur Shah in *The Achehnese*. One passage gives [[1838]]–[[1870]] — a reign of more than thirty years — and styles him the father of Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan. Another passage, in connection with the disputed succession to the *panglimaship* of the [[XXVI Mukims]], names him as **Ibrahim Manso Shah** reigning [[1858]]–[[1870]]. The first passage explicitly notes that the sultan of 1838–1870 "is apparently identical with the [[Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah|Ibrahim Manso Shah]] named elsewhere in the text as reigning 1858–1870," so that the discrepancy is recognised in the source itself. The two dates are reconcilable only on the hypothesis that one figure is wrong, or that the sultan ruled under some form of divided or delegated authority for an earlier period; Snouck does not resolve the question. The merger of the two entries here follows Snouck's own identification, but the source discrepancy is preserved.

The reign of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah fell entirely within the decadent period of the sultanate. The descendants of the great Sultan [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] had long since ceased to exercise effective authority beyond the immediate neighbourhood of the capital, and the territorial chiefs — the *uleebalangs* of the three *sagis* and the *panglima sagi* of the highlands — had become the real wielders of power in [[Great Acheh]]. The Sultan of Acheh had by this time become little more than *primus inter pares* among the chiefs of Great Acheh, and even his formal recognition of a new *panglima* or *uleebalang* was no more than the stamp set upon a settlement already reached by the chiefs and people of the district concerned.

## The Ninefold Seal and the *Sarakata*

The *chab sikureu'eng* — the ninefold seal of the sultans — as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] explains, consists of a large central circle bearing the name of the reigning sultan, surrounded by eight smaller circles each containing the name of a celebrated predecessor. The choice of the eight names rests with the reigning sultan, though the names of the great sultans of the past and of the immediate predecessors of the reigning king are never omitted. The four names that recur on virtually every seal are [[Sultan Sayyidi al-Mukamnal]] (Alaedin al-Qahhar), [[Sultan Meukuta Alam]] (Iskandar Muda), [[Sultan Tajul-alam]] (Sapiatodin), and [[Sultan Ahmat Shah]].

Mansur Shah's appearance among the eight namesakes on his son's seal reflects his position as the immediate predecessor of the pretender-sultan. The preamble of the letters patent (*sarakata*) granted by Daud Shah Juhan to [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] on 4 Rabi' al-Awwal 1307 H ([[1889]]) explicitly invokes the mediation of "His late Majesty Alaedin Manso Shah", alongside [[Sultan Meukuta Alam]], [[Sultan Tajul-alam]], [[Sultan Ahmat Shah]], [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]], [[Sultan Alaedin Muhamat Shah]], and [[Sultan Jauhar Alain Shah]]. In the formal documents of his house, Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah is thus remembered chiefly as the father of the last sultan. His son styles himself in the 1889 *sarakata* "son of the late Sultan Alaedin Manso Shah Juhan, the Blessed, Allah's shadow in the world." Through this formula the memory of Manso Shah remained attached, in the formal style of the dying sultanate, to the last Achehnese effort to express the prerogatives of royalty.

## The Disputed Succession to the *Panglimaship* of the XXVI Mukims

The reign of Ibrahim Manso Shah is mentioned in *The Achehnese* chiefly in connection with the [[Disputed succession to the panglimaship of the XXVI Mukims|disputed succession to the *panglimaship* of the XXVI Mukims]], one of the three *sagis* (territorial divisions) of [[Great Acheh]], which took place during his reign and is characteristic of the constitutional disorder of the late Sultanate.

The *panglima* of the [[XXVI Mukims]] had long been a person of small importance. When the holder of the title died during the reign of Ibrahim Manso Shah, one [[Teuku Muda Lampaseh]] was, according to the *adat*, the proper successor; he was accordingly, after reporting himself in the *Dalam*, solemnly recognised by the Sultan as *panglima*. Even before he had quitted the *Dalam*, however, the firing of guns was heard from the direction of the XXVI Mukims, and it appeared on enquiry that the people of that district, who objected to Teuku Muda Lampaseh on the ground that he was an opium-smoker, had installed a younger brother of the deceased *panglima* as the latter's successor. This was [[Teuku Chut Lamreueng]], father of the later *panglima* [[Teuku Nya' Banta]], who is also called Teuku Lamreueng.

The Sultan knew no better way out of the difficulty than to recognise the second aspirant as well, so that there were then two *panglimas* in the XXVI Mukims. This doubled *panglimaship* is one of the clearest examples of the inability of the late Sultanate to enforce its own decisions even in matters of succession within the *sagis* of Great Acheh itself. The Sultan's authority was then so weak that he could not enforce his chosen candidate (Teuku Muda Lampaseh) against the wishes of the people of the district, and the eventual compromise produced the [[Disputed succession to the panglimaship of the XXVI Mukims|double panglimaship]] of Teuku Lampaseh and Teuku Lamreueng.

At the commencement of the [[Acheh War]] both chiefs fled to [[Keumala]], but Teuku Lamreueng in the end re-established himself in his territory and tendered his submission to the Dutch Government; for this he was murdered in [[Pidie]], whither he had gone to fetch his family, by the adherents of Teuku Lampaseh. His infant son escaped the same fate through the help of a faithful servant, and was afterwards recognised by the Government as Panglima of the XXVI Mukims.

## Patron of the *Taj-ul-mulk*

At the instance of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah, the religious-political leader [[Teungku Kutakarang|Teungku Kuta Karang]] (Shaikh Abbas) wrote the *Taj-ul-mulk*, an important compendium of Islamic mystic arts, prognostications, and native medicine. The commission of such a work by a sultan of the decadent period is itself notable, in that it shows the continued cultural — if not political — authority of the throne as a patron of religious-scientific literature, even at the moment when the Sultan's effective authority over the territorial chiefs of the *sagis* had collapsed.

## Snouck Hurgronje's Anecdote of the Drunken Frenzy

Snouck Hurgronje's portrait of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah is brief but pointed. From his widow Snouck learned that the sultan had once murdered his own child in a fit of drunken frenzy. The anecdote is introduced in *The Achehnese* Vol. I to illustrate the prevalence of intoxicating liquors in the [[Dalam]], the royal palace at [[Banda Acheh|Banda Acheh]], notwithstanding the honoured Achehnese tradition by which any member of the Sultan's family with the reputation of being even a moderate opium-smoker was excluded from the succession. The open presence of strong drink in the Dalam thus coexisted, paradoxically, with a severe *adat* against the use of opium in the royal house. The anecdote, while personal, is offered by Snouck as symptomatic of the moral and constitutional decay of the late Sultanate.

## Succession and the End of the Sultanate

His son and successor, the child [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]], was elevated to the sultanate in [[1879]] (A.H. 1296) after the death of [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]], and ruled — at least nominally — from the sultan's fortress at [[Keumala]] until his [[Submission of Tuanku Muhamat Dawot|submission to the Dutch]]. The son lived to see the destruction of the Dalam and the establishment of the Dutch colonial government in [[Kuta Raja]]. Through the son's long pretender-ship, the name of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah continued to be invoked in the formal style of the sultanate's last documents, long after the practical authority of his house had disappeared.

## Significance

The reign of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah thus coincides with the period of the most rapid decay of the Sultanate's effective authority. The Sultan's inability to settle a disputed succession within his own capital territory — and his recourse to the compromise of recognising both claimants — exemplifies the constitutional principle, repeatedly stressed by Snouck Hurgronje, that the Sultan of Acheh had by this time become little more than *primus inter pares* among the chiefs of Great Acheh. His patronage of the *Taj-ul-mulk* shows the survival of the sultanate's cultural and religious functions even as its political power collapsed; the anecdote of the drunken frenzy shows the moral counterpart of that collapse; and his son's career, from the sultanate of 1879 to the submission to the Dutch, shows the final extinction of the political reality to which Mansur Shah's name remained, in formal style, attached.
