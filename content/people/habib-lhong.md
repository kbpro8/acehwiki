**Habib Lhong** was a [[Kling (concept)|Kling]] (Indian/Hadhrami Muslim foreigner) [[Habib (concept)|Habib]] — a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad — and "the militant element" in his alliance with [[Teungkoe Panté Gelimah]] (the "*opruiende element*") whose career in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* spans the religious-political-military nexus of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] in the first semester of [[1896]]. Kruisheer records that Lhong "had promised help to the [[Pretender Sultan|pretender sultan]] in [[Pedir]]", and at the head of a band of approximately 500 men he had gone via [[Metareum]], [[Andeue]] and [[Lala]] to [[Keumala]], "burning kampongs along the way, and restored the sultan to his old residence, avenging the sultan's forced flight of the previous year". He was one of the three leaders — alongside [[T. Mad Amin]] and [[Panglima Hassan]] — of the approximately 250-man force that engaged the [[Patrol of Captain Van Blokland (7 March 1896)|patrol of Captain Van Blokland on 7 March]] [[1896]] at [[Ana' Batée]], and he was killed in action at [[Senelop]] on [[17 April]] [[1896]] during the [[Battle of Senelop (17 April 1896)|battle of Senelop]]. Kruisheer also notes, in a retrospective passage, that [[Teungkoe Lam-Oeri]] "had previously pushed forward the Kling Habib Lhong (killed [19] April '96 at Senelop) as a figurehead" — an indication that Lhong's religious-military authority had been cultivated and instrumentalised by other religious figures in the [[III Moekims Ateuh|III Moekims Ateuh]] area.

## The Kling Habib and the Restoration of the Pretender Sultan

The Kling community in [[Aceh]] comprised Muslim foreigners — typically of Indian, Hadhrami, or Afghan origin — who were integrated into the religious and commercial life of the sultanate while retaining a distinct identity. As a Habib (a descendant of the Prophet), Lhong carried the religious authority of his lineage, and as a Kling he occupied the liminal status of the foreign Muslim in Achehnese society. Kruisheer records that he had "promised help to the pretender sultan in Pedir", and that he fulfilled this promise by leading approximately 500 men via [[Metareum]], [[Andeue]] and [[Lala]] to [[Keumala]], burning kampongs along the way. The campaign "restored the sultan to his old residence, avenging the sultan's forced flight of the previous year" — that is, it reversed an earlier setback which had driven the pretender Sultan from [[Keumala]], the traditional capital of the inland sultanate. The choice of route — through the inland villages of the [[Pidië|Pedir]] region — and the burning of kampongs along the way reflect the military logic of a punitive expedition intended to demonstrate the Sultan's restored authority. Kruisheer notes, however, that the pretender Sultan was "by his own countrymen in Pedir blijkbaar niet so hoog aangeschreven stond" (apparently not held in high regard by his own countrymen in Pedir), an assessment which casts doubt on the political durability of the restoration which Lhong had achieved.

## Engagement of 7 March 1896 and Death at Senelop on 17 April 1896

Lhong's most consequential military engagement was the [[Patrol of Captain Van Blokland (7 March 1896)|patrol of 7 March 1896]], in which approximately 250 men under his command — alongside [[T. Mad Amin]] and [[Panglima Hassan]] — engaged the patrol of [[H. F. T. van Blokland|Kapitein Van Blokland]] of the [[15e Bataljon]] from [[Ana' Galong]]. The patrol had set out along the kampong edges of [[Ana' Batée]], [[Klieng]] and the *sawah* toward [[Sibreh]], and a heavy five-hour engagement developed against a superior enemy. Van Blokland "personally fought with sabre", and "het gelukte hem persoonlijk met de sabel twee tegenstanders neer te slaan, of which een nagenoeg in tweeën gedeeld werd" (it was his personal fortune with the sabre to strike down two opponents, one of whom was nearly cut in two). Lhong's role in this engagement — as one of the three principal commanders — established his military credentials and confirmed his presence in the western [[XXII Moekims]] theatre. His death at [[Senelop]] on [[17 April]] [[1896]] during the [[Battle of Senelop (17 April 1896)|battle of Senelop]] — in which a Dutch column under [[J. B. van Heutsz|Lt.-Col. Van Heutsz]] attacked the hostile *stelling* at [[Lam Bada]] — removed one of the most active Habib commanders from the Achehnese order of battle. The retrospective observation that [[Teungkoe Lam-Oeri]] had "previously pushed forward the Kling Habib Lhong as a figurehead" suggests that Lhong's military prominence had been at least partly orchestrated by the *oelama* network of the [[III Moekims Ateuh|III Moekims Ateuh]], and that his death at Senelop therefore represented not only a personal loss but the collapse of a particular configuration of religious-military authority in the western mukims.

## Significance

Habib Lhong's significance in *ATJEH '96* lies in his embodiment of the Kling Habib as a religious-military actor in the Achehnese resistance — a figure whose descent from the Prophet gave him religious authority, whose Kling status gave him a liminal position in the Achehnese social order, and whose military activity in support of the pretender Sultan placed him at the intersection of the dynastic, religious, and military dimensions of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. His death at [[Senelop]] on [[17 April]] [[1896]] removed one of the most active Habib commanders from the spring [[1896]] order of battle, and his instrumentalisation by [[Teungkoe Lam-Oeri]] illustrates the dense interconnections between the Habib network and the Achehnese *oelama* establishment.
