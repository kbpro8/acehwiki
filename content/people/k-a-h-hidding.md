**K. A. H. Hidding** — published in the volume as K. A. Hidding — was a Dutch scholar of Sundanese religion and ethnography whose work *Gebruiken en Godsdienst der Soendaneezen* ("Customs and Religion of the Sundanese", Batavia, 1935) is cited in the closing bibliography of Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]]'s chapter *De invloed van den Islam* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as one of the principal sources on Indonesian Islam. Hidding also co-edits, with H. Th. Fischer, the volume's broader ethnographic frame: Fischer's *Inleiding tot de volkenkunde van Nederlandsch-Indië* (Haarlem, 1940) is also cited.

## Role in *Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk*

Hidding himself does not contribute a chapter to the volume; he is named only in the bibliography to Kernkamp's chapter, alongside G. H. Bousquet (*Introduction à l'étude de l'Islam indonésien*, Paris, 1938), H. Th. Fischer (*Inleiding tot de volkenkunde van Nederlandsch-Indië*, Haarlem, 1940), W. Juynboll (*Handleiding tot de kennis van de Mohammedaansche Wet*, 4th ed., Leiden, 1930), R. A. Kern (*De verbreiding van den Islam*, in Stapel's *Geschiedenis van Nederlandsch-Indië* Vol. I, Amsterdam, 1938), [[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]] (*Atjèh*, Leiden, 1922/3), and [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] (*De Atjéhers* and *Verspreide Geschriften* Vol. IV). The placement of Hidding's Sundanese monograph in this Atjeh-heavy list reflects the comparative method of Kernkamp's chapter, which moves between Sundanese, Javanese, Atjehnese and other Indonesian Islams to illustrate general points.

## Significance

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Hidding's significance is indirect: his Sundanese material supplies one of the comparative reference points against which Kernkamp reads the Atjeh case. Where the Atjeh chapter describes the [[Meunasah]], the [[Teungku|Teungkoe]], the *idja pinggang*, the [[Soerat te'h]], the *ratib* of mid-Ramadan and the [[Toean di boh]] fertility saint, the Sundanese material in Hidding's monograph supplies the parallel material on the *lebé* (village religious official), the *pesantren* (boarding school), the *Safar* end-Wednesday baths, and so on, against which the Atjeh case is shown to be both part of a wider Indonesian pattern and a distinctive variant of it.
