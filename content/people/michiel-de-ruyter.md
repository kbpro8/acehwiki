**Michiel de Ruyter** was a pre‑eminent [[Dutch Republic|Dutch]] admiral who served the [[Dutch Navy]] and the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] from the mid‑[[1640s]] until his death in [[1676]], commanding major naval actions such as the [[Battle of Schooneveld]] [[1666]], the [[Battle of Kijkduin]] [[1673]], the [[Battle of Solebay]] [[1672]], the raid on the [[River Medway]] during the [[Second Anglo‑Dutch War|Second Anglo‑Dutch War]] [[1665]]–[[1667]], and leading a large squadron to the Caribbean in [[1674]]. His tactical skill secured the safety of the [[Indische retourvloot|Indies return fleet]]—the lifeline of Dutch spice trade with the Sultanate of [[Aceh]]—and his strategic decisions were later invoked in discussions of Dutch colonial naval doctrine.

## Early career and Aceh‑related operations

De Ruyter entered the service of the [[Dutch Navy]] in the [[1650s]] and quickly rose through the ranks of the VOC fleet. In [[1659]] he was appointed commander of a squadron dispatched to the east coast of [[Sumatra]] to suppress Acehnese resistance to Dutch trade monopolies. Contemporary reports credit him with the capture of several Acehnese forts and the seizure of valuable spice cargoes, actions that temporarily secured Dutch access to the pepper trade and opened the way for the regular passage of the [[Indische retourvloot]] through Aceh’s ports. The expedition lasted until [[1660]], after which the fleet returned to Batavia with a convoy of spice‑laden ships.

## Naval career and Caribbean expedition ([[1674]])

De Ruyter’s career continued to advance, and by the early [[1670s]] he held the rank of [[lieutenant‑admiral|lieutenant‑admiral]]. In [[1674]] the Admiralty colleges ordered him to lead a “*behoorlijk groot eskader*” (a fairly large squadron) to the Caribbean. The squadron’s primary task was to transport troops to [[Martinique]] and to provide “*schippersdiensten*” (shipping services) for the Dutch colonial outposts. After reaching [[San Domingo]], the force proceeded to the [[Bermudas]]. From there De Ruyter dispatched the captain‑mariner [[Thomas Tohiaszoon]] and the colonel‑marines [[Jan van Hoorn]] back to the Dutch Republic with a report in [[September]] [[1674]].  

The source argues that the failure of the landing operation lay not with the troops themselves but with higher‑level strategic decisions made by the “*opdrachtgevers in Den Haag*” (the commissioners in [[The Hague]]) and the [[Admiralty colleges]], which had issued orders that were “*onwaarschijnlik*” (unlikely) to include the transport of a large marine landing force. This interpretation exonerates De Ruyter from direct blame for the aborted landing.

## Role in the Second Anglo‑Dutch War and the raid on the Medway

During the [[Second Anglo‑Dutch War|Second Anglo‑Dutch War]] [[1665]]–[[1667]], De Ruyter commanded the Dutch fleet in several key engagements. In [[1667]] he led a detonation‑heavy detachment up the [[River Medway]], contributing to the capture of the English flagship [[Royal Charles]] and the broader success of the Dutch raid on [[Chatham]]. The same war saw him participate in the Battle of [[Solebay]] (often dated [[1666]]–[[1667]] in Dutch sources), where his daring tactics were later praised by naval historians.

## Battles of Schooneveld and Kijkduin (Franco‑Dutch War)

In the early phase of the [[Dutch–French War|Franco‑Dutch War]] [[1672]]–[[1678]], De Ruyter was tasked with defending Dutch home waters while ensuring the safe passage of the [[Indische retourvloot]] through the North Sea and the English Channel. His fleet achieved decisive victories at the [[Battle of Schooneveld]] [[1666]] and the [[Battle of Kijkduin]] [[1673]]. Julian Corbett later described the Schooneveld engagement as “an example of naval defence against a superior enemy” (see page [[275]] of the source).

## Later years, death and legacy

After the Franco‑Dutch War, De Ruyter continued as the Republic’s chief naval commander. He fought again at the [[Battle of Solebay]] [[1672]], where he was mortally wounded and succumbed to his injuries in [[1676]]. His reputation as a master tactician endured into the nineteenth‑century Dutch colonial period, where his name was invoked in discussions of naval strategy for protecting the [[East Indies]]—including the Sultanate of [[Aceh]]—against foreign powers. De Ruyter’s strategic vision helped shape Dutch maritime doctrine that underpinned VOC operations in the Indonesian archipelago for decades.
