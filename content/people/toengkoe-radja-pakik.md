**Toengkoe Radja Pakik** was the prince (*radja*) of [[Pedir|Pidir]], the most important of the little states on the north coast of [[Atjeh]]. His submission of [[29 February]] [[1876]] opened the way for the Dutch [[Occupation of Pidir (31 May - 4 June 1876)|occupation of Pidir]] in June [[1876]].

## War and submission

Toengkoe Pakik's sister had been married to Sultan [[Mansjoer Sjah]], and his daughter to the last Sultan [[Machmoed Sjah]] — a closeness of kin that explained much of Pidir's loyalty to the fallen sultan's cause. During the Second Expedition he had sworn, so the Dutch records report, not to return to his realm before Atjeh came out of the struggle as victor or had bowed to Dutch arms; and he was on the side of the enemy with his armed force when his capital was bombarded and landed troops occupied the coast on [[31 December]] [[1873]].

The renewal of the Dutch offensive from December [[1875]], however, convinced him of the hopelessness of the resistance in Groot-Atjeh — and his own interest, Meijer judges, forced him at last to a better disposition. In the beginning of February [[1876]] he gave the first signs; at the end of the month the Dutch flag was hoisted at the capital of Pidir, he confirmed his declaration of submission with an oath on the Koran, and — in a clause deviating from earlier declarations — ceded free of charge a terrain for the Dutch establishment.

## The honourable burden of occupation, June–August 1876

At the landing of Captain [[J. R. Engelhard]] on [[1 June]] he asked for delay "to speak with his heads and his people"; he yielded on [[3 June]]. But the great market of his capital, crowded with armed men of the whole war party —*the rabble of all the former Atjehnese realm* in the words of the Dutch — was beyond his power to restrain; he could not, or did not dare to, expel them. That the prince's own position was ambiguous is shown by reports from the Gigiëng side reaching the Dutch: Toengkoe Pakik had once been in Groot-Atjeh to join the enemy with his fighters.

In July he asked that Dutch soldiers should not go near the neighbouring kampongs; on [[14 August]] a Dutch pioneer-coolie was killed a hundred metres from the assistant-resident's house, and the prince promised to hunt the perpetrator, sent his son to the military commander, came himself the next day, and on [[5 August]] sent an embassy to [[Kota Radja]] to show his good disposition — the first since his submission — which was shown the whole Dutch establishment in and around the Kraton and the lines.

## After 1876

The Dutch post at Pidir was moved to [[Segli]] in February [[1877]]. Toengkoe Pakik's submission stands in Meijer's history as the first great trophy of the north-coast establishment policy and the first step on the long road to the lasting administrative occupation of Pidir (Sigli).
