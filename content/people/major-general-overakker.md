**Major-General R. T. Overakker** was the Dutch officer who, after being directed from Java to Sumatra on [[9 February]] [[1942]], assumed command of the newly established territorial command Middle-Sumatra and was entrusted with the central leadership of the defense of all of Central and North Sumatra during the Japanese invasion of March [[1942]]. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Overakker as the strategist of the last-stand defense of the Atjèh highlands — the decision, taken after the fall of Java, to withdraw the troops elsewhere on Sumatra via [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] to Central Atjèh and to try to hold out in the Atjèh highlands for as long as possible.

## Appointment and command

Colonel Overakker — "shortly thereafter promoted to major-general" — was directed from Java to Sumatra on [[9 February]] [[1942]] to assume command of the newly established territorial command Middle-Sumatra. The territorial command of Sumatra's East Coast had in the meantime been separated from that of Atjèh, and Overakker was entrusted with "the central leadership of the defense of all of Central and North Sumatra." The command of Colonel [[Gosenson|Gosenson]] was hereby limited to the residency Atjèh and Dependencies, "the area where he was preeminently" qualified to operate.

## The decision to hold the highlands

After the fall of Java, General Overakker "decided to also withdraw the troops elsewhere on Sumatra via Kaban Djahé to Central Atjèh, in order to try to hold out in the Atjèh highlands for as long as possible." The focus of the defense thus came to lie in the [[Alasvalley|Alasvalley]] and the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]], where General Overakker would personally lead the defense. [[Takéngón|Takéngón]], where Colonel [[Gosenson|Gosenson]] would remain in command, became the main center covering the rear. According to the official report of the military operations (cited by Piekaar from the *Militaire Spectator*), the reorganisation reflected the strategic insight that the Atjèh highlands, with their difficult terrain and limited access, offered the best prospect of prolonged resistance.

## The capitulation

The Dutch forces on Sumatra capitulated on [[28 March]] [[1942]], after the collapse of the highlands defense. Piekaar notes that "only after the completion of this publication did we become aware of an article by captain A. Zijlmans in the 'Militaire Spectator' of August 1948, under the title 'Capitulate or fight through to the last man' which gives an overview of the action in particular of the troops of General Overakker in the Atjèh highlands." The post-war reckoning with the decision to capitulate, rather than to fight to the last man, would form part of the Dutch military-historical literature.

## Significance

Overakker's highlands defense, though ultimately unsuccessful, established the framework within which the Dutch military on Sumatra met the Japanese invasion. The withdrawal via Kaban Djahé to the Atjèh highlands concentrated the surviving KNIL forces in the interior, where their command structure was preserved until the capitulation. The decision shaped the geography of the final Dutch military action in the Netherlands-Indies.
