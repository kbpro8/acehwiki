**Generaal Van Swieten** — the school-textbook spelling of **Jan van Swieten** (1807–1888), the Dutch general who commanded the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second Aceh expedition]] of November [[1873]]–January [[1874]] and the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the kraton of 24 January 1874]] — is the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]]'s principal Dutch commander of the triumph phase of the Aceh War, and the staff history's principal instance of the Dutch general whose courtesy toward the Atjehnese contrasts with the Atjehnese cruelty toward Dutch envoys.

**General Van Swieten** — the grey war-hero and conqueror of the Acehnese — was the Dutch general who, after the historic storming of the [[Meusigit Raja|misigit]] in [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta Radja]] by the Dutch troops in [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]] (the [[Eerste Atjeh-expeditie (1873)|Eerste Atjeh-expeditie]]) and the burning-down and demolition of the mosque, managed to bring about that the colonial government, entirely at its own cost, built a new mosque — the [[Meusigit Raja]] — which was given to the Acehnese population "to make them forget the suffering of the war and to demonstrate, in the Islamic land par excellence, that the Netherlands respected the Mohammedan religion perfectly." Van Swieten is the figure through whom *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]) narrates the colonial state's religious politics in [[Atjeh|Aceh]]: the rebuilding of the Meusigit Raja as the forced benefaction of the *kafir Beulanda* (unbelieving Hollander).

## Account from *De Kleine Oosterling (school reader)* — Generaal Van Swieten

**Generaal Van Swieten** — the school-textbook spelling of **Jan van Swieten** (1807–1888), the Dutch general who commanded the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second Aceh expedition]] of November [[1873]]–January [[1874]] and the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the kraton of 24 January 1874]] — is the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]]'s principal Dutch commander of the triumph phase of the Aceh War, and the staff history's principal instance of the Dutch general whose courtesy toward the Atjehnese contrasts with the Atjehnese cruelty toward Dutch envoys.


## Significance

General Van Swieten is significant in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* as the conqueror of the Acehnese who brought about the rebuilding of the [[Meusigit Raja]] as the colonial state's gift to the Acehnese population. The book's argument — that the Van Swieten rebuilding was intended as a proof of the colonial state's respect for the Mohammedan religion, but that the Acehnese population had received it with indifference and mockery because it was the forced benefaction of the unbelieving Hollander — is the book's clearest example of the colonial state's failure to understand the Acehnese religious politics. Van Swieten is therefore the book's marker of the colonial state's religious politics in Aceh: the conqueror of the Acehnese who tried, and failed, to make the Acehnese population forget the suffering of the war.

Generaal Van Swieten is, in the [[De Kleine Oosterling|school-textbook]] narrative, the principal Dutch commander of the triumph phase of the Aceh War, and the staff history's principal instance of the Dutch general whose courtesy toward the Atjehnese contrasts with the Atjehnese cruelty toward Dutch envoys. The school reader's emphasis on Van Swieten's capable character — and on the *Wien Neerlandsch bloed* ceremonial of the kraton's capture — frames the second Aceh expedition as the Dutch triumph that vindicates the first expedition's adversity, and establishes Van Swieten as the moral counterpart to [[General Kaler|Kaler]]: the capable commander whose success closes the adversity–triumph arc of the school reader's Aceh War.

## The 1874 return to the Netherlands

The school reader records Van Swieten's return to the Netherlands after the kraton's capture: General Van Swieten considered his task accomplished and departed once more for the mother country. Van Swieten's departure left supreme command in the hands of [[Kolonel Pel|Kolonel Pel]], later appointed general.

## The 6 January 1874 mosque and the 24 January kraton

The school reader records the operational sequence of the second Aceh expedition: the landing near the Aceh river, the two letters to the sultan (carried by the seventy-year-old Javanese who was killed), the fierce battles on 25 and 26 December, and the storming of the great mosque on [[6 January]] [[6 January 1874|6 January 1874]]. The kraton itself was entered on [[24 January]] [[24 January 1874|24 January 1874]]: on 24 January our soldiers advanced, in order to enclose the kraton completely. It surprised us greatly that the enemy, who had still been firing heavily the previous day, now let nothing be heard of itself. A detachment of our troops was sent to reconnoitre the kraton more closely. It pushed through the brushwood, climbed a high earthen wall, and entered the kraton. Soon Van Swieten himself followed with more troops. A loud hurrah and the striking up of *Wien Neerlandsch bloed* by the band made known that the kraton was in our possession.

Van Swieten's *Wien Neerlandsch bloed* — the playing of the Dutch national anthem by the military band on the capture of the kraton — is the school reader's principal instance of the ceremonial triumph of the second Aceh expedition.

## The Acehnese indifference

The book also registers the Acehnese indifference to Van Swieten's rebuilding: "On the contrary: both during the construction and later, the Acehnese stood at his most indifferent towards it. Not one Acehnese made himself available as a workman at the construction, which was entirely performed by Chinese workfolk — that is, by pig-eating unbelievers — and the work was bitterly mocked by the Acehnese. They regarded it as the forced benefaction of their most unsympathetic unbeliever, the Hollander." The Acehnese indifference is the book's — and the colonial state's — register of the failure of Van Swieten's religious politics: the forced benefaction of the unbelieving Hollander was received with indifference and mockery.


## The conqueror of the Acehnese

"It was the conqueror of the Acehnese, Van Swieten, who managed to bring about that, on the part of the government and entirely at its own cost, a new mosque was built, which was given to the Acehnese population to make them forget the suffering of the war and to demonstrate, in the Islamic land par excellence, that the Netherlands respected the Mohammedan religion perfectly." Van Swieten — the conqueror of the Acehnese — is the figure who brought about the rebuilding of the Meusigit Raja as the colonial state's gift to the Acehnese population. The gift was intended to make them forget the suffering of the war — and to demonstrate, in the Islamic land par excellence — that the Netherlands respected the Mohammedan religion perfectly.


## The missive of 8 February 1874

"The grey war-hero, General Van Swieten, addressed to some Acehnese chiefs, in a missive of 8 February 1874, the well-known lines: 'that the great mosque would be rebuilt and would be made into a true place of mercy, from which no more death and destruction, but prayers to the almighty God and works of love would go out.'" The grey war-hero, General Van Swieten, in a missive of [[8 February]] [[1874]] to some Acehnese chiefs, wrote the well-known lines: that the great mosque would be rebuilt and made into a true place of mercy, from which no more death and destruction, but prayers to the almighty God and works of love would go out.

The missive of [[8 February]] [[1874]] is the book's — and Van Swieten's — clearest statement of the colonial state's religious politics in Aceh: the rebuilding of the mosque as the place of mercy, the replacement of death and destruction with prayers and works of love. The book — and the pastoor who narrates the chapter — immediately qualifies: "One should consider that one then lived in the raw reality of the war years."


## The second Aceh expedition of November 1873

The [[De Kleine Oosterling|Wijma school reader]] records the second Aceh expedition of November [[1873]] as the Dutch response to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]]'s adversity: the first expedition to Aceh had thus not turned out well. But one did not now sit idle. No, the adversity experienced fired both the Netherlands and the Indies to still greater zeal. In the summer of 1873 great preparations were made for a second expedition. In the Netherlands many soldiers were recruited; a large number of officers were, at their request, transferred to the Indies, and the capable General Van Swieten left the mother country to take supreme command of the troops that were to proceed to Aceh.

The second Aceh expedition was thus launched in the summer of [[1873]] with newly recruited troops and newly transferred officers, under Van Swieten's command. The school reader's framing of Van Swieten as capable establishes the contrast with the adversity of the first expedition under [[General Kaler|Kaler]].

## The size of the expedition

The school reader records the size of the second Aceh expedition: in November the army was ready. It was the largest that had ever been sent out by us in the Indies. It numbered 8,000 men, besides a further 4,000 servants and labourers. The fleet consisted of 25 ships, which together were manned with 2,000 hands. The second Aceh expedition was thus the largest Dutch expedition ever sent to the East Indies up to that date, with a total force of 8,000 soldiers, 4,000 servants and labourers, and 25 ships crewed by 2,000 men.

## The Snouck Hurgronje judgement

The book immediately registers the Snouck Hurgronje judgement of Van Swieten's rebuilding: "Dr Snouck Hurgronje called this House of God — intended as a proof of our reconciling disposition and generosity towards the Acehnese enemy — 'one of the many expensive mistakes that marked the beginning of our Aceh interference,' and this eminent scholar qualified the mosque as 'an empty monument of our folly.'" The Snouck Hurgronje judgement — "an empty monument of our folly" — is the book's — and the colonial state's — sharpest critique of the Van Swieten rebuilding.
