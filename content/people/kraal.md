**Kraal** — referred to in P. H. van der Kemp's 1900 article as *commies Kraal* (Clerk Kraal) — was a Dutch colonial clerk serving at [[Malacca]] in the years immediately after the restoration of Dutch rule under [[Godert Alexander Gerard Philip van der Capellen|Governor-General Van der Capellen]]. He is mentioned but once in Van der Kemp's text, in a passage of [[Timmerman Thyssen|Governor Thyssen]]'s letter of 5 December 1818 to [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|rear-admiral Wolterbeek]], but his appearance there carries considerable weight. Thyssen, in warning Wolterbeek of rumoured British plans for an attack on Malacca, observed that such an attack could be withstood "thanks also to the 4,000 [men] under commies Kraal." The figure indicates that Kraal exercised a substantial command — most probably of a labour or commissariat force — at a critical moment in the defence of the recently-recovered settlement.

## The December 1818 Context

In December 1818, [[Malacca]] had only recently been handed back to the Dutch by [[Major William Farquhar|Major Farquhar]] under the terms of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|treaty of 1814]]. Thyssen, as the newly-installed Dutch governor, was preoccupied with the city's defences and with the disposition of his European and indigenous troops. The rumoured British attack — which, as Van der Kemp notes, Thyssen believed "could well be withstood" — was the immediate context of his letter to Wolterbeek, and Kraal's 4,000 men are cited as one of the principal reasons for the governor's relative composure. Van der Kemp himself directs the reader to "page 11 of my Malacca-Riouw verhandeling" for the wider setting of Kraal's command, indicating that Kraal's role was more fully treated in his earlier monograph on the Malacca–Riouw affair.

## Administrative Function

The rank of *commies* in the late-eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century Netherlands Indies administration denoted a senior clerk or commissary — typically a position of considerable responsibility in matters of logistics, supply, and personnel. The command of 4,000 men (probably a mixed force of indigenous auxiliaries, coolies, and labourers engaged in fortification or commissariat work) by an officer of this rank is unusual, but consistent with the improvised character of the early post-restoration administration, in which formal military command and administrative responsibility often overlapped. Kraal's appearance in Thyssen's letter thus illustrates the practical looseness of the Dutch chain of command in this period, and the dependence of senior figures like Thyssen and Wolterbeek on subordinate officials of relatively modest titular rank.

## Significance in Van der Kemp's Narrative

Kraal's role in Van der Kemp's article is small but emblematic. The article as a whole is concerned with the Dutch failure to translate formal treaty rights — at [[Riouw|Riouw]], at [[Acheh]], at [[Pontianak]] — into effective control on the ground. Kraal's 4,000 men are mentioned precisely because, despite their number, they did not suffice to prevent the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]] a few weeks later or to deter the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] in April. The discrepancy between paper strength and effective power is, in Van der Kemp's reading, the central theme of the Dutch experience in the archipelago between 1818 and 1824.
