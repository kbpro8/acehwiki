**Affonso d'Albuquerque** (1453-1515) — **Afonso de Albuquerque**, "the Great", Governor of Portuguese India from [[5 November]] [[5 November]] [[1509]] until his death in [[1515]] — was the principal architect of the Portuguese empire in the Indian Ocean and the commander of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka of August 1511]], the foundational event of the Portuguese presence in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the proximate cause of the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]] as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malaka. Albuquerque's conquest defined the strategic framework within which all subsequent Malay-Portuguese and Malay-Dutch relations unfolded.

## The Governorship (1509-1515)

Albuquerque had been appointed Governor of Portuguese India in [[1509]], succeeding [[Francisco de Almeida|Francisco de Almeida]]. His strategic vision was the construction of a network of Portuguese fortresses commanding the chokepoints of the Indian Ocean trade — [[Goa|Goa]] (taken in [[1510]]), [[Malaka|Malaka]] (taken in [[1511]]), [[Aden|Aden]] (projected but never taken), [[Ormuz|Ormuz]] (taken in [[1515]]). The strategic aim was threefold: to dominate the Muslim trade of the Indian Ocean, to redirect the spice trade away from the Alexandrian route through Mamluk Egypt and toward the Cape route through Lisbon, and to establish the *Padroado Real* — the Portuguese crown's ecclesiastical and political jurisdiction over its conquests.

## The conquest of Malaka (1511)

The full account of Albuquerque's conquest of Malaka is given in the entry on the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest itself]]. In summary: he sailed from [[Cochin|Cochin]] on [[2 May]] [[1511]] with nineteen ships, having received a letter from the captive Portuguese factor **Ruy d'Aranjo** at Malaka urging that the trade of the city could only be secured by force. He called at the Atjeh pepper ports of [[Pedir|Pedir]] and [[Pasei|Pasei]] on the way, where he alarmed the rulers by seizing Muslim merchant vessels. He anchored before Malaka in mid-June [[1511]], demanded the surrender of the prisoners and compensation for the damaged goods of the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]], and added the demand that he be allowed to build a fort. The first assault on [[25 July]] [[1511]] failed; the second, renewed on [[10 August]] [[10 August]] [[1511]], took the city after a few days of street fighting.

Albuquerque immediately built the **Fortaleza de Malaca** with materials from the royal tombs and from demolished mosques — a desecration that, as Van der Woude notes, "allerminst bevorderlijk was voor een goede verstandhouding". He introduced a Portuguese administration, a new coinage, and the seizure of the tin mines as crown property. By these acts Malaka became a Portuguese colony. In Europe, Pope **Leo X** ordered a general thanksgiving with a procession to the Roman churches of Santa Maria del Popolo and Sant'Agostino.

## The strategic legacy

Albuquerque's conquest of Malaka produced the strategic framework within which the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]] must be understood. The deposed sultan of Malaka retired to **Bintang** and later to [[Djohor|Djohor]], from where he and his successors harassed Portuguese shipping and blockaded Malaka in turn. As the Malay siege of Malaka developed, the sultans of Atjeh — pressing from the west along the north Sumatran coast — became the most dangerous Muslim rivals of the Portuguese. The great Achehnese siege of Malaka of [[1 February|1-17 February]] [[1575]] nearly succeeded; the [[1587]] treaty between Atjeh and the Portuguese, by which [[Aru|Aru]] was again assigned to Djohor, opened a generation of relative peace. The Dutch arrival at Atjeh in [[1599]] reopened the strategic contest that Albuquerque's conquest had inaugurated.

## Significance

Affonso d'Albuquerque is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal architect of the Portuguese empire in the Indian Ocean and the principal author of the strategic framework within which the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]] must be understood. The [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest of Malaka of August 1511]] was the foundational event of the Portuguese presence in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the proximate cause of the rise of Atjeh as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malaka for the next hundred and thirty years. Albuquerque's strategic vision — the network of fortresses commanding the Indian Ocean chokepoints, the redirection of the spice trade, the *Padroado Real* — defined the framework within which the Portuguese empire in the East would operate until its displacement by the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] in the seventeenth century. The [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Dutch arrival at Atjeh in 1599]], with which Van der Woude's chapter opens, was thus a continuation by new hands of the Muslim-Christian contest that Albuquerque's conquest had inaugurated.
