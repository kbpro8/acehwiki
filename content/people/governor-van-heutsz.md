**Governor Van Heutsz** — General **Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz** ([[1851]]–[[1924]]) — was the Dutch military officer who, as **Governor of Acheh** from [[1898]] onwards, carried through the systematic subjugation of the Achehnese sultanate and ended the long war that had begun with the [[First Dutch attack on Acheh|first Dutch expedition]] of [[1873]]. He is the central colonial figure of Snouck Hurgronje's Introduction to *The Achehnese*, where his governorship is presented as the vindication, after twenty-five years of vacillation, of the policy of vigorous offensive first adumbrated by [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]] in [[1786]] and put briefly into practice by [[General van der Heijden|General van der Heijden]] between [[1877]] and [[1881]].

Van Heutsz took the direction of affairs in [[1898]], two years after the [[Defection of Teuku Uma|defection of Teuku Uma]] had finally opened the eyes of the colonial government to the need for vigorous action. Snouck writes that "since Governor Van Heutsz in 1898 took the direction of affairs, everything has been methodically done to make the necessary end, once and for all, of this insurrection". The previous policy of holding a "concentrated line" of forts around the old seat of the sultanate had been abandoned in favour of the offensive, and from 1898 a new rule was enforced: "all houses and settlements should be spared in the case of enemies as well as in the case of those who submitted"; "one should always behave with the greatest indulgence towards hereditary chiefs"; and "even repeated evidence of treachery formed no sufficient reason for not" extending clemency once more. The population was thus weaned from the war-party by a combination of military pressure and scrupulous justice.

The tactics employed were those which [[Captain Francis Light|Francis Light]] and [[James Price|James Price]] had advocated a century before. "Only as guerillas against guerillas, by using the most lightly equipped native troops under the leadership of first-rate European officers with non-commissioned officers of like quality, and by operating in small detachments, could any real results be arrived at. In less than six years General Van Heutsz made such progress that now even the most sceptical must admit that we have come to the beginning of the end. The traitor Uma and numbers of adventurers, fanatical leaders and guerilla chiefs perished; the chiefs of dependent states, the members of the Sultan's family, and finally their head himself who in [[1878]] as a child had been proclaimed Pretender Sultan, submitted almost without exception and thousands of refugees came back to their gampongs under the authority of Government".

The "head" here referred to is [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot|Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]], the last of the Achehnese sultans, whose [[Submission of Tuanku Muhamat Dawot|submission]] in [[January 1903|January 1903]] closed the line of Achehnese sovereigns. The continuance of the revolt after that date was, in Snouck's words, "censured by all chiefs of mark except a limited number of universally respected religious leaders"; and this exception itself, Snouck notes, "confirms anew the accuracy of what was said in this book in 1892 regarding the significance of the religious factor in the war; at that time no one believed it, but for the last two years every one has accepted it as a truth that he acknowledged from the first".

Van Heutsz's governorship was thus the practical embodiment of Snouck Hurgronje's analysis: the recognition that the resistance of [[Acheh|Acheh]] was religious as well as political, that no mere treaty with the sultan could pacify a country divided among countless independent chiefs, and that the war could only be brought to an end by patient, methodical, small-war operations conducted in conformity with the principles of clemency and good faith. His success made him, in the eyes of the colonial administration, the conqueror of Acheh; he was subsequently appointed Governor-General of the Netherlands East Indies ([[1904]]–[[1909]]).
