**Ibn Arabi** — in Malay tradition simply **Ibn ʿArabī** — was the pre‑eminent Andalusian Sufi mystic, philosopher and author, born in [[Murcia]] in [[560]] H / [[1165]] C.E. and dying in [[Damascus]] in [[638]] H / [[1240]] C.E. He composed the monumental *[[al-Futūḥāt al-Makkīya|al‑Futūḥāt al‑Makkīya]]* (“The Meccan Revelations”) and the *[[Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam|Fuṣūṣ al‑ḥikam]]* (“The Bezels of Wisdom”), the chief expositions of the doctrine of the unity of being (*waḥdat al‑wujūd*). In the Sufi intellectual network he served as teacher of [[Ibn Sabʿīn]], whose literary practices later influenced Acehnese scholars working on the *[[Burda (poem)|Burda]]* poem, thereby transmitting Ibn Arabi’s ideas to the [[Sultanate of Acheh]].

## Life, Works and Intellectual Lineage

Ibn Arabi’s early life in the multicultural milieu of [[Andalusia]] shaped his synthesis of Qur’anic exegesis, Neoplatonic philosophy and Sufi mysticism. After completing his studies in [[Mecca]], he settled in [[Damascus]] where he composed the *al‑Futūḥāt al‑Makkīya* (c. [[1202]]–[[1240]]) and the *Fuṣūṣ al‑ḥikam* (c. [[1220]]–[[1240]]). His writings articulate a vision of God as the absolute Being (*al‑Wājib al‑wujūd*) and elaborate the concept of *waḥdat al‑wujūd* that would dominate later Sufi thought.

Through his disciple [[Ibn Sabʿīn]] (d. [[1266]]), Ibn Arabi’s teachings entered the Malay world. Ibn Sabʿīn’s literary methods were adopted by Acehnese scholars who produced Malay translations and commentaries on the *Burda* poem, a devotional text that became central to the spiritual life of the [[Acheh]] Sultanate.

## Citations in Malay Sufi Literature

Ibn Arabi is cited repeatedly in the Malay writings of [[Nūruddīn ar‑Rānīrī|Raniri]], a 17th‑century Sufi scholar of [[Acheh]]. In the *[[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|Ḥujjat aṣ‑ṣiddīq li‑dafʿ az‑zindīq]]* Raniri invokes Ibn Arabi’s *al‑Futūḥāt al‑Makkīya* to refute the heretical [[Wujudiyyah]] of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]. Raniri quotes Ibn Arabi’s rejection of those who “identify the visible world with God,” using the authority of the greatest Sufi metaphysician to bolster his orthodox polemic.

Other Raniri works also draw on Ibn Arabi:

* In the *[[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|Ḥujjat aṣ‑ṣiddīq]]* (p. 17) he cites Ibn Arabi on the meaning of *wājib al‑wujūd* (“the Necessarily Existent”) as “the absolute Being,” i.e., the being of God.  
* The *[[Hall al-zill|Ḥall aẓ‑ẓill]]* names Ibn Arabi among “the great mystics.”  
* The *[[Djawahir al-'ulum|Jawāhir al‑ʿulūm]]* contains multiple citations of Ibn Arabi’s metaphysical concepts.  
* The *[[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|Tibjān]]* references him in its discussion of the Wujūdiyyah.

## The Orthodox Authority against the Wujūdiyyah

Raniri’s polemical strategy hinges on Ibn Arabi’s status as the principal authority of *waḥdat al‑wujūd*. By quoting Ibn Arabi’s rejection of a literal identification of the world with God, Raniri distinguishes the “orthodox” Sufi Wujūdiyyah from the “heretical” currents represented by Hamzah Pansuri’s school and Shamsuddin of Pasai. This illustrates the difficulty of separating orthodox from heterodox positions within 17th‑century Sufi scholasticism in [[Acheh]].

## Significance

Ibn Arabi’s influence spans continents and centuries. He is the foundational authority for the doctrine of the unity of being, cited by later Sufi scholars such as [[Abd al‑Karim al‑Jili|al‑Jīlī]] and by the Acehnese Sufi Shamsuddin of Pasai. Raniri’s use of Ibn Arabi both to support and to oppose various strands of Wujūdiyyah demonstrates the complex role of interpretation in Islamic theological disputes. Moreover, through his disciple [[Ibn Sabʿīn]] and the transmission of the *Burda* poem, Ibn Arabi’s ideas reached the literary and spiritual life of the [[Sultanate of Acheh]], shaping the region’s mystical heritage.
