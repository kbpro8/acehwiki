**Aḥmad ar-Rifāʿī** (d. [[1182]] CE) was the great mystic saint and founder of the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]] Sufi order — one of the principal *ṭuruq* (mystic orders) of orthodox Sunni mysticism — a younger contemporary of [[Abdul-Qadir Jilani|ʿAbdul-Qādir Jīlānī]]. His tomb at [[Mocha]] — the coffee port of [[Yemen]] on the Red Sea — is reverenced, and his order was the *silsila* (spiritual genealogy) into which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] was initiated by his *shaikh* [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]]. The Rifāʿiyya was the spiritual genealogy by which Raniri, on his arrival in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]], claimed the authority of an orthodox mystic against the [[Wujudiyyah]] of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]. In Acheh itself, Ahmad Rifaʿī is reverenced as the eponym of the [[Rapa'i]] religious performances, in which his name is "of course always invoked."

## The Rifāʿiyya

The Rifāʿiyya — also called the *Baṭāʾiḥiyya* — was founded in the marshlands of southern [[Iraq]] in the twelfth century CE and spread rapidly through the Muslim world. The disciples of the Rifāʿiyya are famous in many lands for their fire-handling and self-wounding demonstrations, feats that they perform as part of their devotional *dhikr* (remembrance) exercises. By the sixteenth century the order had reached [[Gudjarat|Gujarat]], where it was transmitted to [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]] by [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]] at [[Surat]]. From Bā Shaybān the *silsila* was transmitted to two of the principal Malay-Indonesian Sufis of the seventeenth century — [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] and [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]] — and so reached the Malay-Indonesian archipelago.

## The Tomb at Mocha

[[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] records, in the introduction to the [[1955]] edition of Raniri's two Malay treatises, that Raniri "was initiated into the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]], founded by the saint Aḥmad Rifāʿī, whose tomb at [[Mocha]] is reverenced." The association of the founder's tomb with Mocha — rather than with the marshlands of southern Iraq where the order was actually founded — is itself a document of the devotional geography of the Rifāʿiyya as it was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world: a geography in which the founder's sanctity was located at a Red Sea port frequented by Indian and Indonesian pilgrims and traders, and so made accessible to the Malay-Indonesian devotees of the order.

## The Rapa'i Performances in Acheh

In Acheh, Ahmad Rifaʿī is revered as a "master of mysticism" (*tasawwuf*) from whom the Achehnese religious performances called [[Rapa'i]] take their name, and who is "of course always invoked" at those performances. He is named by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] alongside the great founders of mystic orders, of whom the most celebrated in Acheh is [[Abdul-Qadir Jilani]].

The *Rapa'i* (the Achehnese form of the Arabic *rabāʿī*, the "fourth" [of the mystic recitations]) are religious performances in which the performers wound themselves with knives, sear their bodies with red-hot chains, and similar feats, while the bystanders chant religious formulas. They belong to the same class of Achehnese religious phenomena as the *liké* and *rateb* (the Achehnese equivalents of the Arabic *dhikr* and *ratib*) and the *sadati-pantons* (chanted love-stories). Snouck Hurgronje groups them with those "curious proceedings which in Acheh are included under the terms *liké* and *rateb*," at which "the help of these holy men and especially of [[Abdul-Qadir Jilani]] is invoked." The performers of the *Rapa'i* claim descent from Ahmad Rifaʿī, though the performance has in Snouck's judgement "degenerated into theatrical conjuring." The Achehnese *Rapa'i* performances — in which the performers "wound themselves with knives" and "sear their bodies with red-hot chains" — strongly suggest an Achehnese survival or adaptation of the Rifāʿī *dhikr*.

Ahmad Rifaʿī is described as a "master of mysticism" — that is, a teacher or eponym of a *tariqah* (mystic order) — rather than as a departed *wali* (saint) in the strict Achehnese sense. The *tariqahs*, as Snouck Hurgronje observes, "so popular in other parts of [[Sumatra]] do not flourish in Acheh, though sundry Achehnese *hajis* have enrolled themselves as members of a *tariqah* in [[Mecca]]." The spiritual successors of the founders of mystic orders in Acheh, who teach mystic practices and formulas, "also enjoy much respect, but are not very numerous." The invocation of Ahmad Rifaʿī at the *Rapa'i* performances is thus one of the comparatively rare survivals of organised mystic practice in Achehnese religious life.

## Significance

Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī is, through his order, one of the principal spiritual ancestors of [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] and of [[Yusuf Makasar|Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar]]. The Rifāʿiyya gave both Malay-Indonesian Sufis the authority of an orthodox *silsila* by which they were able to confront the [[Wujudiyyah]] mystics of the Malay-Indonesian courts — in [[Acheh]] in the case of Raniri, in [[Makassar]] in the case of Shaikh Yūsuf — and to claim for their own teaching the sanction of a *ṭarīqa* that had its origin in the heartlands of Sunni Islam. The transmission of the Rifāʿiyya from the marshlands of Iraq to the harbours of Gujarat and the courts of Acheh and Makassar is one of the principal channels by which the orthodox Sufi scholasticism of the Arab and Persian worlds was carried to the Malay-Indonesian archipelago in the seventeenth century.

At the same time, the Achehnese *Rapa'i* performances document a separate, popular-level survival of the founder's name and of the self-wounding Rifāʿī *dhikr* in the ritual life of Achehnese villages — a survival continuous with, though distinct from, the scholastic *silsila* transmitted to Raniri through Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān.
