M. C. Roos van Raadshoven was a Dutch colonial administrator who served as *Assistent-Resident van de afdeeling Noordkust van Atjeh* (Assistant-Resident of the North Coast of Atjeh division) during the period of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]]. As the senior European official responsible for the [[Noordkust van Atjeh]], Roos van Raadshoven occupied a position of considerable administrative and strategic importance within the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]]. The *afdeeling* (division) he administered comprised the northern coastal region of Atjeh, an area that included some of the most densely populated and economically significant territories in the region, as well as areas that had been central to the long-running Atjeh conflict. Roos van Raadshoven was present at the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) held at [[Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]], though no recorded remarks from him appear in the official minutes of these proceedings.

## Administrative Context of the Noordkust van Atjeh

The [[Noordkust van Atjeh]] represented one of the most important and complex administrative territories within the Atjeh government. The northern coast had been the primary theater of the *Atjeh-oorlog* (Atjeh War) since the initial Dutch invasion in 1873, and the region had experienced profound social, political, and economic disruption as a result of decades of military conflict. The coastal areas had been the first to come under effective Dutch control, and they consequently had the longest history of European administration in Atjeh. However, the persistence of resistance in the interior meant that even the relatively pacified coastal districts existed in a state of heightened security concern. The *uleëbalang* (territorial chiefs) of the northern coast were among the most powerful indigenous rulers in Atjeh, and managing the relationships between these chiefs and the colonial administration required a combination of diplomatic skill, local knowledge, and the backing of military force when necessary.

As *Assistent-Resident*, Roos van Raadshoven would have been responsible for overseeing both the European administrative apparatus and the *Inlandsch Bestuur* (Native Administration) within his division, mediating between colonial policy directives and the realities of local governance in a region where indigenous authority structures remained deeply entrenched. The northern coast was also significant as a corridor for trade and communication, connecting the administrative center at [[Koeta-Radja]] with the broader network of ports and settlements along the Atjeh coastline. The economic life of the region, which included pepper cultivation, trade in forest products, and the emerging plantation economy, required careful administrative oversight to balance colonial economic interests with the rights and needs of the indigenous population.

## Proposed Merger with the Oostkust

The reorganization deliberations at Koeta-Radja proposed that the [[Noordkust van Atjeh]] be merged with the [[Oostkust van Atjeh]] into a single, consolidated *afdeeling* designated "Noord- en Oostkust van Atjeh behalve Tamiang" (North and East Coast of Atjeh except Tamiang). This new division would encompass a combined population of approximately 480,000, making it one of the largest and most populous *afdeelingen* in the proposed new *gewest* (province) of [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]]. The merger represented a significant departure from the existing administrative structure, which had maintained separate *Assistent-Residenten* for the northern and eastern coasts. The consolidation was motivated by a desire for administrative efficiency and rationalization, reflecting the view that the existing division of the coastal territories into separate administrative units was a legacy of the military campaign that no longer corresponded to the operational needs of colonial governance.

The exclusion of Tamiang from the new combined division was a noteworthy detail of the proposal, indicating that this sub-region was considered sufficiently distinct—in geographic, economic, or administrative terms—to warrant separate treatment. The creation of a division with a population approaching half a million would have placed enormous demands on the *Assistent-Resident* assigned to it, requiring a capacity for delegation and coordination that exceeded what had been necessary in the smaller, pre-existing divisions. While Roos van Raadshoven did not contribute recorded remarks to the proceedings, his presence at the meetings ensured that the perspective of the official responsible for one of the two constituent divisions was represented. The proposed merger would have directly affected his professional position, potentially eliminating his current role or requiring his reassignment within the reorganized administrative structure. The scale of the new division also raised questions about whether the existing staffing and resources of the *Assistent-Resident*'s office would be adequate for the expanded responsibilities.
