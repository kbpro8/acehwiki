**John Davis** (c. 1550-1605) was an English navigator and pilot — one of the most experienced English pilots of the late sixteenth century, famous for his Arctic voyages in search of the Northwest Passage ([[1585]]-[[1587]]) — who served as stuurman (pilot) aboard the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers' voyage of 1599]] to [[Acheh|Atjeh]] under [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]] on the ships *De Leeuw* and *De Leeuwin* outfitted by [[Balthasar de Moucheron|Balthasar de Moucheron]], and again as pilot aboard [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]]'s [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]] in [[1602]]. Davis's dual service — Dutch under Houtman, English under Lancaster — marks him as the principal English pilot of the early Dutch-English commercial partnership in the East Indies and the principal instance of the early English intelligence interest in the Dutch East India trade.

## The Houtman voyage as spy

Davis shipped aboard the Moucheron fleet of [[1598]] in the service of the Earl of Essex, as he later wrote: "when it pleased you to employ mee in this Voyage, for the discovering of these Easterne parts of the world, to the service of her Majestie, and the good of our Countrey". His role was, in modern terms, that of an intelligence officer: he was to learn the Dutch East India route, the Dutch commercial methods, and the political and commercial situation in the East Indies, and to report back to Essex. The arrangement was acceptable to [[Balthasar de Moucheron|Moucheron]], who needed an experienced pilot and was willing to take the risk of an English observer aboard.

Davis's presence on the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman voyage]] may have contributed to the Dutch decision to trust the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]]'s initial welcome: his experience of Asian courts was greater than Houtman's, and his reports would later be used by [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]] to plan the English East India Company's first voyage. Davis survived the [[11 September]] [[1599]] ambush at Atjeh, in which [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]] was killed and [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] taken captive, and returned to England.

## The Lancaster voyage (1601-1603)

Davis served as pilot aboard the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|Lancaster fleet]] that left Woolwich on [[13 February]] [[1601]]. His earlier experience at Atjeh — and his knowledge of the Achehnese court, the pepper prices, and the Portuguese position in the Strait of Malaka — was the principal English intelligence asset for the first English East India Company voyage. Van der Woude notes that Lancaster's expectation of pepper prices at Atjeh was based on "advies van zijn stuurman John Davis", though the prices Lancaster actually found were higher than Davis had led him to expect.

Davis died in [[1605]] on a later voyage, killed in a skirmish with Japanese pirates off the coast of [[Borneo|Borneo]] — a death that, in the English maritime mythology of the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, sealed his reputation as one of the great Elizabethan navigators.

## Significance

John Davis is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal English pilot of the early Dutch-English commercial partnership in the East Indies and the principal instance of the early English intelligence interest in the Dutch East India trade. His dual service — Dutch under [[Cornelis de Houtman|Houtman]] in [[1598]]-[[1599]], English under [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]] in [[1601]]-[[1603]] — frames the brief moment in which the English and Dutch East India interests co-operated rather than competed, and his presence on both voyages made the intelligence transfer from the Dutch to the English East India companies possible. Davis's role also marks the early-modern practice of using experienced pilots as intelligence officers — a practice that would continue throughout the seventeenth century and that helps explain the rapid English catch-up with the Dutch in the East India trade after [[1600]].
