**W. van Goltstein** — Willem van Goltstein, born in [[1831]] — was a Dutch colonial minister who served two non-consecutive terms as Minister of Colonies ([[1874]]–[[1876]] and [[1879]]–[[1882]]) during the administrations of the Governor-Generals [[J. Loudon|Loudon]] and [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]], playing a crucial role in shaping colonial policy during the later years of the [[Aceh War]] and the transition to civil governance in [[Aceh]]. He is known in the source literature principally for his telegram of [[4 November]] [[1874]] to Loudon — an intervention that led to the [[Conversiequaestie|conversiequaestie]] and the fall of the Governor-General — and for the conservative, gradualist colonial policy he pursued across both terms.

## Early Career

Van Goltstein began his career at the Department of Colonial Affairs in [[1855]], later serving in the [[Second Chamber]] ([[1864]]–[[1871]]) and the [[First Chamber]] ([[1872]]–[[1874]]). He was involved in the founding of the society for natural research in the Dutch colonies and later held various prestigious positions, including ambassador to [[London]] ([[1894]]–[[1899]]).

## First Ministry ([[1874]]–[[1876]])

Van Goltstein entered the Heemskerk ministry in [[1874]] and was responsible for Van Lansberge's appointment as Governor-General in [[1875]]. He was initially cautious regarding the [[Aceh]] policy, expressing concern about the costs and advocating for a more measured approach. However, he supported Van Lansberge's initiatives, including the [[1875]] visit to Aceh, though he warned the Governor-General against excessive optimism about a quick end to the war.

During this period, Van Goltstein:
- Supported the introduction of patent and personal taxes on Europeans.
- Engaged in debates about the *poenale sanctie* (penal sanctions) for labour contracts, initially supporting some restrictions but later becoming more cautious.
- Oversaw the early stages of the [[Tanjung Priok]] harbour project.
- Worked to improve the quality of judicial and financial officials in the Indies.

### The Telegram of 4 November 1874

The most consequential intervention of Van Goltstein's first term concerned the [[Conversiequaestie|conversiequaestie]] — the question of converting communal land tenure on [[Java]] into individual ownership. Reports received in the Netherlands about the conversions carried out by the general secretary [[H. D. Levyssohn Norman|Levyssohn Norman]] gave Van Goltstein occasion to send a telegram on [[4 November]] [[1874]] whereby Governor-General [[J. Loudon|Loudon]] was requested to cause the official journey of the general secretary through Java and Madura to be stopped. "In writing the viceroy was invited to guard against all pressure for conversion on the part of European officials, and to issue no general *ordonnantie*, except after consultation with the Opperbestuur, in the spirit of art. 20 of the [[Regeeringsreglement|Regeeringsreglement]]. Such consultation seemed to be desired both because of the great interest involved in the matter, and because it had appeared that in Indië very divergent opinions existed about the proposed regulation."

The Governor-General complied with the instruction given him, but at the same time requested honourable discharge, which was granted him by Royal Decree of [[17 December]] [[1874]]. The intervention directly led to the conversiequaestie and to the fall of Loudon.

Van Goltstein's first term ended in [[September]] [[1876]] when he resigned following the rejection of military-law amendments.

## Interregnum ([[1877]]–[[1879]])

After his resignation, Van Goltstein remained active in colonial affairs, writing newspaper articles about [[Aceh]] that drew criticism from General [[Jan van Swieten|J. van Swieten]]. He was consulted on various matters and maintained a private correspondence with Van Lansberge.

## Second Ministry ([[1879]]–[[1882]])

Van Goltstein returned to office in [[August]] [[1879]] as part of the conservative [[Van Lynden van Sandenburg]] cabinet. His second term was characterised by a more cautious, conservative approach to reform across several policy domains.

### Aceh Policy

Van Goltstein was more cautious than Van Lansberge about the transition to civil governance in Aceh. He advised against haste, stating that "it is better to bear the high costs a little longer than to have to start anew later." He was involved in the delicate negotiations surrounding General [[K. van der Heijden]]'s replacement, working to manage the King's preference for Van der Heijden while supporting Van Lansberge's preference for civil governance.

He raised concerns about the pace of change, noting that "our influence on the Acehnese coast is evidently not yet great" and warning that "premature" decisions could lead to renewed conflict.

### Financial Policy

Van Goltstein introduced a new financial arrangement between the Netherlands and the Indies, proposing:
- A fixed contribution of 2 million guilders.
- A variable contribution of up to 6 million guilders (half of the surplus).
- Surpluses to be used exclusively for Indian needs.

His proposal was ultimately rejected by the [[Second Chamber]], though he remained in office to handle other pressing matters.

### The Conversion Question

On the issue of converting communal land tenure to individual ownership on Java, Van Goltstein was a cautious reformer. He resisted pressure from liberal parliamentarians for rapid conversion, arguing that:
- The Javanese people were not yet ready for such radical change.
- Conversion should occur gradually and only when desired by the population.
- It was not the government's role to impose European concepts of property on Javanese society.
- The *sawah melkers* (land speculators) would exploit the population if conversion was forced.

His position led to the *Kabinetscirculaire* of [[15 August]] [[1880]], which was criticised by liberal members of the Second Chamber and contributed to his eventual resignation. Van Goltstein's insistence on consultation with the Opperbestuur before the issue of a general *ordonnantie*, and on the avoidance of pressure for conversion by European officials, set the policy that would be followed by the Indies government for the next decades: that the conversion of communal into individual land ownership should not be forced by the administration, but should be left to the spontaneous development of the native population.

### The Corvée (Heerendiensten) Question

Van Goltstein opposed the wholesale abolition of the *pantjens* (personal services to indigenous chiefs), arguing that:
- These services were a "bond" between the population and the aristocracy.
- The Javanese social order was not yet ready for the removal of traditional bonds.
- Abolition should be gradual, starting with minor chiefs and some services.
- A uniform head tax of 1 guilder for all service-liable individuals was "highly unfair" as it did not account for varying burdens.

### Administrative Policy

Van Goltstein supported the improvement of the civil service through:
- Sending qualified specialists from the Netherlands.
- Encouraging the study of languages other than [[Javanese]].
- Maintaining the principle of hereditary succession for regents.
- Avoiding unnecessary disruption of the indigenous social order.

## Resignation and Legacy

Van Goltstein's resignation in [[1882]] was prompted by parliamentary criticism of his conversion policy. The Second Chamber adopted a resolution criticising the [[15 August]] [[1880]] circular, and Van Goltstein, seeing this as a "reprimand in the most unambiguous terms," offered his resignation. When the cabinet fell and he was asked to return, he declined, and [[J. de Brauw]] succeeded him.

His legacy lies in his cautious, gradual approach to reform, his defence of the indigenous social order, and his efforts to balance Dutch and Indian financial interests. He is remembered as a conservative administrator who prioritised stability and continuity over rapid change.

## Account from Verstege's *Een beroep op het Nederlandsche volk* (1886)

[[J. J. W. E. Verstege|Verstege]] records of the minister's Atjeh course the following fixed dates. On [[28 August]] [[1874]] van Goltstein replaced minister [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]], and in December [[1876]], in an article "Atjeh sedert 1874", he publicly testified that kolonel [[Jacobus Pel|Pel]]'s position in the summer of 1874 had been precarious ("hachelijk"), that the Atjehsche population "ons zoo weinig met rust gelaten heeft, dat onze stelling, vóór de aankomst van versterkingen, niet zonder gevaar was" ("left us so little in peace that our position, before the arrival of reinforcements, was not without danger"), that only by bloody fights was some safety secured, and "alléén van offensieve bewegingen zijn vruchten geplukt — en stilzitten was steeds achteruitgaan" ("only from offensive movements was fruit gathered — and standing still was always going backwards"). On [[18 November]] [[1881]] he spoke in the Tweede Kamer of the "valsche philanthropie" (false philanthropy) "which nearly brought luitenant-generaal [[Karel van der Heijden|K. van der Heyden]] on the bench of the accused", as the minister then expressed it — words by which Verstege reminds the reader how the celebrated commander had indeed been virtually put there, "through this counsellor of the crown, after theories from the Queen". On [[28 March]] [[1881]] the same minister, defending the replacement of Van der Heyden as inevitable so as to be able to *opbeuren* ("raise") the Atjehsche population, declared that the answer whether the conqueror had reached his goal had to be affirmative, "vermits alle waarborgen aanwezig waren, dat de oorlog niet meer zou worden hervat" ("since all guarantees were present that the war would not be resumed") — while on the administrator's standpoint, he owned, the answer was less satisfying, with the population of Groot-Atjeh, once estimated at 800,000 souls, declined to 50,000. In [[1882]] he was out of office; his successor [[Willem Maurits de Brauw|de Brauw]] carried the same line.
