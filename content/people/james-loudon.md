**James Loudon** was the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies]] from [[1 January]] [[1872]] to [[17 December]] [[1874]], and his rule was dominated by the outbreak of the [[Aceh War]]. He approved the mission that became the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)]], oversaw the immediate preparation of the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)]], and later became one of the main targets of criticism in [[De enquête over den mislukten tocht naar Atjeh]].

Loudon is important in Aceh-related history because his name stands at the meeting point of policy, war, and blame. He helped turn the dispute with [[Aceh|Atjeh]] into open war, then faced sharp criticism after the first expedition failed.

## Governor-General at the outbreak of war

Loudon took office on [[1 January]] [[1872]]. During his rule, tensions with [[Aceh|Atjeh]] grew around sovereignty, foreign diplomacy, and Dutch fears of outside intervention. After reports that Acehnese envoys in [[Singapore]] had approached American and Italian representatives, the Dutch colonial government moved toward force.

In the official record, Loudon and the [[Council of Netherlands India|Council of the Netherlands Indies]] approved the dispatch of a strong mission to [[Aceh|Atjeh]]. This led directly to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)]]. Later writers and speakers in the [[Tweede Kamer]] argued over how much initiative came from Loudon and how much came from [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte]].

## Loudon and the first expedition

The first Dutch expedition failed in [[April]] [[1873]]. [[J. H. R. Köhler|General Köhler]] was killed, the Dutch position became untenable, and the force withdrew. After this defeat, Loudon remained central to the public argument. Critics said the war had been begun too quickly and with poor preparation. Defenders said the retreat had been forced by circumstance.

## The inquiry after the defeat

In [[De enquête over den mislukten tocht naar Atjeh]], [[H. B. van Daalen]] made Loudon one of his main targets. Van Daalen argued that after the failed expedition the government questioned officers in secret before appointing a formal [[Commission of Inquiry into the First Aceh Expedition]]. He believed these steps were meant either to prove that the withdrawal had been unavoidable or to shift blame onto the commanders in the field.

Van Daalen also attacked the anonymous pamphlet [[Bemoediging]], which he treated as an almost official defense of Loudon. He even suggested that only someone close to the Governor-General could have written with such confidence about confidential letters and secret discussions.

## The day order and the controversy

Van Daalen compared [[Bemoediging]] with Loudon's day order to the returning troops in [[May]] [[1873]]. In his view, both texts followed the same line: they admitted that the Dutch had suffered loss, but still claimed that much had been gained, and above all they protected Loudon from blame.

Van Daalen also argued that Loudon's supporters tried to place the whole design of the failed conquest on [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte]], while keeping the Governor-General clear of responsibility. This made Loudon a central figure not only in the military history of the war, but also in its early pamphlet war.

## Loudon and the second expedition

Despite the failure of the first attack, Loudon remained in office while preparations for a second expedition were pushed forward. The new invasion force was larger, better armed, and better supplied. Under [[Jan van Swieten]], it landed in [[December]] [[1873]], took the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]], and led to the [[Van Swieten's Proclamation of 31 January 1874]]. Van Swieten later wrote that the policy for this expedition had been shaped in discussion among Loudon, [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte]], and himself.

## Significance

**James Loudon** is significant because he was the governor-general who stood at the center of the Dutch move from pressure to war in [[Aceh|Atjeh]]. He appears in later history both as an administrator who acted decisively and as a public figure whom critics accused of protecting himself after defeat. The early history of the [[Aceh War]] cannot be told without him.
