**Frederik de Houtman** (1571–1627) was a Dutch navigator, linguist and astronomer whose twenty-six-month captivity in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] from [[1599]] to [[1601]] produced one of the earliest European grammars and dictionaries of the Malay language and the first European catalogue of southern-hemisphere stars. A member of the pioneering [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s "wilde vaart" generation of pre-VOC Dutch adventurers, De Houtman is a pivotal figure in the early Dutch-Atjeh encounter — and his Atjeh imprisonment, by his own later account, was a providential mercy.

## The De Moucheron expedition of 1598–1601

The South-Netherlander of French descent [[Balthazar de Moucheron|Balthazar de Moucheron]], trusting the experience of the [[Cornelis de Houtman|De Houtman]] brothers, took them into his service on a fleet of two ships, with the English pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] serving as pilot "om in opdracht van den Graaf van Essex spionnenwerk te verrichten" — to do spy-work on behalf of the Earl of Essex. The voyage to the Indies took fifteen months. The Madagascar population fled at the reappearance of the rough Dutchmen. When at length the fleet reached Atjeh in June [[1599]], the good understanding with the Sultan lasted only briefly.

## The Atjeh disaster of 1599

The Atjehnese — first with poisoned fruits and drinks, then with a *kris*-attack — sought to be rid of these foreigners. There were many dead, including Frederik's brother [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]], and many prisoners, of whom Frederik was one. Godee Molsbergen's terse summary runs: "Er waren veel dooden, waaronder Cornelis de Houtman, en veel gevangenen, o.a. Frederik de Houtman." See [[Death of Cornelis de Houtman at Atjeh (1599)]].

## Twenty-six months in Atjeh, 1599–1601

Frederik de Houtman was held prisoner in Atjeh for twenty-six months. He later interpreted the disaster as a mercy of Heaven: he saw in his captivity "de bedoeling des Hemels … om een beginsel (= begin) te wezen, daar door Gods eer en kennis … onder de Heidenen bekend gemaakt" kon worden — the intention of Heaven to be a beginning, through which God's honour and knowledge might be made known among the heathen — for which he received "het verstand en de genade van de Maleische spraak, die in gansch Oost-Indië als een gemeene (algemeene) spraak is" — the understanding and grace of the Malay language, which in all the East Indies is a common tongue.

He recorded the fruit of that grace in the introduction to his *Spraeck ende woord-boeck in de Maleysche ende Madagaskarsche talen* (Hague speech- and word-book in the Malay and Madagascar languages), published in [[1603]], containing twelve dialogues. The Madagascar part he learned from a native "die al 4 jaar met ons volk gevaren had" — who had already sailed with our people for four years.

## The *Spraeck ende woord-boeck* (1603)

The dialogues in the *Spraeck ende woord-boeck* were partly borrowed from a Flemish-French model, as the ninth dialogue proves: it is very un-Indian in tone, going on about "komt U warmen bij het vuur" (come warm yourself by the fire), the meal, and the use of napkins. The tenth dialogue has "Hij is van Ghent. In wat strate woont hij? Hij woont bij de nieuwe Kerk, waar het schild van Engeland uithangt" — He is from Ghent. In what street does he live? He lives by the New Church, where the sign of England hangs.

The *Spraeck ende woord-boeck* had only an eight-year author's right or octroy. After its expiry it was reworked by A. C. Ruil, whose work was later translated into Latin, and through that language into English. In England, the orientalist [[Thomas Hyde]] was, in the seventeenth century, the only scholar to cultivate the Malay language; in the eighteenth a Malay dictionary appeared, and in the early nineteenth two more — among them that of [[W. Marsden]], the British colonial official known in the Archipelago from the English interregnum, "die hiermee de Hollandsche-Maleische woordenboek-schrijvers voor was" — who thereby outstripped the Dutch-Malay dictionary-writers.

## Southern-hemisphere astronomy

In his twenty-six months in Atjeh, De Houtman also observed the southern sky and added to his language-book "de declinatien van vele vaste sterren staande omtrent den Zuyd-pool voor desen tydt noyt ghesien" — the declinations of many fixed stars standing around the South Pole, never before seen at this time. The Atjeh captivity thus produced not only the first Dutch-Malay dialogue-book but also one of the earliest European star-catalogues of the deep southern sky.

## Release and after

The ships that had departed Atjeh made for [[Pedir]], where they beat off an Atjehnese naval attack; they then touched at the Nicobars and, after much misfortune, reached Middelburg in the summer of [[1600]]. [[Balthazar de Moucheron|De Moucheron]] suffered heavy losses from the voyage, and [[John Davis]] turned his spy-material to English advantage. As the Dutch had once wrung the secrets of the India trade from Portugal, England now sought profit from Dutch experience — including in the Malay language.

Frederik de Houtman himself, however, was still in Atjeh. His release finally came thanks to a show of Portuguese force: see [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)]] and [[Cornelis de Houtman]].

## Later visit to Atjeh under Van Caerden (1600)

The [[1600]] visit of [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] to Atjeh brought De Houtman briefly aboard the Dutch ships, where he warned Van Caerden of an impending attack. He then returned to shore, hoping to obtain the release of his fellow-prisoners, to deliver a cargo to Van Caerden, and to negotiate a contract between the Sultan and the Dutch. All in vain — De Houtman remained a prisoner. See [[Pigskin parchment letter incident at Atjeh (1600)]].

## Significance

Frederik de Houtman's Atjeh captivity is the foundational episode of European Malay studies and of European astronomy of the southern sky. The *Spraeck ende woord-boeck* of [[1603]] — composed from notes taken during his Atjeh imprisonment — directly shaped Dutch, Latin and English Malay lexicography for the next two centuries, and through [[W. Marsden]] influenced the British tradition as well. For [[Acheh|Atjeh]], De Houtman is the European prisoner who turned captivity into the earliest substantial Western record of the sultanate's lingua franca — a Malay of the Atjeh roadstead that would become the diplomatic and commercial *lingua franca* of the entire Archipelago.
