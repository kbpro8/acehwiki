**Tengkoe di Tirou** — also written *Teungku di Tiro* — was the principal religious leader (*teungku*) of the Atjehese resistance during the period of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] from [[1884]] to his sudden death in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]], and the soul of the Atjehese resistance in the judgment of [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]]. His influence, which grew steadily throughout the governorship of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] and [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]], made him the principal obstacle to the Dutch policy of negotiated submission; and his death, together with that of [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] in the same month, removed the principal addressee of the Dutch conciliation and led to the failure of the Van Teijn governorship.

## The Soul of the Resistance (1884–1890)

Under the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] (1884–1886), the Dutch retired into the concentrated *stelling* around [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] to await patiently the enemy's submission. The enemy — abundantly supplied from the coasts, familiar with Dutch capriciousness, knowing that the Dutch were never sure of what they really wanted ("*Kompanie tidah tentoe; orang blanda bodoh*" — the Kompanie is never sure of its case; the Hollanders are stupid, as the Atjehers said) — prosecuted the war in his own way. [[Toeankoe Hasschim|Toeankoe Hasschim]], guardian and father-in-law of the youthful pretender, sent the principal leaders to the coastal statelets to stir up the population, to call all to the holy war, and to collect money and men; and he did so "in overleg met het geestelijke hoofd Tengkoe di Tirou, wiens invloed steeds toenam" (in concert with the religious leader Tengkoe di Tirou, whose influence ever increased).

Those who openly took part in the struggle were henceforth called *[[Muslemin|Muslemin]]*. The Sultan's influence greatly increased. In all Atjeh and for all Atjehers he was — and remained, at the time of Kruijt's writing — the lawful head of the country. His decision was invoked in many disputes. From his name the orders for the war were everywhere given. The principal leaders bound themselves afresh under oath against the Dutch, among them the later Dutch "friends" [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] and [[Nja Makam|Nja Makam]].

## The Encirclement of the Dutch Line (1887–1890)

Under [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]'s governorship (1886–1891), Tengkoe di Tirou's power and influence continued to grow. The Dutch *stelling* was regularly surrounded by a ring of fortifications thrown up by Tengkoe di Tirou; from these the line itself was bombarded with artillery, the means of communication destroyed, raids made within the line, and harm done where possible. The enemy even entrenched himself within the line repeatedly, and had to be driven out with force of arms in [[April 1887|April]] and [[October 1887|October]] [[1887]] and in [[July 1888|July]] [[1888]]; and a part of the railway had to be moved further inward.

Kruijt characterises Tengkoe di Tirou's strategy in the following terms: convinced that the Atjehers in the open field must lose to the Dutch, he judged it best to make clever use of Dutch credulity, by means of apparently well-meant proposals of submission, reports of discord among the Atjehers, and so on, through supposed friends or well-disposed persons, to throw sand in the Dutch's eyes and bring them into a sweet delusion of conciliation — and then at the same time to creep up on them and spring on them like a tiger on its prey, where possible to commit acts of piracy *à la* [[Hok Canton affair|Hok Canton]] to bring the Dutch into difficulties with England, and constantly to urge perseverance: "*wat wij niet kunnen doen zullen onze kinderen en kleinkinderen volbrengen*" (what we cannot do, our children and grandchildren will accomplish).

## The 1889 Offer to Bring the Sultan

Given the existing tension between [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] and Tengkoe di Tirou, Tengkoe di Tirou — fearful of eventually being caught behind the net, or seeking to be ahead of the others — in [[1889]] offered to bring the Sultan to the Dutch. Convinced of being on the right path (for the Dutch had long been sending friendly chiefs and emissaries, supposedly at the Sultan's invitation — for so the *adat* ran — with gifts to [[Kemala|Kemala]], to take soundings), the Dutch did not accept Tengkoe di Tirou's offer, in which he had of course stipulated certain advantages for himself. More missions to Kemala, very solemn and imposing, followed; presumably the members of the missions received, or rather bought with Dutch money, Sultan's appointments — but for the Dutch all led to nothing.

## The Strong Position Opposite the Dutch Line

The strong position taken by Tengkoe di Tirou opposite the Dutch line, the difficulty the Dutch had in keeping him at bay, together with the Dutch sweet, conciliatory attitude toward Kemala and other chiefs, heightened among the Atjehers the hope that it would ultimately succeed to drive the Dutch into the sea. When, moreover, the health of Van Teijn — whose early replacement was foreseen, promising a change of administration and direction — gave the Atjehers reason to adopt, like the Dutch, a policy of waiting and to postpone conciliation for the time being, that was an additional reason.

## Death and Succession (January 1891)

In [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] Tengkoe di Tirou and [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] died suddenly, and this too was an occasion for the Kemala-party in any case to await first the consequences of the impending change of administration. In [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]] Van Teijn, thus disappointed in his expectations, after years of heavy laborious work, departed owing to increasing indisposition. His successor was [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Colonel Pompe van Meerdervoort]].

In [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], Tengkoe di Tirou was succeeded as head of the resistance by his son [[Tengkoe Mohamed Amin|Tengkoe Mohamed Amin]], who was recognised as such by the Sultan in [[January 1892|January]] [[1892]].

## Significance

Tengkoe di Tirou is the principal religious leader of the middle phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and the architect of the strategy of deceptive conciliation that frustrated the Dutch policy of negotiated submission under [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]. His encirclement of the Dutch *stelling* with a ring of fortifications, his bombardment of the Dutch line with artillery, his tiger-like strategy of ambush, and his diplomatic manoeuvring to extract Dutch money and arms while preserving the Atjehese resistance, made him the soul of the Atjehese war effort in the judgment of Kruijt. His death in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] — together with that of [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] — was the immediate cause of the failure of the Van Teijn governorship and the descent into the brief and contentious interregnum of [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Pompe van Meerdervoort]] and the subsequent disaster of [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]] and the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]].

Tengkoe di Tirou was a member of the famous [[Tiro Teungku|di Tiro]] family of religious leaders, whose predecessors had led the original Atjehese resistance to the Dutch in [[1873]]–[[1874]] and whose successors would continue to lead Atjehese resistance into the twentieth century. The family's role as religious leaders of the Atjehese resistance is one of the constants of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].
