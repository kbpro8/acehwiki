**Tengkoe di Tirou** — also written *Teungku di Tiro* — was the principal religious leader (*teungku*) of the Atjehese resistance during the period of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] from [[1884]] to his sudden death in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]], and the soul of the Atjehese resistance in the judgment of [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]]. His influence, which grew steadily throughout the governorship of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] and [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]], made him the principal obstacle to the Dutch policy of negotiated submission; and his death, together with that of [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] in the same month, removed the principal addressee of the Dutch conciliation and led to the failure of the Van Teijn governorship.

## The Soul of the Resistance (1884–1890)

Under the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] (1884–1886), the Dutch retired into the concentrated *stelling* around [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] to await patiently the enemy's submission. The enemy — abundantly supplied from the coasts, familiar with Dutch capriciousness, knowing that the Dutch were never sure of what they really wanted ("*Kompanie tidah tentoe; orang blanda bodoh*" — the Kompanie is never sure of its case; the Hollanders are stupid, as the Atjehers said) — prosecuted the war in his own way. [[Toeankoe Hasschim|Toeankoe Hasschim]], guardian and father-in-law of the youthful pretender, sent the principal leaders to the coastal statelets to stir up the population, to call all to the holy war, and to collect money and men; and he did so "in overleg met het geestelijke hoofd Tengkoe di Tirou, wiens invloed steeds toenam" (in concert with the religious leader Tengkoe di Tirou, whose influence ever increased).

Those who openly took part in the struggle were henceforth called *[[Muslemin|Muslemin]]*. The Sultan's influence greatly increased. In all Atjeh and for all Atjehers he was — and remained, at the time of Kruijt's writing — the lawful head of the country. His decision was invoked in many disputes. From his name the orders for the war were everywhere given. The principal leaders bound themselves afresh under oath against the Dutch, among them the later Dutch "friends" [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] and [[Nja Makam|Nja Makam]].

## The Encirclement of the Dutch Line (1887–1890)

Under [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]'s governorship (1886–1891), Tengkoe di Tirou's power and influence continued to grow. The Dutch *stelling* was regularly surrounded by a ring of fortifications thrown up by Tengkoe di Tirou; from these the line itself was bombarded with artillery, the means of communication destroyed, raids made within the line, and harm done where possible. The enemy even entrenched himself within the line repeatedly, and had to be driven out with force of arms in [[April 1887|April]] and [[October 1887|October]] [[1887]] and in [[July 1888|July]] [[1888]]; and a part of the railway had to be moved further inward.

Kruijt characterises Tengkoe di Tirou's strategy in the following terms: convinced that the Atjehers in the open field must lose to the Dutch, he judged it best to make clever use of Dutch credulity, by means of apparently well-meant proposals of submission, reports of discord among the Atjehers, and so on, through supposed friends or well-disposed persons, to throw sand in the Dutch's eyes and bring them into a sweet delusion of conciliation — and then at the same time to creep up on them and spring on them like a tiger on its prey, where possible to commit acts of piracy *à la* [[Hok Canton affair|Hok Canton]] to bring the Dutch into difficulties with England, and constantly to urge perseverance: "*wat wij niet kunnen doen zullen onze kinderen en kleinkinderen volbrengen*" (what we cannot do, our children and grandchildren will accomplish).

## The 1889 Offer to Bring the Sultan

Given the existing tension between [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] and Tengkoe di Tirou, Tengkoe di Tirou — fearful of eventually being caught behind the net, or seeking to be ahead of the others — in [[1889]] offered to bring the Sultan to the Dutch. Convinced of being on the right path (for the Dutch had long been sending friendly chiefs and emissaries, supposedly at the Sultan's invitation — for so the *adat* ran — with gifts to [[Kemala|Kemala]], to take soundings), the Dutch did not accept Tengkoe di Tirou's offer, in which he had of course stipulated certain advantages for himself. More missions to Kemala, very solemn and imposing, followed; presumably the members of the missions received, or rather bought with Dutch money, Sultan's appointments — but for the Dutch all led to nothing.

## The Strong Position Opposite the Dutch Line

The strong position taken by Tengkoe di Tirou opposite the Dutch line, the difficulty the Dutch had in keeping him at bay, together with the Dutch sweet, conciliatory attitude toward Kemala and other chiefs, heightened among the Atjehers the hope that it would ultimately succeed to drive the Dutch into the sea. When, moreover, the health of Van Teijn — whose early replacement was foreseen, promising a change of administration and direction — gave the Atjehers reason to adopt, like the Dutch, a policy of waiting and to postpone conciliation for the time being, that was an additional reason.

## Death and Succession (January 1891)

In [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] Tengkoe di Tirou and [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] died suddenly, and this too was an occasion for the Kemala-party in any case to await first the consequences of the impending change of administration. In [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]] Van Teijn, thus disappointed in his expectations, after years of heavy laborious work, departed owing to increasing indisposition. His successor was [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Colonel Pompe van Meerdervoort]].

In [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], Tengkoe di Tirou was succeeded as head of the resistance by his son [[Tengkoe Mohamed Amin|Tengkoe Mohamed Amin]], who was recognised as such by the Sultan in [[January 1892|January]] [[1892]].

## Significance

Tengkoe di Tirou is the principal religious leader of the middle phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and the architect of the strategy of deceptive conciliation that frustrated the Dutch policy of negotiated submission under [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]. His encirclement of the Dutch *stelling* with a ring of fortifications, his bombardment of the Dutch line with artillery, his tiger-like strategy of ambush, and his diplomatic manoeuvring to extract Dutch money and arms while preserving the Atjehese resistance, made him the soul of the Atjehese war effort in the judgment of Kruijt. His death in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] — together with that of [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] — was the immediate cause of the failure of the Van Teijn governorship and the descent into the brief and contentious interregnum of [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Pompe van Meerdervoort]] and the subsequent disaster of [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]] and the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]].

Tengkoe di Tirou was a member of the famous [[Tiro Teungku|di Tiro]] family of religious leaders, whose predecessors had led the original Atjehese resistance to the Dutch in [[1873]]–[[1874]] and whose successors would continue to lead Atjehese resistance into the twentieth century. The family's role as religious leaders of the Atjehese resistance is one of the constants of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893)

In the [[1893]] tract *[[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|De onderwerping van Atjeh]]*, [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] singles out Tengkoe di Tirou — *Tëngku di Tiro*, in the older Dutch spelling *Tengkoe di Tirou*; historically *Tengku Chik di Tiro*, *Teungku di Tiro* — alongside the political chief [[Toeankoe Haschim]] and the sultan-candidate [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] at [[Kemala]], as one of the principal authorities of the Atjehnese war party in the *cajoleering*-decade of [[1884]]–[[1886]]. He is the figure Van Heutsz names as the Koranic preacher who, *met den koran in de hand* (with the Koran in hand), *spoorde de geloovigen met succes aan tot volharding in den strijd tegen den ongeloovigen indringer* — exhorted the faithful, with success, to perseverance in the struggle against the unbelieving invader — and whose religious authority gave the resistance the sanction the political and dynastic authorities at Kemala could not themselves supply.

### Koranic preacher of the resistance

Tengkoe di Tirou enters Van Heutsz's narrative as the religious counterpart to the political settlement at Kemala. Where [[Toeankoe Haschim]] supplied the dynastic form — installing his pupil [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] as sultan at the [[Indrapoeri]] mosque — and where the sultan-candidate supplied the constitutional authority, Tengkoe di Tirou supplied the religious sanction: the Koranic exegesis that legitimised the resistance against a non-Muslim invader. Van Heutsz's text fixes the role with precision:

> "vooral Tengkoe di Tirou met den koran in de hand de geloovigen met succes aanspoorde tot volharding in den strijd tegen den ongeloovigen indringer, vooral nu deze allerwegen blijk had gegeven van zwakte en onmacht". — above all Tengkoe di Tirou, with the Koran in hand, exhorted the faithful with success to perseverance in the struggle against the unbelieving invader, especially now that he had everywhere given evidence of weakness and impotence.

The formula *met den koran in de hand* — with the Koran in hand — is Van Heutsz's: the *ulama* preached the *volharding* (perseverance) the war party required, and the Koran was, in his preaching, both the warrant and the proof. The resistance was, by this exegesis, not a political rising against a colonial power but a religious duty of the faithful against an *ongeloovige indringer* (unbelieving invader) — a *sabil* struggle of the kind the Atjehnese tradition would remember as the *sabil* war.

### Exploitation of Dutch weakness and onmacht

The second clause of Van Heutsz's sentence fixes the conjuncture that gave Tengkoe di Tirou's preaching its force: the *ongeloovigen indringer* had *allerwegen blijk gegeven van zwakte en onmacht* — had everywhere given evidence of weakness and impotence. The reference is to the *cajoleering*-decade of [[1884]]–[[1886]] — the years of the [[Concentratiebesluit (19 Augustus 1884)|Concentratie]] of [[19 August]] [[1884]], the [[Demmeni]] hybrid *half sluiting / half scheepvaartregeling* of [[13 October]] [[1884]], the [[Staatsblad No. 85 van 1885|Staatsblad No. 85 of 1885]] repeal of the [[Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884|blokkade]], and the December [[1885]] abolition of the residual *scheepvaartregeling*. The Dutch position had, in those years, been reduced to the *geconcentreerde linie* at [[Kota Radja|Kota-Radja]] and a few coastal points, with the *Onderhoorigheden* abandoned to the *politiek van non-interventie*. The Atjehnese chiefs and population had *allerwegen* — everywhere — seen the evidence: the Dutch withdrawals, the Dutch compromises, the Dutch *zwakte en onmacht*.

The Koranic exegesis was thus also a political diagnosis. The *ongeloovige indringer* had shown himself weak; the Koran itself, Van Heutsz notes elsewhere, *voorschrijft, dat een geloovige zich nooit anders aan een ongeloovige mag onderwerpen, dan genoodzaakt door dwang* — prescribes that a believer may never submit to an unbeliever except when compelled by force. The Dutch *zwakte en onmacht* had removed the *dwang* (compulsion) that could have made submission religiously permissible; Tengkoe di Tirou's preaching filled the space the Dutch had vacated, and the *volharding* of the faithful was its result.

### Religious authority of the resistance

The religious authority Tengkoe di Tirou exercised in the resistance was, in Van Heutsz's narrative, the third leg of the Atjehnese constitutional settlement at [[Kemala]]: the dynastic authority of the sultan-candidate [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]], the political authority of the regent [[Toeankoe Haschim]], and the religious authority of the *ulama* Tengkoe di Tirou. The three were complementary. The sultan-candidate supplied the form — the name in which the orders dispatched from Kemala "als komende van het eenig wettige gezag, van den sultan" (as coming from the only lawful authority, from the sultan) were issued. The regent supplied the substance — the *zeer krachtige persoonlijkheid* (very forceful personality) at the head of the *tegenpartij*, the manipulator of his pupil, the architect of the *emmedji Indrapoeri* installation. The *ulama* supplied the sanction — the Koranic warrant that legitimised the resistance and exhorted the faithful to its *volharding*. The arrangement was the Atjehnese analogue of the constitutional division of authority — sultan, *ulèëbalang*, *ulama* — that would later be analysed in detail by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]; Van Heutsz's 1893 text gives it its first systematic Dutch description.

### The Van Teijn repulse (first year of the Van Teijn governorship, 1887)

The military counterpart to Tengkoe di Tirou's religious authority was his command of armed *benden* (bands) that, in the first year of the [[Van Teijn]] governorship ([[1886]]–[[1891]]), entrenched themselves near [[Kota Moesapi]] close to the Dutch linie. Van Heutsz records the two repulses — the first Dutch military success of the Van Teijn governorship:

> "hoe hij de steeds driester geworden Tengkoe di Tirou en zijne benden reeds in het eerste jaar van zijn bestuur tot tweemaal toe met bebloede koppen uit onze linie sloeg, toen zij zich daar nabij Kota Moesapi genesteld hadden". — how, in the first year of his administration, he twice drove the ever-bolder Tengkoe di Tirou and his bands, with bloody heads, out of our line, when they had entrenched themselves near Kota Moesapi.

The *steeds driester geworden* (ever-bolder) Tengkoe di Tirou was twice driven, *met bebloede koppen* (with bloody heads), out of the Dutch linie. The repulse was the operational proof that the religious authority of the resistance could be met by force; it was also the political capital Van Teijn would spend, in the years [[1888]]–[[1890]], on the closure of the Noord- en Westkust and the *actieve verdediging* of the linie. The *ulama* himself survived the repulses, and his *bende* reappears in Van Heutsz's text in the *Koeala Pantjoer* prauw-rooftocht of the same governorship — a subordinate chief from the [[VI Moekims]] was required, as a test of his sincerity of submission, to recover a stolen *prauw* belonging to *Tekoe Neq van Merassa* from "eene bende van Tengkoe die Tirou" at [[Koeala Pantjoer]].

### Significance (per Van Heutsz)

Tengkoe di Tirou's significance in Van Heutsz's narrative is that of the *ulama* whom Van Heutsz names as the religious leader of the Atjehnese resistance in the *cajoleering*-decade of [[1884]]–[[1886]] — the Koranic preacher who, *met den koran in de hand*, *spoorde de geloovigen met succes aan tot volharding in den strijd tegen den ongeloovigen indringer*; the religious authority whose exegesis exploited the *zwakte en onmacht* the Dutch had *allerwegen blijk gegeven* — the political diagnosis that gave the Koranic warrant its force, since the Koran itself *voorschrijft, dat een geloovige zich nooit anders aan een ongeloovige mag onderwerpen, dan genoodzaakt door dwang*, and the Dutch weakness had removed the *dwang* that could have made submission religiously permissible; and the third leg of the Atjehnese constitutional settlement at [[Kemala]] — the religious sanction that complemented the dynastic authority of [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] and the political authority of [[Toeankoe Haschim]], and that gave the resistance the religious form the political and dynastic authorities could not themselves supply. His two repulses by [[Van Teijn]] in the first year of the [[1886]]–[[1891]] governorship — *met bebloede koppen uit onze linie* — were the first Dutch military success against the religious authority of the resistance; the *ulama* himself survived, and his *bende* would reappear in the [[Koeala Pantjoer]] prauw-rooftocht of the same governorship.
