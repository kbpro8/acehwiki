The most colorful figure of the later Aceh War, a "priman" (freeman) without office or territory who led attacks in the IV Mukims. He staged a sensational apparent submission to the Dutch, obtaining money and a man-of-war before his followers attacked its crew. His followers' seizure of the steamer Hok Canton in [[1886]], taking Mrs. Hansen and John Fay hostage, is described in detail in the Hikayat Prang Gompeuni. He later became master of Krueng Sabe and met ceremonially with Teungku Tiro, declaring fealty both knew to be a polite fiction.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Teuku Uma

**Teuku Uma** (also written **Teuku Umar**, and rendered in the OCR of *The Achehnese* as **Teuku Unia**) was an Achehnese political adventurer and *panglima* of the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] whose career during the later years of the Dutch-Achehnese War made him, for a time, the most powerful native ruler in the country. Snouck Hurgronje describes him in the Preface to Vol. I as a typical Achehnese in his "complete untrustworthiness and in his slavery to opium and gambling," but distinguished him from most Achehnese headmen by his "energy and consummate tact in his relations with all sorts and conditions of men."

Uma came from the [[Meulaboh]] country, and by cunning, intrigue and violence gradually acquired a commanding influence over the entire West Coast and over one of the three *sagis* of [[Great Acheh]] — the [[VI Mukims]] of the [[XXV Mukims]] whose hereditary chiefs were the [[Teuku Nanta Seutia|Nanta]] family. He rose, in Snouck Hurgronje's phrase, "from a mere *panglima* in the war against the Dutch to be the dreaded lord of a great part of the country," and ultimately obtained from the Sultan a deed of appointment as "Sea-guardian of the West Coast" (*panglima laot* of the West Coast), by which a large portion of that region was formally made over to him in his letters-patent. When some of the dispossessed hereditary chiefs complained of this edict, it was pointed out to them that Teuku Uma was already too strong to be resisted.

In [[1893]], having "repeatedly asked for the forgiveness of his past offences," Uma submitted to the Dutch. The acceptance of his submission was, in Snouck Hurgronje's view, not in itself censurable, since the policy of "concentration" then in force precluded any attempt to dispossess him. What followed, however, is denounced in the strongest terms: a Governor wholly unacquainted with native affairs gave his entire confidence to the adventurer, and "in defiance of all warnings persisted in strengthening Uma by generous subsidies of Dutch weapons and Dutch money." Assisted thus, Uma overcame his own enemies under cover of fighting the foes of the Dutch regime, distributed plentiful subsidies to his friends in the War-party, and strove by every means to acquire the maximum of influence over both the Dutch and their opponents.

The last three years of the "concentration" period — from Uma's submission in [[1893]] to his open defection in March [[1896]] — earned an unhappy notoriety under the name of the **"Uma period."** In March [[1896]] Uma, feeling the claims pressed upon him by [[General Deykerhoff]] burdensome and having by then sufficient money and arms to play a leading part among the War-party, threw off the mask and openly joined the resistance. Snouck Hurgronje observes that since the real purport of his actions had long been as obvious as the day, one could only in a highly specialised sense speak of his "defection." After his open break, the Nanta family — to which his wife [[Chut Din]] belonged — was declared deposed from the government of the [[VI Mukims]] for having taken a prominent part in his treachery, and the young [[Teuku Raja Itam]], son of the late Teuku Ne' of [[Meura'sa]], was placed in authority there.

Snouck Hurgronje classifies Teuku Uma as the type of the energetic adventurer who profited by the prevailing confusion to essay fortunes beyond the reach of hereditary chiefs. Beloved by his followers for his generous and kindly demeanour, he was yet a rival whom even an *uleebalang* of moderate strength could keep at bay: against an energetic chief such as the [[Imeum of Teunom]] "even Teuku Uma could make but little head." His relations with the religious leaders of the war were equally ambiguous; he was the man who at length persuaded [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] that it was in many respects desirable for the chiefs and notables within the Dutch *linie* to keep in touch with the sultan's court, and thus drew the great *ulama* into a temporary accommodation with the political adventurers of the resistance.

The Uma episode, "however melancholy in itself," indirectly exercised a healthy influence on the management of Achehnese affairs. Its termination opened all eyes to the need for vigorous action, and in [[1896]] the war against the Achehnese, suspended without real gain since [[1881]], was resumed. Under [[Governor Van Heutsz]] from [[1898]] onwards, the subjugation of the country was methodically pursued to its end.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Teukoe Oemar

**Teukoe Oemar** was an [[Atjehers|Acehnese]] chieftain whose death in battle near [[Meulabôh|Meulabôh]] is commemorated by a monument on the West Coast, mentioned in *De Buitenbezittingen* in the legend to one of its plates: "Monumentje op de plek, waar Toekoe Oemar sneuvelde; nabij Meulabôh." His fall — *sneuvelde*, the Dutch military vocabulary's term for a soldier killed in action — marked a turning point in the pacification of the West Coast, where his shifting allegiances and military capability had made him one of the most formidable Acehnese leaders of the late-nineteenth-century war.

## The West Coast theatre

The West Coast of Atjeh, which Meulabôh commands, was throughout the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-krijg]] a critical theatre. Its maze of estuaries, swamps, and forested foothills made it both a corridor for arms and supplies from the overwal — the Malay peninsula, especially [[Penang]] — and a refuge for *onverzoenlijken* (irreconcilables) driven out of the [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] plain. The *kustvaart* (coasting trade) that supplied the interior also supplied the resistance; the Dutch response, in the years after [[1874]], was a blockade that closed the harbours one by one and concentrated what trade remained at [[Oelèë Lheuë|Oelèë Lheuë]] and [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]].

## The monument near Meulabôh

The plate in *De Buitenbezittingen* shows the landscape of the West Coast from Seudoe northward, with the *Glé Radja*, the cape of *O. Masam Moeka* (the Aceh Head), and the islands of *P. Breuë* in the background; in the foreground stands the small monument that marks the spot where Teukoe Oemar fell. The choice to commemorate his death in this way — and to publish the image in an official handbook — reflects the Dutch respect for a formidable adversary and the symbolic importance they attached to the place where he was killed. His elimination removed one of the last effective chieftains of the West Coast and opened the region to the administrative consolidation described in the handbook's chapter on the *Onderhoorigheden*.
