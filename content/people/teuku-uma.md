**Teuku Uma** (also written **Teuku Umar**, Dutch sources *Teukoe Oemar*) was an Acehnese guerrilla leader and the most celebrated adventurer of the later [[Aceh War]], a "priman" (freeman) without hereditary office or territory who rose through cunning, intrigue, and military skill to become, for a time, the most powerful native ruler in the country. His career — encompassing a sensational apparent submission to the Dutch in [[1893]], years of manipulation of colonial resources, open defection in March [[1896]], and final defeat and death near [[Meulaboh]] in [[1899]] — became a touchstone for Dutch debates about Acehnese policy, and his story is told across multiple accounts that testify to his importance from every angle of the war.

## Early career and rise to power

Uma came from the [[Meulaboh]] country on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] of Aceh, the most colourful figure of the later war. He was, in [[Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s phrase, a typical Acehnese in his "complete untrustworthiness and in his slavery to opium and gambling," yet distinguished from most Acehnese headmen by his "energy and consummate tact in his relations with all sorts and conditions of men." He led attacks in the IV Mukims and by cunning, intrigue, and violence gradually acquired a commanding influence over the entire West Coast and over one of the three *sagis* of [[Great Acheh]] — the [[VI Mukims]] of the [[XXV Mukims]] whose hereditary chiefs were the [[Teuku Nanta Seutia|Nanta]] family. He rose, in Snouck Hurgronje's phrase, "from a mere *panglima* in the war against the Dutch to be the dreaded lord of a great part of the country," eventually obtaining from the Sultan a deed of appointment as "Sea-guardian of the West Coast" (*panglima laot* of the West Coast), by which a large portion of that region was formally made over to him; when some of the dispossessed hereditary chiefs complained, it was pointed out that Teuku Uma was already too strong to be resisted.

He also met ceremonially with [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]], the great religious leader of the resistance, declaring fealty both men knew to be a polite fiction. He was the man who at length persuaded Teungku Tiro that it was in many respects desirable for the chiefs and notables within the Dutch *linie* to keep in touch with the sultan's court, and thus drew the great *ulama* into a temporary accommodation with the political adventurers of the resistance.

## Submission and the "Uma period" (1893–1896)

In [[1893]], having "repeatedly asked for the forgiveness of his past offences," Uma submitted to the Dutch. The acceptance of his submission was, in Snouck Hurgronje's view, not in itself censurable, since the policy of "concentration" then in force precluded any attempt to dispossess him. What followed, however, he denounces in the strongest terms: a Governor wholly unacquainted with native affairs gave his entire confidence to the adventurer, and "in defiance of all warnings persisted in strengthening Uma by generous subsidies of Dutch weapons and Dutch money." Uma staged a sensational apparent submission, obtaining money and a man-of-war before his followers attacked its crew. His followers' seizure of the steamer Hok Canton in [[1886]], taking Mrs. Hansen and John Fay hostage, is described in detail in the [[Hikayat Prang Gompeuni]]. He later became master of Krueng Sabe. Assisted by Dutch subsidies, Uma overcame his own enemies under cover of fighting the foes of the Dutch regime, distributed plentiful subsidies to his friends in the War-party, and strove by every means to acquire the maximum of influence over both the Dutch and their opponents.

The last three years of the "concentration" period — from Uma's submission in [[1893]] to his open defection in March [[1896]] — earned an unhappy notoriety under the name of the **"Uma period."**

## Defection and final campaign (1896–1899)

In March [[1896]], Uma, feeling the claims pressed upon him by [[General Deykerhoff]] burdensome and having by then sufficient money and arms to play a leading part among the War-party, threw off the mask and openly joined the resistance. Snouck Hurgronje observes that since the real purport of his actions had long been as obvious as the day, one could only in a highly specialised sense speak of his "defection." After his open break, the Nanta family — to which his wife [[Chut Din]] belonged — was declared deposed from the government of the [[VI Mukims]] for having taken a prominent part in his treachery, and the young [[Teuku Raja Itam]], son of the late Teuku Ne' of [[Meura'sa]], was placed in authority there.

Teukoe Oemar was eventually tracked down and killed in battle near [[Meulaboh]] in [[1899]], during the campaign led by [[J. B. van Heutsz|Governor Van Heutsz]]. *De Buitenbezittingen* commemorates this in the legend to one of its plates: "Monumentje op de plek, waar Toekoe Oemar sneuvelde; nabij Meulabôh." His fall — *sneuvelde*, the Dutch military vocabulary's term for a soldier killed in action — marked a turning point in the pacification of the West Coast, where his shifting allegiances and military capability had made him one of the most formidable Acehnese leaders of the late-nineteenth-century war.

## Legacy

The Uma episode, "however melancholy in itself," indirectly exercised a healthy influence on the management of Acehnese affairs. Its termination opened all eyes to the need for vigorous action; in [[1896]] the war against the Achehnese, suspended without real gain since [[1881]], was resumed; and under [[Governor Van Heutsz]] from [[1898]] onwards, the subjugation of the country was methodically pursued to its end. Van Heutsz used the episode — and the earlier episode of Teukoe Oema — to argue that the Dutch must never trust local leaders without binding guarantees, a lesson that later informed his introduction of the [[Korte Verklaring]].

A later *Teukoe Oemar* — not related to the famous guerrilla leader — was the oelèëbalang of the *landschap* [[Keumangan]] in the subdistrict [[Sigli]], whose removal by the Dutch administration in [[1938]] was one of the "notorious removals" of prominent self-governing officials of the late colonial period. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats this removal as a defining episode of the late colonial administration and as the origin of the estrangement between the Keumangan house and the religious movement led by [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]], whose landschap Keumangan was. After the removal, the successor "appeared little in the foreground," retreating into farming in Lam Meuló and leaving the field open to the religious movement.
