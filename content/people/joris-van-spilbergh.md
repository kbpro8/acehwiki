**Joris van Spilbergh** (also *Spilbergen*, c. 1568–1620) was a Dutch Admiral whose voyage of [[1601]]–[[1604]] touched at [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in [[1602]] — where he found the English already present under [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] and where the first VOC fleet (sent out after the Company's foundation on [[20 March]] [[1602]]) arrived during his stay. Spilbergh sold one of his ships to the new Company and reached the fatherland with a richly-laden vessel. The episode is one of the hinge-moments of the Dutch-Atjeh relationship: the moment at which the pre-VOC "wilde vaart" gave way to the chartered Company's monopoly.

## The voyage of 1601-1604

Spilbergh's voyage took him via the Cape to the Indian Ocean. Past the Cape he met a French fleet on which the Dutchman [[Wouter Willekens|Wouter Willekens]] served as pilot. After many difficulties at the Comoro Islands — losing two sloops and twenty-eight men — the fleet came to Ceylon's west coast, then under Portuguese control. Sailing around the Point of Galle, Spilbergh reached Batticaloa on the east coast.

## The Kandyan alliance of 1602

The *Radja* of Batticaloa did not know what to make of the foreigners. He knew and feared the Portuguese, and dared not begin negotiations without authorisation from the Overlord at Kandi. He allowed a *kommies* (clerk) with letters and gifts to go to Kandi; once the clerk returned safely, Spilbergh himself, with ten men, made the difficult journey to the capital. The Admiral was received with splendour; seeing that the King was averse to trade, Spilbergh turned the conversation to [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]]'s success against the Spaniards at the Battle of Nieuwpoort, gave the King an equestrian portrait of the Prince, and proposed an alliance. The Sinhalese Prince entered into the proposal with enthusiasm and declared himself willing, as a mason, to haul lime and stone for a joint fortification against the arch-enemies.

## Atjeh, 1602

After this brilliant success of his embassy, Spilbergh returned to Batticaloa and from there sailed to [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. There the *Lam* — a ship from his own fleet that had strayed — was already lying, hired by the English Company under [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]], with [[John Davis|John Davis]] under Lancaster's command. In cooperation with the English, Spilbergh took a richly-laden Portuguese *kraak* (merchantman); after unloading the goods, he returned the *kraak* to the Portuguese "met hun vrouwen en kinderen" (with their wives and children) to sail to Malakka.

At Atjeh the first VOC fleet arrived — the one sent out by the newly-founded [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Company]] (charter of [[20 March]] [[1602]]). Spilbergh sold one of his ships to the new foundation and reached the fatherland with his well-laden ship. The sale of his ship to the VOC at Atjeh is one of the practical moments at which the pre-VOC "wilde vaart" was absorbed into the chartered Company's structure.

## The Dutch-English encounter at Atjeh

The Dutch-English encounter at Atjeh in [[1602]] is one of the earliest recorded joint operations between the two Protestant powers in the East Indies. The English, under [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]], had hired the strayed Dutch ship *Lam*; the Dutch, under Spilbergh, joined the English in taking a Portuguese *kraak*. The clemency of returning the *kraak* (with its women and children) to the Portuguese after unloading the cargo was a deliberate signal of good faith to the Atjeh court, where the Sultan was watching the European powers' conduct carefully.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, Spilbergh's visit of [[1602]] is the moment at which the new [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] formally arrived in the sultanate — the first VOC fleet reaching Atjeh while Spilbergh was still there, and Spilbergh selling one of his ships to the new Company. The episode also documents the close Dutch-English cooperation at Atjeh in the early years of the European intrusion — cooperation that would not survive the [[Amboyna massacre|Amboyna]] incident of [[1623]] and the long Anglo-Dutch rivalry that followed.
