The **Yam Tuan** was the hereditary ruler of [[Jelebu]] in the Malay Peninsula, against whom the Sayyid Ali — Penghulu of Jelebu — revolted, and whom he eventually displaced as ruler of that small independent state before it came under British protection. The Yam Tuan is mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje in a footnote to his discussion of the Sayyids of Acheh, where the parallel case of Sayyid Ali is adduced to illustrate the way in which a Sayyid's inherited prestige could, in the Malay world, carry him to a political authority that would otherwise have been inaccessible to a foreigner.

## The Penghulu of Jelebu

Snouck Hurgronje's note on the political range of the title *penghulu* explains that in some Malay states the same title was given to an official whose position somewhat resembled that of the *uleebalang* in Acheh. "Such for example," he continues, "was Sayyid Ali the Penghulu of Jelebu in the Malay Peninsula, who revolted against the hereditary Yam Tuan and became the ruler of that small independent state before it came under British protection." The Yam Tuan, in other words, was the indigenous holder of the hereditary chieftaincy of Jelebu, and his displacement by his own penghulu is one of the clearest examples of the way in which a Sayyid's religious prestige — his descent from the Prophet — could be converted, in favourable circumstances, into political power. A member of the same family, Snouck Hurgronje adds, lies buried at Luar Batang in Batavia, and his tomb is widely venerated.

## The Wider Pattern

The case of the Yam Tuan and Sayyid Ali is adduced in a discussion of the Sayyids of Acheh, where it serves to illustrate a wider pattern. Not by any means all of the Sayyids of Acheh were theologians or pandits; most devoted themselves to trade or, in later generations, to agriculture; but they enjoyed none the less the customary reverence based on religious feeling, even where their life was far from exemplary. Some Sayyids — those "who have found their way over from Arabia in the guise of random adventurers" — used that reverence as a stepping-stone to political authority. The revolt of Sayyid Ali against the Yam Tuan of Jelebu is the example Snouck Hurgronje chooses to bring this possibility home to his readers, and it stands as a warning against treating the Sayyids of the Malay world as a purely religious class.
