One of the most important Acehnese religious scholars of the 19th century. The son of an unlearned man from Tiro, he studied in Pidie and at Mecca, where he served as haji-shaikh to pilgrims, before settling at the capital and serving Teuku Kali Malikon Ade and the kali of the XXVI Mukims. A prolific writer, his works include the Minhajoy Abidin (an abbreviated translation of al-Ghazali), the Meunajat, and the Nalam Jawoe. His son-in-law was Teungku di Lam Gut, and his writings, alongside those of Teungku Tiro, are cited by Hurgronje as the high-water mark of Acehnese learning.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Cheh Marahaban

**Cheh Marahaban** was a scholar of [[Pidie]], long resident in [[Mecca]], who during the last years of the Sultanate of [[Acheh]] served as *ulama* and *kali* to the sovereign of the port — that is, to the Sultan himself. He was the son-in-law of [[Teungku Lam Gut]], the chief *kali* of the [[XXVI Mukims]] when the Dutch first came to Acheh; born in [[Lam Gut]] and appointed as the successor of his father, Teungku Lam Gut had some reputation for learning but subsequently changed his abode, in consequence of his marriage, to [[Lam Bhu]] close to the *Dalam*. There he married his daughter to Cheh Marahaban, who was thus able to support his father-in-law in his official work; for Teungku Lam Gut, though a man of sound intelligence, was himself devoid of learning.

In the rare cases where the Sultan of Acheh had need of the advice of those learned in the law, his real adviser was Cheh Marahaban, and not the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Kali]] — the hereditary "supreme judge" who, as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, could neither read nor write. The office of *kali raja* (chief *kali*) thus effectively devolved upon Cheh Marahaban, whose authority as *ulama* of Teuku Kali placed him, in all questions of religious law, above the titular holder of the supreme judicial office.

Cheh Marahaban's example is cited by Snouck Hurgronje in connection with the willingness of some *ulamas* to come to terms with the Dutch, should they have been able to find conclusive arguments strong enough to counterbalance the contumely attending such a step. At the time when the Dutch still occupied a great portion of Acheh, "the example of Cheh Marahaban, the earlier *kali raja* and *ulama* of Teuku Kali, showed clearly enough that some of them would have been quite willing to change sides." His position as *kali raja* and *ulama* of the Sultan's court gave him a unique authority among the learned; his willingness to treat with the infidel — had it been politically feasible — would have carried great weight in favour of submission.

Cheh Marahaban is one of the few Achehnese *ulamas* of the late Sultanate period whose personal name is recorded by Snouck Hurgronje. He exemplifies the type of the learned *kali* — the *ulama* proper, as distinguished from the hereditary title-bearers — on whom the real work of administering justice according to religious law always devolved, even when the formal office belonged to an illiterate *uleebalang*.
