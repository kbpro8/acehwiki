**Cheh Marahaban** — in Kreemer's spelling **Teungkoe Tjhèh Marahaban** — was one of the most important Acehnese religious scholars of the nineteenth century, a Pidiësche *oelama* who served as *ulama* and *kali* (religious judge) to the last two Sultans, [[Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah|Sultan Mantsoer Sjah]] ([[1838]]–[[1870]]) and [[Sultan Ala ad-dïn Mahmoed Sjah|Sultan Ala ad-dïn Mahmoed Sjah]] ([[1870]]–[[1874]]). The son of an unlearned man from [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri|Tirö]] (Tiro), he studied in Pidië and at [[Mecca|Mekka]], where he served as *haji-shaikh* to pilgrims, before settling at the capital and serving [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]] and the *kali* of the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]]. A prolific writer, his works include the *Minhajoy Abidin* (an abbreviated translation of [[Al-Ghazali|al-Ghazali]]), the *Meunajat*, and the *Nalam Jawoe*. His son-in-law was [[Teungku di Lam Gut|Teungku Lam Gut]], and his writings, alongside those of [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]], are cited by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Hurgronje]] as the high-water mark of Acehnese learning.

## Scholarly formation

Tjhèh Marahaban was the son of an unlearned man from [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri|Tirö]]. He studied in Pidië and in [[Mecca|Mekka]], where he attained the rank of *hadji-sjaich*. He was known as a "rustlievende oelama" — a peace-loving oelama — in contrast to the more militant oelama's of the [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri|Tirö]] school.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Cheh Marahaban

In the rare cases where the Sultan of Acheh had need of the advice of those learned in the law, his real adviser was Cheh Marahaban, and not the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Kali]] — the hereditary "supreme judge" who, as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, could neither read nor write. The office of *kali raja* (chief *kali*) thus effectively devolved upon Cheh Marahaban, whose authority as *ulama* of Teuku Kali placed him, in all questions of religious law, above the titular holder of the supreme judicial office. He was the son-in-law of [[Teungku di Lam Gut|Teungku Lam Gut]], the chief *kali* of the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]] when the Dutch first came to Acheh; born in [[Lam Gut|Lam Gut]] and appointed as the successor of his father, Teungku Lam Gut had some reputation for learning but subsequently changed his abode, in consequence of his marriage, to [[Lam Bhu|Lam Bhu]] close to the *Dalam*. There he married his daughter to Cheh Marahaban, who was thus able to support his father-in-law in his official work; for Teungku Lam Gut, though a man of sound intelligence, was himself devoid of learning.

Cheh Marahaban's example is cited by Snouck Hurgronje in connection with the willingness of some *ulamas* to come to terms with the Dutch, should they have been able to find conclusive arguments strong enough to counterbalance the contumely attending such a step. At the time when the Dutch still occupied a great portion of Acheh, "the example of Cheh Marahaban, the earlier *kali raja* and *ulama* of Teuku Kali, showed clearly enough that some of them would have been quite willing to change sides." His position as *kali raja* and *ulama* of the Sultan's court gave him a unique authority among the learned; his willingness to treat with the infidel — had it been politically feasible — would have carried great weight in favour of submission.

Cheh Marahaban is one of the few Achehnese *ulamas* of the late Sultanate period whose personal name is recorded by Snouck Hurgronje. He exemplifies the type of the learned *kali* — the *ulama* proper, as distinguished from the hereditary title-bearers — on whom the real work of administering justice according to religious law always devolved, even when the formal office belonged to an illiterate *uleebalang*.

## Loyalty oath and mosque directorship

In [[1881]], Tjhèh Marahaban swore loyalty to the Gouvernement and became director of the great mosque at [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]], where he established the mosque personnel in [[1881]] with monthly bezoldiging: imeum ƒ75, hatib ƒ50, bileuë ƒ35 each (total ƒ195/month). His son, [[Teungkoe Tjhèh Ibrahim|Teungkoe Tjhèh Ibrahim]], succeeded him as hatib of the mosque and became Kreemer's principal informant.

## Death

Tjhèh Marahaban died in [[1885]], having served as director of the Koeta Radja mosque for only four years after his [[1881]] loyalty oath. His establishment of the mosque personnel — the imeum, hatib, and bileuë — laid the foundation for the institutional structure of the great mosque that continued into Kreemer's time.

## Significance

Cheh Marahaban is significant in the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Atjèh]] history as the learned *kali* and *ulama* under whom the religious-judicial office of the late Sultanate effectively devolved from the hereditary [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Kali]] to the scholarly *ulama* proper. Snouck Hurgronje treats him as the type of the learned *kali* — the man who actually did the work of the supreme judicial office while the titular holder could neither read nor write. Kreemer's account, in turn, supplies the additional facts of his service to the last two Sultans (Mantsoer Sjah and Ala ad-dïn Mahmoed Sjah), his loyalty oath and mosque directorship at Koeta Radja in [[1881]], and his founding of the institutional structure of the great mosque — the imeum, hatib, and bileuë — that would continue under his son [[Teungkoe Tjhèh Ibrahim|Tjhèh Ibrahim]] into Kreemer's own time.
