**Captain Francis Light** (c. 1740 – [[1794]]) was the British sea-captain and country-trader who, in [[1786]], founded the British settlement of [[Penang|Pulau Pinang]] on behalf of the East India Company, and who figures in *The Achehnese* as one of the earliest European observers to grasp the practical impossibility of establishing a secure European settlement on the Achehnese coast. His warning, recorded in correspondence with the Governor-General of [[Bengal|Bengal]] in the same year that [[Warren Hastings|Warren Hastings]] received a discourteous letter from the Sultan of [[Acheh]], proved prophetic of every subsequent attempt — British and Dutch alike — to deal with Acheh by ordinary diplomatic or commercial means.

Light's advice to his superiors was that "a settlement at Pulau Pinang possessed greater advantages than one on the Achehnese coast". His description of Acheh, quoted at length by Snouck Hurgronje in the Introduction to *The Achehnese*, is one of the classic European appraisals of the country's situation before the colonial wars. "Acheen", he wrote, "is a good road but no place of security against an enemy there. The country is fertile beyond description and very populous. The inhabitants rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous. To form a settlement there of safety and advantage, a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary".

The last phrase is the heart of Light's assessment. He recognised that the difficulty of Acheh lay not in its military strength — for the country was "hopelessly divided against itself" — but in the very absence of central authority, which meant that no treaty with a Sultan could bind the countless miniature states under chiefs whose power depended on personal energy, and who were "generally in a state of warfare with their neighbours unless in peace and alliance with them for the joint perpetration of their national offences". The foreigner wronged in Achehnese waters "sought vainly for some authority that might redress his wrongs".

Snouck treats Light, together with [[James Price]] and (later) [[Governor R. Fullerton|Governor R. Fullerton]] of [[Penang|Penang]], as having "grasped the situation in its most literal sense" half a century before the Dutch. "Truly Francis Light and James Price, whom we have already quoted, had grasped the situation in its most literal sense. It was necessary 'to subdue all the chiefs', and their name was legion!" Only as guerillas against guerillas, Snouck continues, by using the most lightly equipped native troops under the leadership of first-rate European officers, could the work be done — a prescription that anticipates the very tactics later imposed on the Dutch by [[Governor Van Heutsz|Governor Van Heutsz]] and [[General van der Heijden|General van der Heijden]].

Light's decision to settle Penang rather than Acheh thus deflected British ambitions away from the northern tip of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], and left the problem of Achehnese disorder to be re-discovered by the Dutch in the second quarter of the nineteenth century. His forecast of [[1873]] was, in effect, fulfilled.
