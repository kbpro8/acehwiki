Sultan of Acheh, reigned approximately [[1838]]-[[1870]]. At his instance, the religious-political leader Teungku Kuta Karang (Shaikh Abbas) wrote the Taj-ul-mulk, an important compendium of Islamic mystic arts, prognostications, and native medicine.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Ibrahim Manso Shah

**Ibrahim Manso Shah** (also **Ibrahim Mansur Shah**) was a Sultan of [[Acheh]] who reigned from [[1858]] to [[1870]]. His reign is mentioned in *The Achehnese* chiefly in connection with the disputed succession to the *panglimaship* of the [[XXVI Mukims]], one of the three *sagis* (territorial divisions) of [[Great Acheh]], which took place during his reign and is characteristic of the constitutional disorder of the late Sultanate.

The *panglima* of the [[XXVI Mukims]] had long been a person of small importance. When the holder of the title died during the reign of Ibrahim Manso Shah, one [[Teuku Muda Lampaseh]] was, according to the *adat*, the proper successor; he was accordingly, after reporting himself in the *Dalam*, solemnly recognised by the Sultan as *panglima*. Even before he had quitted the *Dalam*, however, the firing of guns was heard from the direction of the XXVI Mukims, and it appeared on enquiry that the people of that district, who objected to Teuku Muda Lampaseh on the ground that he was an opium-smoker, had installed a younger brother of the deceased *panglima* as the latter's successor. This was [[Teuku Chut Lamreueng]], father of the later *panglima* [[Teuku Nya' Banta]], who is also called Teuku Lamreueng.

The Sultan knew no better way out of the difficulty than to recognise the second aspirant as well, so that there were then two *panglimas* in the XXVI Mukims. This doubled *panglimaship* is one of the clearest examples of the inability of the late Sultanate to enforce its own decisions even in matters of succession within the *sagis* of Great Acheh itself. At the commencement of the Achehnese War both chiefs fled to [[Keumala]], but Teuku Lamreueng in the end re-established himself in his territory and tendered his submission to the Dutch Government; for this he was murdered in [[Pidie]], whither he had gone to fetch his family, by the adherents of Teuku Lampaseh. His infant son escaped the same fate through the help of a faithful servant, and was afterwards recognised by the Government as Panglima of the XXVI Mukims.

The reign of Ibrahim Manso Shah thus coincides with the period of the most rapid decay of the Sultanate's effective authority. The Sultan's inability to settle a disputed succession within his own capital territory — and his recourse to the compromise of recognising both claimants — exemplifies the constitutional principle, repeatedly stressed by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], that the Sultan of Acheh had by this time become little more than *primus inter pares* among the chiefs of Great Acheh, and that even his formal recognition of a new *panglima* or *uleebalang* was no more than the stamp set upon a settlement already reached by the chiefs and people of the district concerned.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah

**Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah**, known in Achehnese pronunciation as **Manso Shah** and styled in full Alaedin Mansur Shah, was [[Sultan|Sultan of Acheh]] from [[1858]] to [[1870]]. He was the father of Sultan Alaedin Muhamad Daud Shah Juhan (the pretender Tuanku Muhamat Dawot), the last sultan recognised by the Achehnese before the final establishment of Dutch rule over the kingdom. His name appears in the centre of the [[chab sikureung|ninefold seal]] of the sultanate, where his son's patent invokes "Sultan Alaedin Manso Shah Juhan, the Blessed, Allah's shadow in the world" as the sire of the reigning prince.

Snouck Hurgronje's portrait of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah is brief but pointed. From his widow Snouck learned that the sultan had once murdered his own child in a fit of drunken frenzy. The anecdote is introduced in *The Achehnese* Vol. I to illustrate the prevalence of intoxicating liquors in the [[Dalam]], the royal palace at [[Banda Acheh|Banda Acheh]], notwithstanding the honoured Achehnese tradition by which any member of the Sultan's family with the reputation of being even a moderate opium-smoker was excluded from the succession. The open presence of strong drink in the Dalam thus coexisted, paradoxically, with a severe adat against the use of opium in the royal house.

The reign of Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah fell in the period of accelerating collapse of the central power of the sultanate. The descendants of the great Sultan [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] had long since ceased to exercise effective authority beyond the immediate neighbourhood of the capital, and the territorial chiefs — the uleebalangs of the three sagis and the panglima sagi of the highlands — had become the real wielders of power in Great Acheh. His son and successor, the child Tuanku Muhamat Dawot, was elevated to the sultanate in [[1879]] (A.H. 1296) after the death of Sultan Mahmut Shah, and lived to see the destruction of the Dalam and the establishment of the Dutch colonial government in [[Kuta Raja]].

In the formal documents of his house, Sultan Ibrahim Mansur Shah is remembered chiefly as the father of the last sultan. His name recurs in the genealogical preamble of the letters patent issued by his son to [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] in [[1889]], where his son styles himself "son of the late Sultan Alaedin Manso Shah Juhan, the Blessed, Allah's shadow in the world." Through this formula the memory of Manso Shah remained attached, in the formal style of the dying sultanate, to the last Achehnese effort to express the prerogatives of royalty.
