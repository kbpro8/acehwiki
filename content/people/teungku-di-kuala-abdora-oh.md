**Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh**, known as "the saint of Singkel," is one of the great saints of Acheh whose tomb is the goal of a popular pilgrimage. He is identified by Snouck Hurgronje with [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel|Abdurra'uf of Singkel]] (Teungku di Kuala alias Abdora'oh), the renowned Achehnese Sufi author of the seventeenth century, whose shrine at Kuala Singkel on the West Coast long enjoyed the highest reputation of any holy place in Acheh. By the early twentieth century, the tomb of Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh had been outshone in popular veneration only by that of [[Teungku Anjong]] in Gampong Java; but it remained, in Snouck Hurgronje's classification, one of the two pre-eminent saints' resting-places to which parents vowed pilgrimages on behalf of their sick children.

## The Vow of Pilgrimage

A common Achehnese custom, described in detail by Snouck Hurgronje, was for the father or mother of a sick child to vow, at the sickbed, that in the event of recovery they would not merely bring the child to the nearest holy tomb to inaugurate its first contact with the earth, but would take it on a pilgrimage to the resting-place of one of the great saints of Acheh. The two saints named in this connection are Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh, "the saint of Singkel," and the holy Sayyid Teungku Anjong buried in Gampong Java. In such cases the child is escorted to the sacred spot by a great procession of men and women, and cattle are slaughtered and a feast given at the tomb. Where the vow includes the expression of an intention to head the procession with *geundrang*, the two drums (or *geundrang*) and the flute (*srune*) are marched in front.

## Historical and Religious Importance

The pilgrimage to Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh represents one of the most distinctive features of Achehnese popular religion: the practice of attaching a vow to a particular saint's tomb, in the belief that the barakah of the saint can intercede for the recovery of the sick. The saint of Singkel occupies a unique place in this devotional system, partly because of his historical reputation as a Sufi author and traveller, and partly because his shrine lay at a distance from the capital which made the pilgrimage to it a serious undertaking. The very difficulty of the journey enhanced the value of the vow and the merit of its fulfilment. Even after the shrine of Teungku Anjong had, in popular estimation, surpassed that of Abdora'oh, the saint of Singkel continued to draw pilgrims from the whole of Acheh, and the long processions with drums and flutes which accompanied the recovered child to his tomb remained a familiar sight of Achehnese religious life.
