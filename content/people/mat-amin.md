**Mat Amin** was the eldest son of the great Achehnese ulama [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] (Muhamat Amin), and during his father's lifetime acted as his chief *panglima* (military commander) in the [[Acheh War|war against the Dutch]]. He is the subject of an extended portrait by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in Vol. I of *The Achehnese*, where he is held up as a cautionary example of the way in which the religious authority of a great ulama could be transmitted, in degenerate form, to a son who lacked every quality that had made the father formidable.

Mat Amin, Snouck Hurgronje explains, "possessed no more religious learning as every person of ordinary piety requires to know, and was always distinguished by the title *teungku* as being the son of the great ulama." As a matter of fact, however, he was "a guerilla chief and nothing else, and borrowed from the great authority which his father's name everywhere inspired an influence which raised him to the position of chief panglima." In this capacity he and his bands proved "a great burden to the people," and his life was "in other respects also far from harmonizing with the teaching of his father."

The most celebrated episode in the relations between father and son concerns the alleged poisoning of [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]]. During the last years of the latter's life, Mat Amin lived openly with another man's wife, and Teungku Tiro, to give him a severe lesson, forbade him access to his presence. This appears to have so enraged the young man that he brooded over some device to rid him of his tiresome father. When Teungku Tiro, while still in the full vigour of life, suddenly fell ill, he is said to have expressed the conviction that poison had been administered to him by some agent of his son. Notwithstanding this, he had Mat Amin summoned to his bedside when dying, and solemnly warned him to go to the devout ulamas for advice, and to forsake his evil ways. He seemed, however, to realise how little chance there was of his son's ever proving his true successor in any respect.

After his father's death Mat Amin gave the rein more than ever to his evil passions. He assumed the dress and manners of an uleebalang: while his father, in receiving distinguished guests, had always risen to meet them, the young man would remain sitting until they came up to him. "Even the Achehnese consider Mat Amin proud, ignorant and headstrong," Snouck Hurgronje observes, "and when after his father's death he gave the rein more than ever to his evil passions, the great body of the adherents of [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] at once melted away."

The case of Mat Amin is cited by Snouck Hurgronje as a typical instance of the structural fragility of charismatic authority in Acheh. The religious prestige of a great ulama could not be inherited; and the moment the personal qualities on which it rested were seen to be wanting, the movement collapsed. Teungku Tiro's other sons — [[Teungku Beb]] (died [[1900]]) and [[Teungku Mahidin]] (or Ma' Et) — together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died [[1901]]), much respected for his piety and learning, continued after Mat Amin's death to do their utmost to maintain the collection of the [[Sabil contributions (lui sabi aqq sabil)|sabil contributions]] in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]; but, as Snouck Hurgronje notes in a translator's bracketed addition, [[Great Acheh]] "no longer furnishes a field for the labour of these men and their fellows."

Mat Amin himself was killed in action in [[1896]], when the Dutch troops captured the fortress of [[Battle of Aneu Galong (1896)|Aneu' Galong]]. His death closed the brief and inglorious chapter of the son's military career, and confirmed the lesson of his father's dying warning.

## Account in Hooijer's *De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh* ([[1897]])

**Mat Amin** — written by [[G. B. Hooijer]] as *Mad Amin* and *Mad-Amin* — was the son and successor of the *oelama* [[Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro|Teungkoe di Tiro]] and the principal leader of the *[[Moslemin]]* religious war-party in [[Groot-Atjeh]] during the years of the Dutch alliance with [[Teukoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]], from whose territory he was expelled in [[1893]] and against whose bands the whole system of Dutch outposts described in *[[De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh (Hooijer, 1897)|De verlaten posten in Groot-Atjeh]]* was directed.

## Succession to Teungkoe di Tiro

Hooijer recorded that [[Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro|Oelama Teungkoe di Tiro]], the spiritual leader of the Achehnese in the holy war, had died at [[Anagaloeng]] lying on the gravestone that had been erected there for [[Kapitein Miseroy]] during the period of Dutch occupation, a morally and physically broken man whose influence with friend and foe had declined. Hooijer then reported, explicitly as hearsay — *naar men zeide* ("as it was said") — that Mad Amin had poisoned his father in rage at the strictures the *oelama* had made upon his immoral conduct.

## Expulsion by Toekoe Oemar

Before the alliance, [[Teukoe Oemar]] had shown himself willing to resist the *geestdrijvers* (religious zealots) by thrashing Mad Amin and his companions out of his own territory, and it was precisely this demonstration that persuaded the Dutch that Oemar's *kerfstok* might be overlooked and his services employed.

## The struggle in the XXVI and XXII Moekims

When Oemar was set to clear the [[Sagi der XXVI Moekims|XXVI Moekims]], fighters streamed in from the coastal lands to place themselves under Mad Amin's command, including a party of two hundred from [[Samalanga]] under an opponent of the pro-Dutch *[[Oeleebalang]]* of that district. Oemar nevertheless dispersed Mad Amin's bands entirely in [[November]] [[1893]] once Dutch troops had occupied [[Tjot Rang]] to cover his rest. By the time the Dutch built their *benteng* at [[Anagaloeng]], Hooijer could write that Mad Amin had been driven out of both the [[XXII Moekims]] and the XXVI Moekims, and that Dutch soldiers were quartered *in den navel van het land* — "in the navel of the country," as the Achehnese called Anagaloeng, the granary of the XXII Moekims.

## Continuing threat

The expulsion was not final. In [[March]] [[1895]] Dutch troops at [[Tjot Goeë]] came under heavy fire from *kwaad volk* gathering in the [[IX Moekims]], and in [[1896]] the *Moslemin* returned in force wherever Achehnese auxiliaries withdrew. Hooijer observed that when [[Toekoe Nja Banta]] transferred the women of [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]] from the Toengkoeb district to [[Lamreng]] inside the *[[Geconcentreerde linie|geconcentreerde linie]]*, and the population consequently dispersed, the *Moslemin* immediately occupied the abandoned terrain and cut communications between [[Kroeng Kali]] and [[Toengkoeb]].

## Significance

Mat Amin represented, in Hooijer's account, the continuity of the religious resistance from his father's generation into the alliance period, and the reason for which the Dutch judged an Achehnese auxiliary indispensable. That the *Moslemin* under his leadership could be driven from a district but not destroyed was the structural weakness of the whole outpost system, which Hooijer conceded had been raised *op ondeugdelijken grondslag*.
