**Al‑Busiri** (full name **Sharaf al‑Dīn Abū ʿAbdallāh Muḥammad ibn Saʿīd al‑Dulāst al‑Būsīrī**, AH [[608]]–[[694]] / [[1212]]–[[1296]]) was an Arab‑Berber‑origin Egyptian **Sufi poet**, calligrapher and scholar of the [[Shadhiliyya]] order whose pan‑Islamic ode, the *Qasīda al‑Burda* (commonly called the *Burda* or “Poem of the Cloak”), became one of the most widely recited devotional works in the Islamic world and a cornerstone of religious practice in the Sultanate of [[Acheh]] and the broader Malay world.  Tradition records that he composed the poem after a paralytic stroke was miraculously healed in a dream in which the Prophet [[Muhammad]] cast his mantle over the poet, a legend that underlies the poem’s title and its later reputation as a source of spiritual and physical protection.  Contemporary scholarship places the composition in [[1245]] CE, during the reign of Sultan al‑Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl of the [[Mamluk sultanate]], while later sources locate his death in [[1295]] or [[1296]] in [[Alexandria]].

## Life and Career

### Birth and Early Years  
According to the majority of biographical accounts, al‑Busiri was born in [[1212]] (c. [[1213]]) in the village of [[Abusir|Būsīr al‑Malak]] in the Egyptian province of [[Bani Suef]], a toponym that gave him the nisba *al‑Būsīrī*.  He was of Berber descent.  Some later narratives state that he was born in [[Alexandria]] and later moved to [[Cairo]], where he entered the court of Sultan al‑Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl.  A still‑more concise tradition records his birthplace simply as [[Cairo]], emphasizing his lifelong association with the Egyptian capital’s scholarly circles.  The coexistence of these accounts is noted by scholars without a definitive resolution.

### Education and Occupation  
Al‑Busiri spent most of his adult life in the city of [[Bilbis]] (modern [[Alexandria]]), earning his livelihood as a copyist of manuscripts and a teacher of *ḥadīth* and *qirāʾah* (Qur’anic recitation).  He was also a member of the [[Shadhiliyya]] Sufi order, undertaking pilgrimages and study trips that deepened his mystical outlook.  His reputation as a calligrapher and scholar spread throughout the Mamluk realm.

### Death  
He died in [[1295]] (c. [[1296]]) in [[Alexandria]]; his tomb is said to lie near that of [[Imam al‑Shafiʿi]] in [[Fustat]] (Old Cairo).  The exact year varies between sources, with some manuscripts recording [[1295]] and others [[1296]].

## Composition of the *Qasida al‑Burda* (Al‑Burda)

Al‑Busiri is said to have composed the *Burda* after a dream in which the Prophet Muhammad healed his paralysis and bestowed a cloak upon him.  The poem’s full title, *al‑Kawākib al‑durriyya fī madḥ khayr al‑bariyya* (“The Shining Stars in Praise of the Best of Creation”), reflects this miraculous origin story.  According to the later source, the composition took place in [[1245]] CE while al‑Busiri was serving at the Mamluk court, and the poem’s theological depth—emphasising the Prophet as “the lord of both worlds”—made it a staple of Sufi devotional practice.

The *Burda* consists of 161 verses (some editions count 160) arranged in ten chapters covering the Prophet’s birth, miracles, the Qur’an, the Israʾ and Miʿrāj, battles, intercession, and the poet’s supplication.  Its lyrical structure and vivid imagery secured its popularity across the Islamic world.

### Manuscript Tradition and Editions  
The *editio princeps* of the Arabic text was published at Leiden in [[1761]] by [[Joannes Uri]], a student of [[J. I. Schultens]].  Later critical editions include those of [[V. von Rosenzweig]] ([[1824]]), with a German translation; [[C. A. Ralfs]] ([[1860]]), with interlinear Persian and Turkish; and [[René Basset]] ([[1894]]), French translation with comparative notes.  Numerous commentaries were written, notably by [[Ibn Hajar al‑Haytami]], [[Zarqani]] and [[Bajuri]].

## Transmission and Reception in the Malay World and Aceh

### Early Malay Translations  
The oldest known Malay version of the *Burda* is a 16th‑century translation edited by [[S. G. Evenhage]] (published in [[1955]]).  The manuscript survives in a single copy from the [[Thomas Erpenius]] collection, acquired via [[Pieter Willemsz. Floris van Elbingh]] in [[Aceh]] in [[1604]], establishing a terminus ad quem of ca. [[1600]].  A second, fragmentary Acehnese translation was identified by [[P. Voorhoeve]] in the Bataviaas Genootschap manuscript B.G. 303.  The interlinear format of the Malay translation reflects the pedagogical practice of studying Arabic texts through word‑for‑word vernacular glosses.

### Use in Acehnese Religious Life  
Copies of the *Al‑Burda* attributed to al‑Busiri are held in Acehnese archives, evidencing the poem’s early arrival in the region.  In Aceh the poem became a staple of [[Mawlid]] celebrations (locally called *dikië jadjandji*), sung in Arabic or rendered into Acehnese, often alongside indigenous [[kasida]] forms.  Its inclusion in local *mawlid* rites and manuscript anthologies testifies to its lasting impact on Acehnese devotional culture.

According to the 16th‑century Malay translation, the *Burda* was recited on the 15th of [[Ramadan]] in Ottoman‑controlled holy sites, and the water used to wet the cloak was bottled and given to members of the Acehnese royal household for use during the remaining days of Ramadan.  The poem’s verses were also sung at funerary rites in Egyptian villages, a practice observed by Acehnese pilgrims on the Hajj and brought back to their homeland.

The Malay translation of the *Burda* was copied in Acehnese *kuttab* (primary schools) and recited during *maulid* celebrations.  Al‑Busiri’s poetic motifs—such as the battle against *shaitan* and the promise of divine forgiveness—found resonance in Acehnese *syair* and *gamelan* chants.

Mid‑16th‑century records note that the poem reached the Acehnese court by the mid‑16th century, where it was translated into Malay and incorporated into local religious poetry, serving as a literary tool for Acehnese scholars to legitimize the sultanate’s claim to be a defender of the Islamic faith.

## Critical Reception

The *Burda* attracted criticism from [[Wahhabi]] reformers (followers of [[Muhammad ibn Abd al‑Wahhab]]) in the [[18th century]], who objected to its invocation of the Prophet as intercessor (*shafāʿa*) and to the belief in the mantle’s miraculous healing power, deeming these practices *shirk* (associationism).  [[S. G. Evenhage]] discusses this criticism in his introduction (pp. 31–34).

## Significance

Al‑Busiri’s *Burda* stands as a seminal work of Arabic devotional poetry whose linguistic artistry and spiritual themes transcended regional boundaries, shaping religious expression from the Arab heartlands to Southeast Asia.  Its transmission to Aceh illustrates the dynamic flow of Islamic literary culture across the Indian Ocean and its capacity to be re‑interpreted within local ritual contexts, where it functions both as a devotional text and as a protective amulet.
