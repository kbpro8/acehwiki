**The Sultan of Atjeh (16th century)** was the unnamed [[Aceh Sultanate|Atjehnese sultan]] whose recent conversion to Islam and whose expansionist campaign against the Sumatran east-coast polities — recorded by [[Mendez Pinto|Pinto]] as the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjeh-Aru war of 1539]] — marks the formal opening of the Atjehnese maritime age. He is the principal Atjehnese actor of the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)]], and the figure whose brother-in-law [[Alleredin Mahomet]] commanded the [[Atjehsche vloot|Atjehnese fleet]] of 130 vessels that brought [[Aroe]] to its fall.

## The conversion and the expansion

[[Mendez Pinto|Pinto]], at the opening of his account of the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjeh-Aru war]], records that "[[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s sultan's transition to Islam was a welcome motive for him to throw himself on his neighbours right and left, in order to bring them by fire and sword to submission and the new faith". The conversion — which De Roo de la Faille does not date precisely but which the political context of [[1539]] places in the early sixteenth century — was, on Pinto's reading, the ideological instrument by which the sultan prosecuted his territorial and maritime design: the closure of the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] to Portuguese shipping, and the absorption of the east-coast Hindu-Javanese commonwealth into the Atjehnese sphere.

## The 1539 design on Aroe

The [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|war on Aroe]] was the central act of the sultan's expansion. The [[Atjehsche vloot|fleet]] of 130 vessels, under the command of his brother-in-law [[Alleredin Mahomet]] — who also held the office of *Gouverneur du Royaume de Baarros*, i.e. governor of [[Barus|Barus]] on the Sumatran west coast — was dispatched to take [[Aroe]]: "to the possession of Aroe Atjeh was much attached; it was the station by which Atjeh was able to command the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] and to deny Portuguese ships and other traders the passage to [[Sunda]], the [[Moluccas]] and [[China]]". The sultan's strategic purpose was thus not territorial in the first instance but maritime and commercial — the closure of the Strait to Portuguese shipping.

## The Puteri Idjo suit

The same sultan — or his immediate successor — is, in the [[Puteri Idjo|Putri Hijau]] legend of [[Deli]] and [[Asahan]], the Atjehnese sultan who pressed a marriage suit on the fairy-tale queen [[Puteri Idjo|Puteri Idjo]] of [[Deli]]. The queen's refusal, her illness and death, and the loss of her "glass coffin" at sea on the way to Atjeh are, in [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]]'s reading, the *bedekte taal* record of the sultan's design on the [[Deli]]-[[Asahan]]-[[Aroe]] cultural zone — a design whose outcome, the legend insists, was frustrated not by war but by the queen's death.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, the Sultan of Atjeh (16th century) is the principal Atjehnese actor of the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)]] — the figure whose conversion to Islam and whose expansionist campaign mark the formal opening of the Atjehnese maritime age. His strategic design on the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] and on the [[Deli]]-[[Asahan]]-[[Aroe]] cultural zone is the political frame against which the early-modern history of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Atjehnese sultanate]] is to be measured.
