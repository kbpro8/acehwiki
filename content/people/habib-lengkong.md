**Habib Lengkong** — also written *Habib Lengkočng*, *Habib Tepicèng Wan* — was a [[Habib (concept)|Habib]] (descendant of the Prophet Muhammad) with "disciples from all parts of Atjeh" who, in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*, "occupied the [[Teungkoe di Lengkong]] fort at the edge of the [[Oostergebergte]]" in the spring of [[1896]]. The very brief references to Lengkong — comprising only two page citations in the master list — nonetheless establish him as one of the most distinctive figures of the post-[[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection]] Achehnese religious-military landscape, because the fort he occupied was named after another Teungkoe di Lengkong whose own authority Lengkong had apparently inherited or superseded. His name's alternative form, "Habib Tepicèng Wan", and the location at the edge of the eastern mountain range, suggest a north-eastern Achehnese or Pedir-origin Habib whose religious authority had been extended into the eastern sector of [[Groot-Atjeh]].

## Occupation of the Teungkoe di Lengkong Fort

The [[Teungkoe di Lengkong]] fort — at the edge of the [[Oostergebergte]] (Eastern Mountain Range) which bordered the [[XXVI Moekims]] on the east — was originally the seat of the *teungkoe* whose name it bore. Kruisheer's description of Lengkong as occupying the fort "with disciples from all parts of Atjeh" suggests that the Habib's authority had displaced or absorbed that of the original *teungkoe*, and that the fort had been transformed from a local religious-military seat into a regional strongpoint of the Hadhrami/Arab-descended Habib network. The disciples "from all parts of Atjeh" indicate that Lengkong's network was unusually geographically dispersed, drawing followers not only from the immediate [[XXVI Moekims]] area but from across the Achehnese territories. This geographic reach is consistent with the broader pattern of Habib activity in the spring of [[1896]]: [[Habib Lhong]] (a Kling) had operated in the western [[XXII Moekims]]; [[Habib Samalanga]] had nested at [[Lamsoet]] in the [[III Moekims Kerkoen]]; [[Habib Koeala van Pedir]] (an Afghan) had been killed in the [[Tuchtiging of Lam-Krak Moekims (9–10 June 1896)|tuchtiging of Lam-Krak]]; and [[Habib Lengkong]] occupied the eastern frontier. Together, these figures formed a network of religious-military strongpoints which spanned the full circumference of [[Groot-Atjeh]].

## The Eastern Frontier and the Oostergebergte

The [[Oostergebergte]] (Eastern Mountain Range) formed the natural eastern boundary of the [[XXVI Moekims]] and separated the Groot-Atjeh theatre from the eastern coastal *landschappen* of [[Samalanga]], [[Peusangan]] and [[Pidie|Pedir]]. The location of the [[Teungkoe di Lengkong]] fort at the edge of this range gave it strategic value as a forward defensive position against any Dutch advance from the east, and as a base from which Achehnese forces could descend into the XXVI Moekims. The fort is not listed among the principal targets of the June [[1896]] Dutch operations — the [[Tuchtiging der V Moekims Montassik (16–17 June 1896)|tuchtiging of V Moekims Montassik on 16–17 June 1896]] and the [[Ladoeng Landing and Glé Broeë Attack (22–23 June 1896)|Ladoeng landing of 22–23 June 1896]] were directed at the southern and eastern sectors respectively — which suggests that the Dutch considered the Lengkong fort to be a secondary priority. The fact that Lengkong was able to maintain his position through the end of the first semester of [[1896]] — the period covered by *ATJEH '96* — indicates that the eastern frontier remained an Achehnese-held sone throughout the spring operations.

## Significance

Habib Lengkong's significance in *ATJEH '96* lies in his embodiment of the Habib network's geographic reach and its capacity to absorb and supersede local *teungkoe* authority. The occupation of the [[Teungkoe di Lengkong]] fort at the edge of the [[Oostergebergte]] with disciples "from all parts of Atjeh" represents the most explicit case in the balso of a Habib establishing a regional religious-military strongpoint on the eastern frontier, and his presence confirms that the Hadhrami/Arab-descended Habib network was active across the full circumference of [[Groot-Atjeh]] in the spring of [[1896]]. Though Kruisheer's references are brief, the figure of Lengkong completes the geographic picture of Habib activity which the balso otherwise assembles only piecemeal.
