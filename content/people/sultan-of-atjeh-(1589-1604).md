**The Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)** — the Achehnese ruler who received [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]] in [[1599]], [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] in [[1600]], the Middelburgsche Compagnie in [[1601]], [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] in [[1602]] and [[Joris van Spilbergen|Joris van Spilbergen]] later that year, and who played the European rivals against each other in the years when the Dutch, English and French first appeared together on the Sumatran pepper coast — is the central Achehnese figure of Johan van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]]. He is conventionally identified as **Ala'uddin Ri'ayat Shah Sayyid al-Mukammil**, who reigned from [[1589]] to [[1604]] and under whose rule the sultanate of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] reached one of its early-modern peaks of commercial and diplomatic activity with the northern European powers.

## The European rivals and the sultan's balancing policy

The sultan inherited, and continued, a long-standing policy of playing the [[Portuguese]] at [[Malaka]] off against the rulers of [[Djohor]], while Atjeh itself sought to draw the Mohammedan trade of the straits into its own hands. Van der Woude frames the sultan's conduct toward the newcomers as the same tactic applied to a wider field of competitors. When [[Cornelis de Houtman|Cornelis de Houtman]] appeared in [[Atjeh]] on [[24 June]] [[1599]], the sultan received him "welwillend", offered him a gift as a token of good will and gave him leave to trade; a house was assigned to the Dutch on shore. The Portuguese factor at Atjeh, **[[Affonso Vicenti|Affonso Vicenti]]** — a Malaka-based Portuguese resident whose business was to influence the sultan against any new competitor — turned the sultan against the Dutch by proposing that Atjeh should attack the ships. The sultan accepted the proposal, and on [[11 September]] [[1599]] the ambush took place in which Cornelis de Houtman was killed and [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] taken prisoner.

The sultan's subsequent conduct shows the same calculating stance. When a Portuguese fleet under **[[André Furtado de Mendonça|André Furtado de Mendonça]]** appeared off Atjeh in [[1601]], the sultan — already angered by Portuguese intrigues to obtain his strongest fort — refused to be bullied, allowed his gunners to fire on a Portuguese ship that was making prizes of Arab vessels, and disarmed and imprisoned the crew. Van der Woude observes that the sultan "waakte er voor de Portugeezen tot vijanden te maken", and that his attitude softened toward the Dutch prisoners as soon as the new Middelburgsche Compagnie fleet arrived and Portuguese power visibly waned.

## Treaties and counter-treaties (1600-1602)

The sultan's negotiations with the Dutch oscillated between generosity and treachery. With [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]], who appeared on [[21 September]] [[1600]], the sultan at first freed the survivors of the Houtman affair, including **[[Hans Decker|Hans Decker]]**, then signed a contract on [[28 December]] [[1600]] granting the Dutch a preferential right to the new pepper harvest. When Van Caerden, alarmed by reports that the sultan planned an attack, seized pepper from Arab junks at anchor on [[12 January]] [[1601]], the sultan answered with cannon fire from the fort and the treaty threatened to collapse. The sultan simultaneously pursued a parallel diplomacy: he refused to surrender the Dutch prisoners in exchange for a ship, and demanded an advance payment for the contracted delivery. Van Caerden's unilateral action — condemned by the Zeeland merchants Gerard le Roy and Laurens Bicker in [[1601]] as exceeding his commission — was repaired only by the sultan's willingness to release Frederik de Houtman without ransom when the Zeeland fleet arrived.

When the **[[Middelburgsche Compagnie|Middelburgsche Compagnie]]** fleet under **Hans Huybrecht Tonneman** arrived on [[23 August]] [[1601]] bearing a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]], the sultan received the Zeelanders solemnly, accepted gifts in money and merchandise, and "liet Frederik de Houtman de boeien afnemen, die hij acht maanden lang had gedragen". The sultan then dispatched his own embassy to Prince Maurits — the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits of 1601]] — which Van der Woude, citing [[Huigh de Groot|Huigh de Groot]], treats as a turning-point in Atjeh-Dutch relations. The sultan permitted two Zeeland commiezen and three assistants to settle on shore at a lodge named "Zeelant", and even gave them leave to send two of their number to **Cambay** with his letters of favour to open trade with Guzerat.

## The sultan and the English (1602)

With the arrival of [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]] with four English ships, the sultan extended the same balancing policy to a third European power. Van der Woude records Lancaster's reception: the sultan received him "met veel eerbetoon in een plechtige audiëntie", regaled him with a banquet, feasts and dances, and exchanged gifts that were transported in procession by richly caparisoned elephants. The sultan praised Queen Elizabeth as "a princesse of great noblenesse, for fame speaketh so much of her". He granted Lancaster leave to trade, but Lancaster found the prices far higher than his pilot John Davis had led him to expect and lacked capital for a full cargo. The sultan was shrewdly co-operative in keeping Lancaster's arrival secret from the Portuguese at Malaka. When Lancaster proposed to ambush the annual Portuguese carrack from the Koromandel coast, the sultan supported the plan, and Lancaster, [[Joris van Spilbergen|Joris van Spilbergen]] and the Dutch crews together captured the carrack on [[3 October]] [[1602]] in the Strait of Malaka — a feat Van der Woude treats as the first major joint Anglo-Dutch naval operation in the East Indies, made possible by the sultan's good offices.

## The sultan's religious and strategic posture

The sultan's repeated pressure on Frederik de Houtman to convert to Islam — "die niet afliet hem te pressen Mohammedaan te worden" — is recorded by Van der Woude as one of the few direct glimpses we get of the sultan's personal religious disposition. Strategically, the sultan's aim was constant: to keep Atjeh's pepper trade open to the highest bidder, to deny Malaka its monopoly, and to recover the wingewest of **Aru** from [[Djohor]]. The [[1587]] peace between Atjeh and the Portuguese had transferred Aru to Djohor; the sultan's hostility to Djohor and his parallel hostility to the Portuguese defined his European policy. He neither became a Dutch ally nor a Portuguese client, and his court became the chief theatre in which the rivalry of the northern European companies was first played out.

## Significance

The Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604) is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the central indigenous figure of the opening Dutch-Atjeh chapter. His reign spans the entire arc from the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers' visit of 1599]] through the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy of 1601]] to the Anglo-Dutch joint capture of the Portuguese carrack of [[1602]] — the foundational decade of Atjeh-northern-European relations. Van der Woude treats his policy as the model instance of the Achehnese court's commercial statecraft: a small but powerful Islamic sultanate, sitting at the northern mouth of the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]], extracting maximum advantage from the simultaneous appearance of three European trading nations and refusing to commit to any of them. This precedent would shape every subsequent phase of Atjeh-European diplomacy down to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1903]].
