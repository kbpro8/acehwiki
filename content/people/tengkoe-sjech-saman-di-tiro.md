**Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro** — *Tgk. Sjech Saman di Tiro*, signing himself in Arabic *Hadji SJECH SAMAN TIROU* — was head of the [[Tiro-oelama|Tiro family]] and the most powerful and dangerous of all the [[Oelama|oelama's]] who led the [[Aceh War|Atjeh resistance]]. The Tiro-oelama's, Zentgraff writes, descended from a Javanese *hadji* who had long before settled in the country; under Tgk. Sjech Saman the family reached the zenith of its influence. He had four sons — [[Tengkoe Mat Amin di Tiro|Tgk. Mat Amin]], [[Tengkoe Majet di Tiro|Tgk. Majet]], [[Tengkoe di Boekit|Tgk. Beb (di Boekit)]] (so called because his mother came from Boekit Seboen), and Tgk. Lam or Lambada — and one or more grandsons, of whom the last was [[Tengkoe Tjhi Maät di Tiro|Tgk. Tjhi Maät]].

## Organiser of the prang sabil

After the removal of [[Habib Abdoerrachman el Zahir]] to [[Mekka]] in [[1878]], the leadership of the [[prang sabil|holy war]] fell to Tgk. Sjech Saman. He travelled the land preaching the war against the unbelievers and admonishing every man to this work well-pleasing to Allah. Every man who could not bear arms was obliged to pay his monetary contribution regularly into the war-chest; the collectors of the *ewang sabil* (war-tax), bearing his *tjap* (seal), never returned empty-handed. He lived strictly by the prescriptions of Islam and, Zentgraff notes, exercised more authority than the Sultan himself. He sent letters to [[Uleebalang|oeléëbalangs]] he could not reach in person, urging them to walk the way of God; these letters characteristically opened in Arabic with the formula of praise to Allah who had kept His promise, given victory to His servant, strengthened His army, and alone put the hosts to flight.

## The September 1885 ultimatum

One of these letters, of [[September]] [[1885]], runs in official translation: *"To whom belongs now the Kingdom? To the One, the Almighty! Praise be to Allah alone!"* — a veritable ultimatum addressed by the *"poor man"* (the conventional Arabic humility-formula for the first person) *Hadji SJECH SAMAN TIROU*, servant of those who fight on the way (*sabil*) of Allah in the land of Atjeh, *dar as salam wal aman* (abode of peace and safety). He demanded of the imams, the tengkoe's, the *ketjiq's*, the panglima's, and all Muslims — first of all the honoured *Teukoe Neg Marasa*, *Teukoe Panglima Mesdjid Raja*, and *Teukoe Quadli* — that they fear Allah and His commandments, prepare for war with the unbelievers, with their bodies, their property, and their subjects, or, if they could not equip themselves there, perform the *hidjra* (emigration) and join their brothers in Islam to wage war on the way of Allah. Those who obeyed might hope for manifold reward; those who refused should fear manifold punishment in this world and in the lowest rank of the Hell-fire. Let not the power of the unbelievers deceive them: nobody has power, nobody has wealth, nobody has good armies but Allah alone.

In [[1888]] he summoned the Dutch Resident to embrace Islam if he wished to avoid a shameful expulsion from Atjeh. The Minister of Colonies, Keuchenius, answered with a cabinet letter of [[15 August]] [[1888]], letter X no. 52, that the unlawfulness of the demand that the Dutch should convert to Islam was itself proven to Tengkoe di Tiro by the 257th verse of the 2nd Sura of the Koran: *"Let there be no compulsion in religion…"*

## Death and succession

When Tgk. Sjech Saman felt his end approaching in [[January]] [[1891]] he summoned his eldest son [[Tengkoe Mat Amin di Tiro|Mat Amin]] to his deathbed and spoke to him words of grave and urgent admonition: this son was of loose habits, given to women in a way that shamed an oelama, proud and obstinate, and made free with the funds collected for the holy war — yet none of this prevented the father from naming him as his successor, and the pretendent-sultan confirmed him in that position in [[1892]].

## Significance

Tgk. Sjech Saman did not live to see the downfall of his house; the reckoning was settled with his sons, grandsons, and other relatives. But the reputation he left — the *roem van den godsvruchtigen Tgk. Sjech Saman di Tiro* — protected his successors for two decades. Even the most money-grubbing Acehnese dared not betray the schuilplaats of these holy men; the best Dutch patrol-commandants, however zealous their nosing and spying, got nothing out of the population. Only after [[1908]], when governor [[H.N.A. Swart|Swart]] unleashed the destructive apparatus under lieutenant [[Schmidt]], did the bad days come for the Tiro-oelama's.

His grave, beside that of his son Mat Amin who fell at [[Aneukgalong]] in [[1896]], lies at kampong Moereuë near [[Indrapoeri]], under a small white-draped *kandang* that is still honoured.
