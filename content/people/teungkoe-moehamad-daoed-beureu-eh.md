**Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh** was a forty-year-old [[oelama|oelama]] from the *landschap* [[Keumangan|Keumangan]] in the subdistrict [[Sigli|Sigli]], chairman of the central board of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] — the All-Aceh Union of Ulemas founded in [[1939]] — and the principal religious-political figure of pre-war Atjèh, whose influence "extended far beyond the borders of his *landschap*" and whose organisation provided, for the first time in the history of Atjèh, a form in which "both the oelama's and the more militant youth would find a place." Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats him as the central figure of the religious movement that would, in early [[1942]], ally itself with the Japanese and would, after the war, become the principal vehicle of the [[Atjèh|Atjèhnese]] struggle against the Dutch and against the Indonesian Republic alike.

## Personal profile

Piekaar describes Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed as "of medium height, slender build," and notes that his relationship with the old self-governing ruler of Keumangan, [[Teukoe Oemar|Teukoe Oemar]] (whose removal in [[1938]] had been one of the scandals of pre-war Atjèh administration), "left something to be desired." He held the chairmanship of the religious school association Djamiatoeddiniyah in [[Blang Pasèh|Blang Pasèh]]. The combination of his religious authority, his organisational talent, and his ability to bridge the gap between the traditional *oelama*'s and the more militant religious youth gave him a unique position in Atjèhnese society.

## The founding of Poesa

The [[Poesa|Poesa]] — *P(ersatoean) Oe(lama-oelama) S(eloeroeh) A(tjèh)*, the All-Aceh Union of Ulemas — was founded in [[1939]] in [[Bireuën|Bireuën]] under the high patronage of [[Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah|Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah]], self-governing authority of [[Peusangan|Peusangan]]. The founding was, Piekaar records, "intended as a counterweight to the modern currents of the Moehammadijah movement, which were foreign to the fanatically pious Atjèhse religious life." With the Poesa, Piekaar writes, "for the first time in the history of Aceh, the religious life and striving of the Acehnese found its own form," and the movement "managed to acquire a following of an extent unknown to Aceh throughout the entire residency." Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh, as chairman of the central board, was the principal architect of this achievement.

## The modern religious school

The religious school whose main teachers included, besides Teungkoe Isma'el Jacoeb and Teungkoe Hadji Aboe Bakar, also Teungkoe Moehamad Noer — son-in-law of Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh — was the principal institutional vehicle of the movement. Teungkoe Moehamad Noer, who had "also received his training in Egypt and years previously had been noted in the Idi region for anti-authority statements," represented the younger generation that connected the oelama's authority to the political militancy of the religious youth.

## The 1942 alliance with Japan

When the Japanese invasion came in March [[1942]], the Poesa — and through it Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh — emerged as the principal Atjèhnese interlocutor of the Japanese. The religious movement's anti-Dutch orientation, the network of religious schools it had built, and its capacity to mobilise the Atjèhnese population made it the natural ally of the Japanese [[F-organisatie|F-organisatie]] and its successor, the [[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi-kikan]]. The F-propagandists [[Said Aboe Bakar|Said Aboe Bakar]] and [[Said Doellah|Said Doellah]] who entered Atjèh with the Japanese troops at [[Meulabóh|Meulabóh]] were, Piekaar records, "of the same group of persons to whom the letters found on Said Doellah were addressed" — that is, the Poesa network.

## The Ulama–Uleëbalang war and the collapse of the *oelèëbalang* class (1945–1946)

After the Japanese capitulation, the historian Henri J. H. Alers records, the standing of the [[Poesa]] — "well organised" and strengthened by its wartime role — grew rapidly at the expense of the *oelèëbalang* class, and in [[December]] [[1945]] the long-smouldering antagonism between [[Ulama]] and [[Uleebalang|Uleëbalang]] "degenerated into a fierce struggle." The *oelèëbalang*s, Alers writes, "defended themselves only when directly attacked" and, true to a pattern already visible in the [[Aceh War|Atjèh War]], "did not act in concert" — with the result that they "lost the contest." By [[February]] [[1946]] "the matter had already been decided": a large number of *oelèëbalang*s were deposed, imprisoned, or killed, and their possessions and prerogatives were divided among the victorious Ulama, so that, in Alers's phrase, the feudal *adat* class was destroyed and "a priestly class took its place." Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh's own position — though he himself is described by Alers as characteristically reticent, keeping "to the sidelines" ("op de vlakte") during the worst of the fighting — was immeasurably strengthened, since the Poesa's rank and file were drawn overwhelmingly from the militant Ulama class that had carried out the revolution.

Alers compares the episode directly to events in Java: he treats the Atjèhnese Ulama–Uleëbalang war of December 1945–February 1946 as the structural equivalent, "in relative historical terms," of the [[17 oktober-affaire|17 October affair]] of 1952 in Java, with the crucial difference that in Atjèh "the design succeeded" where the Javanese counterpart failed. He also notes that the coup within the Poesa itself — the abortive attempt of [[Sajid Ali|Sajid Ali]] to seize the movement's leadership — should be placed, in the same comparative scheme, on the level of [[Tan Malaka]]'s failed *coup* on Java.

## Military Governor of Atjeh and the struggle for provincial autonomy

Following the death in captivity of [[Teukoe Nja' Arif|Teukoe Nja' Arif]] — the *oelèëbalang* whom the Republic had placed at the head of Atjeh's administration, and whom the Poesa itself arrested at the end of January 1946 as an "enemy of the Republic" — Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh had himself appointed head of the Service of Religious Affairs (*Dienst van Godsdienstzaken*), while a pliant *oelèëbalang*, [[Teukoe Tjhi' Moehamad Daoed Sjah|Teukoe Tjhi' Moehamad Daoed Sjah]], was installed as nominal successor to Nja' Arif. Alers describes Daoed Beureu-éh as the real power "behind the scenes." When an underground movement of dissatisfied Ulama, led by Sajid Ali, tried and failed to wrest control of the Poesa away from him, the episode had far-reaching consequences: Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh had himself named **military governor of Atjeh**, and — in Alers's words — "this Ulama-dictatorship would remain durable for years."

Atjeh, Alers observes, was under his governorship "in fact if not in law" wholly self-governing: the central republican regime, first at [[Bukittinggi|Bukittinggi]] and then at [[Djokja|Djokja]], had neither the means nor, for the sake of a united anti-Dutch front, the desire to interfere. When [[Mr. Sjafrudin Prawiranegara|Mr. Sjafrudin Prawiranegara]], head of the Republic's Emergency Government, visited Atjeh in [[August]] [[1949]], Daoed Beureu-éh — by then republican military governor — pressed demands amounting to near-total autonomy, citing Atjeh's distinct political, economic, cultural, ethnological and religious character (as against the Christian Bataks and Tapanoelese). Prawiranegara conceded on every point, and Atjeh became, in August 1949, a *de jure* autonomous province of the Republic — a status Alers stresses it had, in practice, already held for years. The Central Government at Djokja, however, promptly cabled Prawiranegara that his decision had exceeded his mandate, provoking consternation in Poesa circles before the government partially retreated, allowing the province to continue "*de facto*."

The reprieve was short-lived. In early [[1951]] the Djakarta government created the merged province of [[North Sumatra|North Sumatra]] — combining Atjeh, [[Tapanoeli|Tapanoeli]] and the former federal state of [[East Sumatra|East Sumatra]] — reducing Atjeh to a mere residency and addressing official correspondence to a "resident-coordinator" even before Daoed Beureu-éh's dismissal as governor had been finalised. Daoed Beureu-éh, Alers records, simply returned such letters unopened. He was subsequently ordered transferred to Djakarta for a ministry post and, offered his resignation from government service instead, had it granted "with suspicious speed." His nominee successor as resident-coordinator, the Javanese [[Danubroto|Danubroto]], tried to install loyal, largely *oelèëbalang*-class and non-Atjèhnese officials in posts held by Poesa members, provoking the *oelèëbalang* class to found their own pressure group, the [[Badan Keinsjafan Rakjat|Badan Keinsjafan Rakjat]] (B.K.R.), under [[Teuku Mohammed Ali Samalanga|Teuku Mohammed Ali Samalanga]].

## The *kafir* campaign and accession to the Negara Islam Indonesia (1953)

Alers records that Daoed Beureuh had, as early as 1951, begun denouncing Javanese central-government officials in Atjeh as "*kafirs*" (unbelievers) — a charge Alers reads as more than mere invective, reflecting a genuine perception that the Republic's own "social revolution" of [[17 August]] [[1945]] and the Islamic revolution he had led in Atjeh shared the same social root, even as Djakarta refused to see it that way. Alers argues that Daoed Beureuh restrained himself so long as the national government remained in [[Masjumi]] hands — under Prime Ministers [[Dr. Sukiman Wirjosandjojo|Sukiman Wirjosandjojo]] (from [[26 April]] [[1951]]) and [[Mr. Wilopo|Wilopo]] (from [[23 March]] [[1952]]) — since he could reasonably expect an Islamic government, however imperfectly, to honour Islamic obligations. The fall of Wilopo's cabinet after the [[17 oktober-affaire|17 October 1952]] crisis and the formation, on [[1 August]] [[1953]], of a cabinet under [[Mr. Ali Sastroamidjojo|Ali Sastroamidjojo]] — from which Masjumi was excluded and in which figures such as [[Mr. Moh. Jamin|Moh. Jamin]] held office — removed, in Alers's account, Daoed Beureuh's last reason for restraint.

By early September 1953 the Atjèh police coordinator, [[Njakumar|Njakumar]], had already begun placing guards on public buildings in response to intelligence reports, and on [[21 September]] [[1953]] Atjèh's accession to the [[Negara Islam Indonesia|Negara Islam Indonesia]] (N.I.I.) was proclaimed. The Imam [[Kartosuwirjo|Kartosuwirjo]] appointed Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureuh his **governor** for Atjeh, and the *prang sabil* — holy war — against the "*kafir* government" began at once. Within six days, by [[27 September]] [[1953]], nearly the whole of Atjeh was in rebel hands, with government forces clinging on only around [[Kutaradja|Kota-Radja]] in the north and [[Langsa|Langsa]] in the south. [[Groot Atjeh|Groot Atjeh]] joined the N.I.I. as a body under the acting assistant-coordinator [[Teuku Sulaiman Daud|Teuku Sulaiman Daud]]; only the garrison at [[Kutatjané|Kuta Tjané]] held out in Central Atjeh, and on the West Coast only [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]] and [[Tapatoean|Tapatoean]] remained in government hands.

The reconquest, entrusted to Colonel [[Simbolon|Simbolon]] of the Bukit Barisan territorial command, proceeded through October and November 1953 — [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] and [[Bajeunen|Bajeunen]] on [[3 October]], [[Meureudu|Meureudu]] on [[10 October]], [[Blangkedjeren|Blangkedjeren]] and [[Bireuën|Biruën]] on [[20 October]], and finally [[Takengon|Takengon]] on [[24 November]] [[1953]] — until, by the end of November, the rebellion had been reduced to the scale of "roving bands in the interior," although the underlying insurgency, like its counterparts in West Java and South Celebes, would drag on unresolved. Alers notes the irony that the officer chosen to crush the rebellion, Simbolon, had himself declared for "green" — the Islamic side — in the 17 October 1952 controversy. He also observes that Sukarno himself, on [[14 October]] [[1953]] at [[Bone|Bone]] in South Celebes, alleged that Kartosuwirjo had appealed to the United States for aid — an appeal Alers judges could only sensibly have been made on behalf of the strategically significant Atjeh, not the militarily marginal South Celebes.

## Alers's "Red or Green Merdeka" comparison

Alers's *Om een rode of groene Merdeka* treats Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureuh as the structural counterpart, within Atjeh, of [[Soekarno|Sukarno]] on Java: both men, he argues, "called forth — over the heads of the Japanese authorities — a fanatical psychosis directed against the established feudal state structure," Sukarno's on the scale of the whole of Java and inevitably "red," Daoed Beureuh's confined to Atjeh and, because the Japanese openly courted Islam, comparatively unimpeded and inevitably "green." Alers sets the chronologies of Java and Atjeh side by side as mirrored sequences — the end of the [[Java War|Java War]] (1830) against the end of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh War]] (1903); the founding of [[Moehammadijah|Muhammadiyah]] (1908) against early moderate political awakening (1925); the schism that produced the P.K.I. and S.I. (1921) against the founding of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] (1939); and the social revolution under "red directives" from [[17 August]] [[1945]] against the "green" social revolution beginning [[December]] [[1945]] — concluding that, phase for phase, "the Atjèhnese development was, chronologically speaking, faster."
