**E. Sell** was the author of *The Faith of Islam* (London, [[1880]]), a work cited by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in *The Achehnese* for its account of the Ashura ceremonies observed in British India. The citation places Sell's volume in the small comparative literature on the popular religious customs of Mohammedan peoples that Snouck Hurgronje drew upon when situating Achehnese practice within the wider context of the Eastern Archipelago and the Deccan.

## Citation in *The Achehnese*

Sell is cited in the discussion of the cooking of special viands on [[Ashura]] — the tenth day of Muharram, observed by Shi'ites as the day of the martyrdom of [[Husain]] at [[Kerbela]]. The custom of cooking such foods is, in Snouck Hurgronje's words, "really no more than an insignificant adjunct of the Hasan-Husain festivals, but ... exists elsewhere as an independent usage". In Hindustan, the chosen dish seems to be that known as *khichri*; in [[Cairo]] it is called *hubub*, i.e. "seeds" or "grains" — a usage recorded by [[Edward William Lane|Lane]] (*Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians*, 5th edn., II, p. 149). The custom in Hindustan is referred by Snouck Hurgronje to the *Qanoon-e-islam* of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]] (p. 144), "also see the *Faith of Islam* by E. Sell, London 1880, p. 242".

## Place in the comparative study of Mohammedan customs

Sell's *Faith of Islam* thus stands alongside [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]]'s *Qanoon-e-islam* and [[Edward William Lane|Lane]]'s *Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians* as one of the standard European or Anglo-Indian authorities by means of which Snouck Hurgronje was able to trace the wide diffusion of popular Mohammedan usages — from the Deccan to Egypt, from the Straits Settlements to Acheh — and to demonstrate the formative influence that the Deccan, with its strong Shi'ite colouring, exercised on the Islam of the Eastern Archipelago. The inclusion of Sell's volume in this comparative apparatus indicates its value as a contemporary account of Indian Mohammedan belief and practice in the late nineteenth century.
