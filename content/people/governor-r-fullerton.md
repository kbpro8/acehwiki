**Governor R. Fullerton** was a British colonial official who, in [[1825]], set down in writing one of the most candid early European assessments of the impracticability of establishing influence over [[Acheh]] without large-scale military force. His memorandum, quoted by Snouck Hurgronje in the Introduction to *The Achehnese*, forms part of the documentary record by which the British East India Company came to recognise that [[Acheh]] could not be treated as an ordinary dependency.

In his communication Fullerton observed that, while the future establishment of European influence over Acheen had "long been considered a desirable object," experience had shown such an arrangement to be "utterly impracticable without employing a large military force to overawe the inhabitants." The judgment echoed and amplified the warning given nearly four decades earlier by [[Captain Francis Light]], who in [[1786]] had pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal that the Achehnese were "rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous," and that a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary before any settlement of safety and advantage could be formed.

Fullerton's statement belongs to the same body of British experience as the contemporaneous correspondence surrounding the [[Raffles Treaty with Acheh|treaty made with Acheh]] by [[Raffles]] in [[1819]] and the subsequent dealings with [[Johor Allum]] as would-be King of Acheen. The British had come to realise that the country had no central authority capable of guaranteeing the security of foreign trade, and that European nations attempting to settle there had always been driven out by the "oppression, war and confusion" prevailing on the Achehnese coast.

The conclusion embodied in Fullerton's memorandum was shortly afterwards overtaken by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]], under which Great Britain withdrew from direct interference in Achehnese affairs and left the question of trade security in Dutch hands. The Dutch, however, were slow to profit from the British lesson: it was only after decades of fruitless treaties and the disasters of [[1873]] that they too came round, under [[General van der Heyden]] and later [[Governor Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], to the conviction which Fullerton had expressed in [[1825]].

Snouck Hurgronje quotes Fullerton's words as evidence that the difficulties encountered by the Dutch in the [[Acheh War|Achehnese War]] were not the product of Dutch incompetence alone, but were inherent in the political and social condition of Acheh itself.
