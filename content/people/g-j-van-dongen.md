## Overview

G. J. van Dongen was a Dutch colonial administrator who served as *Controleur van de onderafdeeling XXV Moekims en de Zuidelijke Nederzettingen* (Controller of the XXV Moekims and the Southern Settlements sub-district) in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] during the early twentieth century. He was present at the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) held at [[Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]], and unlike several of his fellow *controleurs*, he contributed a substantive question to the discussion: he asked whether *Inlandsche ambtenaren* (Native officials) could take seats in the proposed *Gewestelijke Raad* (Regional Council) for [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]]. This question touched on one of the most fundamental issues in the entire reform process—the extent to which indigenous officials would be granted a voice in the new regional governance structures being proposed for the enlarged administrative unit that would incorporate Atjeh. Van Dongen's intervention, though brief, revealed a concern for the position of native administrators within the reformed system and an awareness of the tensions between the colonial commitment to indirect rule through indigenous intermediaries and the limited political participation afforded to those intermediaries in formal governance structures.

## The Onderafdeeling XXV Moekims en de Zuidelijke Nederzettingen

The *onderafdeeling* (sub-district) of the XXV Moekims en de Zuidelijke Nederzettingen encompassed a cluster of twenty-five *moekim* (village cluster) administrative units together with a group of southern settlements within the territory of [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The *moekim* system, as described by Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] during the Koeta-Radja meetings, functioned as an intermediate tier of indigenous administration between the individual *gampong* (villages) and the Dutch colonial hierarchy, comparable in certain respects to the Javanese *onderdistricten* (sub-districts) though distinct in its historical origins and social organization. The designation "Zuidelijke Nederzettingen" (Southern Settlements) referred to communities located in the southern reaches of the [[Groot-Atjeh]] district, likely including areas that had been established or resettled as part of the Dutch *concentratie* (concentration) policy, which aimed to bring the scattered Acehnese population into more easily supervised settlements. The combination of a numbered moekim cluster with the southern settlements in a single *onderafdeeling* reflected the pragmatic approach of the colonial administration to grouping territories for administrative purposes, balancing the logic of the indigenous moekim structure with the strategic requirements of colonial governance and control.

## The Question of Inlandsche Ambtenaren in the Gewestelijke Raad

Van Dongen's question about the participation of *Inlandsche ambtenaren* (Native officials) in the proposed *Gewestelijke Raad* (Regional Council) for [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]] struck at the heart of a central tension in Dutch colonial governance. The *Gewestelijke Raad* was envisioned as a representative advisory body at the regional level, part of the broader reform program's attempt to introduce a measure of consultation and representation into the administrative structure of the Indies. The question of whether indigenous officials—particularly those from the *Inlandsch Bestuur* (Native Administration) of Atjeh—could sit on such a council raised complex issues of status, competence, and colonial ideology. On one hand, the Dutch system of indirect rule depended heavily on the cooperation and expertise of native officials, from the *hoofden* (chiefs) of the *Inlandsche Zelfbesturen* (Native Self-Governments) to the *keucik* and *moekim* officials who administered the *gampong* and moekim units within [[Groot-Atjeh]]. On the other hand, the colonial administration had historically been reluctant to grant indigenous officials formal positions in European-style deliberative bodies, preferring to keep the channels of consultation informal and personal rather than institutionalized. Van Dongen's question implied that the success of the proposed reorganization might depend in part on the inclusion of native voices in the new governance structures, a perspective that aligned with the reformist impulse in Batavia while potentially conflicting with the more authoritarian traditions of the Atjeh Gouvernement.

## The Bestuursreorganisatie and the Future of Atjeh's Governance

The *bestuursreorganisatie* (administrative reorganization) discussed at the Koeta-Radja meetings in January 1914 represented the most significant attempt to restructure the governance of the Netherlands Indies in a generation, and its implications for Atjeh were particularly far-reaching. The central proposal was to abolish the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] as a separate administrative entity and incorporate its territories into a new *gewest* (region) of [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]], which would be governed by a *Gouverneur* rather than a *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur*, with a *Gewestelijke Raad* providing advisory input on regional affairs. Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] opposed this plan, arguing that Atjeh's unique conditions—including the ongoing need for military oversight, the distinctive *moekim*-based administrative structure, and the complex relationship between the *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* (directly ruled territory) and the *Inlandsche Zelfbesturen* (Native Self-Governments)—required the continuation of the existing system. Van Dongen's question about native representation on the *Gewestelijke Raad* can be understood as engaging with this debate from the perspective of a field administrator who worked closely with indigenous officials on a daily basis and who understood that any viable governance structure for the region would need to account for their role and their interests.
