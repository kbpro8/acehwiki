**General van Teijn** — *Kolonel* (later *Generaal*) *van Teijn* — was a Dutch officer of the Royal Netherlands East Indies Army ([[K.N.I.L.|K.N.I.L.]]) who served as *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* (Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh and Dependencies) from [[December 1886|December]] [[1886]] to [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]], and whose governorship — lasting nearly four and a half years — was devoted to the persistent attempt to make contact with the [[Sultan of Aceh|Sultan]] and the [[Kemala-party|Kemala-party]] and to obtain their submission, while maintaining the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] and the [[Blokkade (Atjeh, 1886-1892)|coastal blockade]] as instruments of pressure. His departure in [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]], owing to increasing ill-health, marked the failure of the policy of negotiated submission; the death of the principal resistance leaders [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] and [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] having already deprived the negotiations of their principal addressees.

## Inheritance (December 1886)

Van Teijn took over the administration in Atjeh "onder alles behalve rooskleurige omstandigheden" (under anything but rosy circumstances). He was instructed by the government to continue consistently the prescribed system of waiting within the line, but at the same time to attempt to make contact with the Sultan and the Kemala-party, while avoiding the appearance that the Dutch sought it.

## The Encirclement by Tengkoe di Tirou (1887–1890)

The Dutch *stelling* was regularly surrounded by a ring of fortifications thrown up by [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]], whose power and influence ever increased. From these the line itself was bombarded with artillery, the means of communication destroyed, raids made within the line, and harm done where possible. The enemy even entrenched himself there within repeatedly, and had to be driven out with force of arms in [[April 1887|April]] and [[October 1887|October]] [[1887]] and in [[July 1888|July]] [[1888]], and a part of the railway had to be moved further inward. Stepping ever more boldly and seriously threatening the communication between several posts, Van Teijn was finally compelled to act outward. In [[July 1889|July]] [[1889]] the Dutch suffered a painful reverse at [[Kota Toewankoe|Kota Toewankoe]]; though this fortification was taken shortly afterwards, it was at the cost of 21 dead and 92 wounded, and was then abandoned again. In the beginning of [[1890]] they took it again by surprise.

The clearing of the Dutch terrain within the line of enemies demanded all available strength, and only after the establishment of a *[[Corps Maréchaussée (Atjeh)|corps maréchaussée]]* in [[1890]] — chosen from the most suitable volunteers of the army, charged exclusively with the security service within the line, and itself highly meritorious — was safety there gradually obtained.

## The Coastal Blockade (1887–1892)

On the coasts the situation was the same. They continued to import everything to the enemy in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], to which the extraordinarily high pepper prices enabled them better than ever. Under the leadership of the *[[perala-party|perala-party]] — which was in contact with all the bandit chiefs — [[Segli|Segli]] was continually bombarded and attacked, and on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] a state of unrest arose through attacks of the Muslemin under known bandit chiefs on [[Edi|Edi]], so that the garrison had repeatedly to be reinforced, the traders fled to [[Penang|Penang]], and even the *radja* made preparations to do the same; in [[1889]] and [[1890]] important expeditions thither had to take place. On the [[Westkust|West Coast]] [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] virtually controlled the situation, and even succeeded in getting Atjehese bands to cause unrest in [[Troemon|Troemon]] on Dutch territory, which required the intervention of troops from [[Padang|Padang]]. The bandit chiefs always claimed to act "op last en in naam des Sultans" (on the orders and in the name of the Sultan).

In consequence, Van Teijn — seeing that with the policy of waiting alone the Dutch were getting no further — already at the end of [[1887]] proposed to the government the introduction of a [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]], with the re-establishment of the post at [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]] and the temporary closure (blockade) of part of the [[Westkust|West Coast]] where [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] had his pepper lying. The first the government rejected; the latter it approved. This leading to good results, and [[Pedir|Pedir]] &c. opposing with all its might the pending submission of the Kemala-party, at the end of [[1888]] part of the [[Noordkust|North Coast]] around [[Pedir|Pedir]] was closed to all import and export, and this was in [[September 1889|September]] [[1889]] and [[August 1890|August]] [[1890]] further extended over the whole North Coast and over the West Coast as far as [[Tenom|Tenom]], and maintained until the end of [[1892]]. "Dit stelsel had een geduchte uitwerking en bleek een geweldig dwangmiddel" (This system had a formidable effect and proved a powerful instrument of compulsion), Kruijt writes. The [[Koninklijke Marine|Marine]] had a heavy task, but received little cooperation from the administrative officials.

## The Pursuit of the Kemala-party (1887–1891)

Van Teijn had to return to the beginning of his governorship and see what, while all the foregoing was taking place, had come of the search for contacts with the Kemala-party. Naturally, the shrewd Atjehers had very quickly realised that the Dutch sought them, and began to speculate on this at once. [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] and [[Toeankoe Hasschim|Toeankoe Hasschim]] &c., who were little pleased with the ever-increasing influence of [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] and therewith of the religious party, made themselves heard. Also various chiefs outside the line, who had suffered from Tengkoe di Tirou's usurpations, came into apparent good understanding with the Dutch, against payment, licences, or to obtain other advantages. Even [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] and [[Toekoe Nja Makam|Toekoe Nja Makam]] sought to be taken into favour by the administration and had meetings within the line (in secret, of course) with Dutch emissaries. [[Toeankoe Hasschim|Toeankoe Hasschim]] requested a meeting at [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]], and already in [[June 1887|June]] [[1887]] the aforementioned pretender Sultan [[Toeankoe Abdul Medjid|Toeankoe Abdul Medjid]] submitted, and — besides a gift of ƒ25,000 — enjoyed a monthly allowance of ƒ400.

Meanwhile the Atjehers, with their known acumen, continually put off some decision. Dutch administrative officials on the [[Noordkust|North Coast]] and in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] intrigued, each in their own way, in the direction of conciliation, sent emissaries and gifts to [[Kemala|Kemala]], and worked against each other. When in [[August 1888|August]] [[1888]] the Sultan suddenly appeared in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and met with [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]], [[Imam Longbattah|Imam Longbattah]], and [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]], Van Teijn cherished a justified hope that it would now come to a conclusion; but after three weeks the Sultan departed again for Kemala, the chiefs, including those from within the line, brought him gifts, but the Dutch heard nothing from him. On that occasion the Sultan appointed [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] as *panglima* (Emir El Bahr) of the West Coast as far as [[Singkel|Singkel]].

Given the existing tension between [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] and [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]], the latter — fearful of eventually being caught behind the net, or seeking to be ahead of the others — in [[1889]] offered to bring the Sultan to the Dutch. Convinced of being on the right path (for the Dutch had long been sending friendly chiefs and emissaries, supposedly at the Sultan's invitation — for so the *adat* ran — with gifts to Kemala, to take soundings), they did not accept T. di Tirou's offer, in which he had of course stipulated certain advantages for himself. More missions to Kemala, very solemn and imposing, followed; presumably the members of the missions received, or rather bought with Dutch money, Sultan's appointments — but for the Dutch all led to nothing.

## The Deaths of January 1891 and the Departure of April 1891

The strong position taken by T. di Tirou opposite the Dutch line, the difficulty the Dutch had in keeping him at bay, together with their sweet, conciliatory attitude toward Kemala and other chiefs, heightened among the Atjehers the hope that it would ultimately succeed to drive the Dutch into the sea. When, moreover, the health of Van Teijn — whose early replacement was foreseen, promising a change of administration and direction — gave the Atjehers reason to adopt, like the Dutch, a policy of waiting and to postpone conciliation for the time being, that was an additional reason. In [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] and [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] died suddenly, and this too was an occasion for the Kemala-party in any case to await first the consequences of the impending change of administration. In [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]] Van Teijn, thus disappointed in his expectations, after years of heavy laborious work, departed owing to increasing indisposition.

## Significance

Van Teijn's governorship is the period of the most sustained Dutch attempt at a negotiated end to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] through conciliation of the Sultan and the Kemala-party, combined with the [[Blokkade (Atjeh, 1886-1892)|coastal blockade]] as a pressure instrument. His diagnosis of the situation — that the policy of waiting alone was insufficient, and that a [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] and selective blockade were necessary — was vindicated by the success of the blockade he enforced on the [[Noordkust|North]] and [[Westkust|West Coasts]]; his failure to obtain the Sultan's submission was the failure of the policy of conciliation itself, which [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]] identifies as structurally incapable of success against an enemy "die aan groote geslepenheid en volharding, grooten nationalen trots en onverdelgbaren haat tegen den indringer paart" (who combines great cunning and perseverance, great national pride and indestructible hatred of the invader). The death of [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] removed the principal religious leader of the resistance; he would be succeeded as head of the resistance by his son [[Tengkoe Mohamed Amin|Tengkoe Mohamed Amin]].
