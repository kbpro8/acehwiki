**Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun** is the Achehnese saint to whom popular tradition attributes the miraculous origin of the pepper-plant in Acheh. His tomb lies in the gampong of the same name in the [[XXII Mukims of Panglima Poleem|IX Mukims]], and his cult is closely connected with the annual *kanduri bungong lada*, the "pepper-blossom feast" observed on the East and West Coasts whenever the pepper comes into flower.

## The Legend of the Kapok Seed

The Achehnese account for the origin of pepper by a legend similar to that current among the Arabs in regard to coffee. Just as, in the Arabic story, goats' dung sown by a saint grew up into the first trees which bore the delicious product of Mokha, so in Acheh the first pepper-plants are said to have grown from the seeds of the kapok tree (Achehnese *paitjoc*) planted by Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun. From this miraculous origin it is supposed to follow that pepper-plants are propagated by cuttings rather than by sowing — a piece of agricultural practice thus invested with a religious sanction.

## The Kanduri Bungong Lada

In honour of the saint, the *kanduri bungong lada* is annually celebrated on the East and West Coasts when the pepper blossoms. Unlike many Achehnese feasts, this is not the occasion of a public gathering: each pepper-planter holds the feast separately in his own house. As the pepper-plants do not all blossom at the same time, the period during which these *kanduris* are given lasts as long as three months. The constituents of the feast are glutinous rice and its accessories; in a single house as much as a *naleh* of this rice may be prepared. The feast, in short, is a domestic rite of thanksgiving embedded in the agricultural calendar and addressed to the saint from whom the crop is believed to derive.

## Relation to the "Pepper Saint" Cult

A closely related tradition attaches to [[Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun]], "the pepper saint," whose tomb likewise stands in a gampong of the same name in the IX Mukims and who was actively promoted by the ulama [[Teungku Kutakarang]] during the [[Acheh War]]. Whether the two names conceal a single figure or represent parallel hagiologies clustering around the same district, they testify to the importance of the IX Mukims as a centre of pepper-cult legend and to the deep roots of the crop in the religious imagination of the Achehnese.
