**Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun** is the Achehnese saint to whom popular tradition attributes the miraculous origin of the pepper-plant in [[Aceh|Acheh]]. His tomb lies in the *gampong* (village) of the same name in the [[XXII Mukims of Panglima Poleem|IX Mukims]], and his cult is closely connected with the annual *kanduri bungong lada* (pepper-blossom feast) observed on the East and West Coasts whenever the pepper comes into flower. A closely related, and possibly identical, figure appears in the sources under the name [[Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun|Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun]] — popularly called "the pepper saint" — whose tomb likewise stands in a *gampong* of the same name in the IX Mukims and whose cult was actively promoted by the *ulama* [[Teungku Kutakarang|Teungku Kutakarang]] during the [[Aceh War|Acheh War]]. Whether the two names conceal a single figure or represent parallel hagiologies clustering around the same district, they testify to the importance of the IX Mukims as a centre of pepper-cult legend and to the deep roots of the crop in the religious imagination of the Achehnese.

## The Legend of the Kapok Seed

The Achehnese account for the origin of pepper by a legend similar to that current among the Arabs in regard to coffee. Just as, in the Arabic story, goats' dung sown by a saint grew up into the first trees which bore the delicious product of [[Mokha|Mokha]], so in Acheh the first pepper-plants are said to have grown from the seeds of the kapok tree (Achehnese *paitjoc*) planted by Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun. From this miraculous origin it is supposed to follow that pepper-plants are propagated by cuttings rather than by sowing — a piece of agricultural practice thus invested with a religious sanction.

The legend of the pepper-plant's origin is treated in connection with both saints in the Achehnese tradition; the kapok-seed narrative is attached to Teungku Lam Peuneu'eun, while the cult title "the pepper saint" belongs more particularly to Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun. The two traditions evidently point to a single body of folklore attached to the IX Mukims, in which the miraculous introduction of so profitable a crop was attributed to one or more local holy men. Such eponymous saints, named from the *gampong* in which their tombs stand, are a common feature of the Achehnese religious landscape.

## Association with Teungku Kutakarang

The cult of the pepper saint — under the name Teungku Lam Keuneu'eun — received a notable stimulus from [[Teungku Kutakarang|Teungku Kutakarang]], the active and eccentric rival of the great *ulama* [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] during the early years of the [[Aceh War|Acheh War]]. Teungku Kutakarang, whose own residence lay in the neighbourhood of the saint's tomb, delighted to call himself the servant (*glangwang*) of that shrine. By attaching himself in this way to a long-established *kramat* (sacred tomb), he at once legitimised his own presence in the district and placed himself in a position to receive the numerous gifts which pious visitors dedicated to the saint. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] explicitly notes that this was one of the pains to which Kutakarang went in order to enhance the repute of the "pepper saint."

## The Kanduri Bungong Lada

In honour of the saint, the *kanduri bungong lada* (pepper-blossom feast) is annually celebrated on the East and West Coasts when the pepper blossoms. Unlike many Achehnese feasts, this is not the occasion of a public gathering: each pepper-planter holds the feast separately in his own house. As the pepper-plants do not all blossom at the same time, the period during which these *kanduris* (ritual meals) are given lasts as long as three months. The constituents of the feast are glutinous rice and its accessories; in a single house as much as a *naleh* of this rice may be prepared. The feast, in short, is a domestic rite of thanksgiving embedded in the agricultural calendar and addressed to the saint from whom the crop is believed to derive.

## Vows and Intercession

Vows are also made to the pepper saint for recovery from sickness, or for the successful completion of a child's Quranic studies. Such vows are part of the wider Achehnese pattern of *kramat*-veneration, in which local holy men — whose historic personality is no longer recoverable — function as intercessors for the practical concerns of peasant life: health, harvest, and the welfare of children. The offerings made at the saint's grave form one of the more striking examples of the way in which popular hagiology in Acheh clusters around local worthies.

## Veneration

The *kanduri bungong lada* is the most tangible surviving memorial of this cycle of legend. Although the feast is conducted privately in each planter's house rather than as a public gathering at the tomb, the underlying belief — that the crop exists by virtue of a saint's *barakah* (blessing) — keeps the memory of the pepper saint alive in the religious consciousness of the pepper-growing districts. The saint's cult is one of the principal surviving intersections of Achehnese agriculture and popular Islam.
