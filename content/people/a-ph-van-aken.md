**A. Ph. van Aken** was a Dutch colonial controller and administrative official who served as the **Controller of [[Korintji]]** (in the residency of [[Djambi]]) and participated in the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]]. He is particularly notable for his detailed knowledge of local administrative structures in the highland region of [[Korintji]] and for contributing valuable ethnographic and administrative detail to discussions about how indigenous administrative units should be recognized and classified in the context of Dutch colonial governance.

## Administrative Position

Van Aken held the position of Controller of [[Korintji]], a highland administrative region within the larger residency of [[Djambi]] in Sumatra. The controller's role combined inspection, administrative oversight, and advisory functions, requiring detailed knowledge of local conditions, indigenous leadership structures, and community organization.

## Participation in the Reform Meetings

Van Aken participated in the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] at [[Medan]] ([[13 January]]–[[15 January]] [[1914]]) and was present during discussions about how indigenous administrative units in various regions of Sumatra could be adapted to the proposed colonial reorganization scheme.

## Contributions to the Discussions



### The Structure of Korintji Administration

Van Aken provided detailed information about the complex administrative organization of [[Korintji]], correcting misunderstandings held by Government Commissioner **S. de Graaf** and others about the region's structure. He explained that:

1. **Doesoen federations**: [[Korintji]] was organized into "doesoenfederaties" (village federations), each of which possessed its own treasury (*kas*)

2. **Historical collective representation**: The entire region of [[Korintji]] had formerly represented itself externally through a college composed of the highest adat (customary law) heads—a collective body rather than a single chief or governor

3. **The problematic "district" designation**: The Dutch colonial administration had mistakenly designated what Van Aken called these "doesoen communities" (*doesoengemeenschappen*) as "districts" (*distrikten*), a designation that obscured their actual character

4. **The "primus inter pares" structure**: The district head (*districtshoofd*) did not stand hierarchically above the other heads but rather functioned as a "primus inter pares"—first among equals—in a collegial arrangement

5. **Autonomy of constituent units**: The doesoen units that comprised these communities viewed themselves as completely self-governing, though they recognized some larger collective identity with neighboring doesoen in the same region

### Reclassification of Indigenous Units

Van Aken's detailed account prompted **S. de Graaf** to make an important conceptual point: Van Aken's description indicated that the doesoen in [[Korintji]] were comparable to the desa (village administrative unit) on Java, and that what the Dutch had called a "district" in [[Korintji]] actually represented an administrative artifice rather than a true indigenous governing unit with historical roots.

Nevertheless, De Graaf acknowledged, the district structure in [[Korintji]]—despite being an administrative construct—did have a historical foundation in the form of the previous collective representation through federations of highest adat heads. This distinction between purely administrative constructs with no indigenous basis and administrative arrangements that built upon historical indigenous patterns became important to the broader discussion about whether existing units in various regions could serve as vehicles for autonomous or self-governing status under the new colonial reorganization.

## Regional Expertise

Van Aken's intervention illustrated the importance of field administrators' detailed knowledge in shaping reform implementation. His ability to explain the actual local structures and hierarchies of [[Korintji]] provided crucial clarification for colonial administrators attempting to determine how the decentralization and self-governance principles should be applied in different regions. His work demonstrated that administrative categories and structures could not be uniformly imposed but needed to account for the actual institutional and social realities on the ground.

## Account from *Een strijd in Atjeh* ([[1934]])

Van Aken appears in the pamphlet *[[Een strijd in Atjeh (1934)]]* as the [[Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Governor of Atjeh and Dependencies]] who, in August [[1933]], directed the ophthalmologist [[J. Tijssen|Dr. J. Tijssen]] to the [[Atjeh's Westkust|West Coast of Atjeh]] rather than to the North Coast where he had worked before.

Tijssen related that on [[23 August]] [[1933]] he came again to [[Koeta Radja]] and travelled thence to [[Bakongan]]. "Toen ik in Koeta Radja overleg pleegde met den gouverneur, bleek deze erop gesteld, dat ik ditmaal naar de Westkust zou gaan. Gouverneur van Aken heeft een zwak voor dit gebied, waar hij lange jaren heeft gewerkt; misschien gaven ook politieke motieven den doorslag." — When I took counsel at Koeta Radja with the governor, it appeared that he set store on my going this time to the West Coast. Governor Van Aken has a weakness for this territory, where he worked for long years.

The remark is the pamphlet's only comment on Van Aken's administration, and it records two things: that he had worked long years on the West Coast before his governorship, and that in Tijssen's judgement political considerations may have entered into the choice of that district — a coast whose road was flanked by the mass graves of "de vele Atjehoorlogen en den opstand van '26," — the many Atjeh wars and the rising of '26, and where, Tijssen observed, no officer went into a *[[Kampong|kampong]]* without a soldier behind him carrying a loaded carbine.

The resulting tour is described at [[Tijssen's journey to the West Coast of Atjeh and Simaloer (1933)]]; among its cases were the [[Seven juvenile cataracts of Atjeh's Westkust|seven juvenile cataracts]] between [[Meulaboh]] and [[Singkel]]. At [[Bakongan]], Tijssen recorded, the administration paid the travelling costs of his patients when the distance was too great to be covered on foot.
