**Brutus** was the pseudonymous author of [[Open Letter by Brutus to General Knoop]], the [[1874]] pamphlet that defended [[General Knoop]]'s judgement that the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)]] had been only partly successful. Writing from [[Kampen]], Brutus answered [[General Van Swieten's letter in Het Vaderland]] and turned the campaign in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] into a detailed public argument about success, failure, speed, and military method.

Brutus is important because he preserved one of the earliest detailed critiques of the Dutch capture of the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] and the claim that this capture meant victory.

## The pamphlet of [[1874]]

Brutus wrote his open letter after [[Het Vaderland]] published [[General Van Swieten's letter in Het Vaderland]]. He believed that many readers had taken the publication of Van Swieten's defense as proof that [[General Knoop]]'s criticism had been answered. Brutus therefore wrote directly to Knoop and argued that the war itself proved otherwise.

The pamphlet closed with the date [[18 November]] [[1874]] at [[Kampen]], which gives it a clear place in the public debate of the war's second year.

## What Brutus argued

Brutus accepted that the Dutch had taken the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]]. But he argued that this did not mean the expedition had fully succeeded. In his view, the campaign moved too slowly after success at [[Battle of Tiban (14 December 1873)|Tiban]], lost time at [[Penajoeng]], delayed too much before and after the taking of the [[Third Conquest of the Great Missigit|Great Missigit]], and then failed to turn later operations into decisive political results.

He used the [[Expedition to Bital (12 February 1874)]], the [[Expedition to Ketapang Doewa (15 February 1874)]], and the reverse at [[Battle of Lampoe Oek (16 April 1874)]] as proof that the Dutch had not truly subdued the country.

## Criticism of passive strategy

The center of Brutus's criticism was what later Dutch writers called an [[Afwachtende houding|afwachtende houding]] — a waiting or passive posture. He argued that the command relied too much on the hope that occupying the capital would lead naturally to submission, instead of using victory to strike harder and faster.

## Criticism of misplaced leniency

Brutus also became known for his criticism of what he saw as misplaced humanitarian softness. He did not simply argue for blind destruction, but he did argue that the enemy had not been made to fear continued resistance strongly enough. This became one of the best-known lines in the early Dutch argument over how the war in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] should be fought.

## Significance

**Brutus** is significant because he gave the early [[Aceh War]] one of its sharpest public critics. His pamphlet linked battlefield detail with wider judgement and helped fix the idea that the second expedition had won a major military success without securing a final political result.
