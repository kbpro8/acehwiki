**Brutus** was the pseudonymous author of [[Open Letter by Brutus to General Knoop]], the [[1874]] pamphlet that defended [[General Knoop]]'s judgement on the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)]] and criticized [[Jan van Swieten]]'s command in [[Aceh|Atjeh]]. Writing from [[Kampen]], Brutus turned the unfinished result in Aceh into a public argument about speed, pursuit, fortification, and the difference between taking the capital and ending a war.

## Author of the open letter

The title page named only *Brutus*. The pamphlet itself showed a writer deeply interested in military history and the day-by-day progress of the campaign from [[December]] [[1873]] to [[April]] [[1874]]. He did not write as a neutral observer. He wrote as a critic who believed the Dutch had repeatedly failed to exploit their own successes.

## Defender of Knoop's verdict

Brutus wrote in reply to [[General Van Swieten's letter in Het Vaderland]]. His goal was to defend Knoop's judgement that the expedition had been only "slechts ten halve gelukt". He accepted that the Dutch had taken the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]], but he argued that this was not enough because the war in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] continued.

## Critic of passivity

The heart of Brutus's argument was that the command moved too slowly and stopped too often. He pointed to the delays after [[Battle of Tiban (14 December 1873)]], at [[Penajoeng]], before the move on [[Lemboe]], after the taking of the [[Third Conquest of the Great Missigit|Missigit]], and again after the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)]]. The expeditions to [[Expedition to Bital (12 February 1874)]] and [[Expedition to Ketapang Doewa (15 February 1874)]] and the reverse of [[Battle of Lampoe Oek (16 April 1874)]] became his main examples.

## Language of humanitarian criticism

Brutus also became important for the way he criticized what he saw as misplaced leniency. He did not argue for blind cruelty, but he did argue that the enemy had not been made to fear continued resistance. This line of criticism became part of the early Dutch debate over how the war in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] should be fought.

## Significance

**Brutus** mattered because he preserved one of the earliest detailed critiques of the second expedition. His pamphlet turned campaign history into public argument and helped fix the idea that the expedition had won a visible success without gaining a decisive result.
