The **Sultan of Acheh** in the era of the [[Acheh War|war with the Dutch]] was the last effective holder of the sultanate before the Dutch occupation of the capital. His reign is the closing chapter of the Achehnese sultanate as a political institution: when the war with the Dutch began, he fled from [[Banda Acheh]] to [[Keumala]], after which Banda Acheh became the capital of the territory seized by the Dutch and the base of their operations. The Sultan's withdrawal to Keumala, the inland seat of the royal family on the [[XXII Mukims of Panglima Poleem|XXII Mukims]] side of the river, left the city of Banda Acheh in Dutch hands and inaugurated the long period of guerrilla resistance with which the rest of the [[Acheh War]] is concerned.

## The Sultanate at the Outbreak of War

By the time the war began, the Sultan of Acheh had long ceased to be the effective ruler of the country. Real authority had passed, on the one hand, to the hereditary *uleebalangs* who governed the sagis and mukims, and, on the other, to the great ulamas — above all [[Teungku Tiro]] — and to political adventurers like [[Teuku Uma]] who drew their power from the disorders of the time. The Sultan retained, however, a residual prestige as the source of formal deeds of appointment (*sarakata*) and as the centre around which the *Dalam*, the palace establishment, continued to be organised. His flight to Keumala, although it abandoned the capital to the Dutch, did not extinguish this prestige; it merely transferred the seat of the court to the inland fastness where the royal family had long had a secondary residence.

## The Court at Keumala

After the flight from Banda Acheh, the court at Keumala continued, in Snouck Hurgronje's account, to be "merely a court clique, which continued the traditions of the feeble Dalam that subsisted in the capital in former days." Under the influence of figures such as [[Tuanku Asem]], correspondence was kept up with men like Teungku Tiro and Teuku Uma on money matters and on the affairs of the friends or proteges of the Sultan; but Keumala exercised little influence on the actions of these two individuals, who in their turn rarely held consultations with one another. The young sultan treated Sayyid visitors from Mecca with the highest honour, and was prepared, on one celebrated occasion, to establish a new kind of court of justice — the *bale meunhasah* — in which the Habib [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Abdurrahman Zahir]] should decide all questions relating to religion. The establishment of this court, however, was an admission of weakness as much as a bid for reform: it was a means of bringing the religious authority, which had slipped from the Sultan's hands to those of the ulamas, back within the precincts of the palace.

## The Sultan in the Resistance

The Sultan's personal share in the conduct of the war was small. The actual fighting was carried on by the uleebalangs of the three sagis, by the bands of volunteers that gathered around the ulamas, and by the guerilla leaders who rose and fell as the fortunes of the war shifted. The Sultan remained, throughout, a symbolic rather than a strategic figure — the man from whom legitimacy was held to descend, but whose own power was hardly distinguishable from that of his court clique. When, at last, the Sultan submitted to the Dutch Government, the event marked not the end of the resistance — which continued for years afterwards under the ulamas — but the final dissolution of the sultanate as a political institution.
