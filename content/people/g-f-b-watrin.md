**G. F. B. Watrin** was a Dutch military officer and colonial administrator in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] — one of the celebrated *Marechaussee* commanders of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen]] named in [[Musket]]'s roll-call of the great patrol commanders of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], and, by [[1914]], the *Kapitein der Infanterie* (Captain of the Infantry) and *Civiel Gezaghebber van de onderafdeeling XXII Moekims* (Civil Authority Holder of the XXII Moekims sub-district) in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Government of Atjeh and Dependencies]]. His dual military-civil career — from *Marechaussee* patrol commander in the war years to *Civiel Gezaghebber* in the post-war administration — exemplifies the intertwining of military and civilian authority that was the hallmark of the Atjeh administrative system, and is directly comparable to that of his fellow military-civil administrator [[Th. J. Veltman]] and of [[Hendrikus Colijn]], the *Marechaussee* commander who rose from *civiel gezaghebber* in [[Lho' Seumawe|Lho' Seumawé]] to the Prime Ministership of the Netherlands.

The identification of the *Marechaussee* commander Watrin of Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* with G. F. B. Watrin, the Captain of Infantry and *Civiel Gezaghebber* of the XXII Moekims, is not made explicit in either source, but is overwhelmingly probable: Watrin is a rare Franco-Dutch surname, both figures are Dutch military officers serving in Atjeh in the same decades, and the career pattern — a *Marechaussee* commander of the war years moving into a *civiel gezaghebber* post in the post-war administration — is precisely that documented for [[Hendrikus Colijn|Colijn]] and others of Musket's roll-call.

## The Marechaussee Commander

In Merac's *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* ([[1947]]), Watrin is the seventh-named in [[Musket]]'s list of the *Marechaussee* commanders of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]:

> "Gilles Graafland, Van Daalen, Campioni, Christoffel, Van der Maaten, Scheepens, Watrin, Webb, Colijn, Geertsema Beckering, Darlang and the sergeants Nussy, Salowono, Pongoh … a colourful array."

The names of these famous fighters and *Marechaussee* commanders, the book records, "rang through the Indies like carillon music," and their "stories of brave deeds, adventures, and peculiarities went the rounds, as if they were legends." Watrin is therefore one of the book's *fellows of the old time* — the colonial memory of the Atjeh-oorlog whose names were carillon music in the Netherlands Indies. He is named alongside such celebrated *Marechaussee* commanders as [[Gilles Graafland]], [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]], [[Campioni]], [[Christoffel]], [[Van der Maaten]], [[Scheepens]], [[Webb]], [[Hendrikus Colijn|Colijn]], [[Geertsema Beckering]], and [[Darlang]].

## Captain of Infantry and Civiel Gezaghebber of the XXII Moekims

By [[1914]], Watrin held the dual position of *Kapitein der Infanterie* (Captain of the Infantry) and *Civiel Gezaghebber van de onderafdeeling XXII Moekims* (Civil Authority Holder of the XXII Moekims sub-district) in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Government of Atjeh and Dependencies]]. He was present at the *bestuursvergaderingen* (administrative meetings) at [[Koeta-Radja]] on [[19 January]] [[1914]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]], though no recorded remarks appear under his name in the *notulen* (minutes). His dual military-civil role exemplified the distinctive pattern of governance that had evolved in Atjeh, where military officers were regularly appointed to civil administrative positions — a practice that reflected the continued militarization of the territory's governance decades after the initial Dutch conquest of the [[1870s]] and [[1880s]].

## The Onderafdeeling XXII Moekims

The *onderafdeeling* (sub-district) of the [[XXII Moekims]] comprised a cluster of twenty-two *moekim* (village cluster) administrative units within the territory of [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The *moekim* system was a fundamental element of the indigenous administrative structure in Atjeh, with each *moekim* grouping several *gampong* (villages) under the oversight of local officials who mediated between the community and the Dutch colonial hierarchy. Governor [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. N. A. Swart]] described the *moekimschap* during the Koeta-Radja meetings as comparable to Javanese *onderdistricten* (sub-districts), though the comparison was approximate given the distinct historical and social origins of the Acehnese *moekim*. The XXII Moekims formed part of the broader network of *moekim* clusters that constituted the *rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied* (directly ruled territory) of Groot-Atjeh, the core zone of Dutch administrative control centered on the capital at Koeta-Radja. The numbering system used for the *moekim* clusters — a convention also seen in the XXIII en XXIV Moekims, the XXV Moekims, and the XXVI Moekims — reflected the Dutch practice of organizing the indigenous administrative units into manageable groupings for the purposes of colonial governance, with each cluster placed under the supervision of a European official, whether a *Controleur* (Controller) or a *Civiel Gezaghebber*.

## Military Officers in Civil Administration

The appointment of military officers such as Watrin to *civiel gezaghebber* (civil authority holder) positions was a distinctive feature of the administrative system in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Government of Atjeh and Dependencies]], one that set it apart from the governance structures in other parts of the Netherlands Indies. In the standard colonial hierarchy, *civiel gezaghebbers* were civilian officials who administered *onderafdeelingen* (sub-districts) under the supervision of an *Assistent-Resident* or comparable authority. In Atjeh, however, the persistence of armed resistance — even in diminished form — meant that military officers with combat experience were often deemed more suitable for these positions than civilian administrators, particularly in areas where the threat of violence remained real. The practice of assigning Captains and other officers to civil administrative roles created a hybrid governance model in which military and civil functions were interwoven at the local level, reflecting the reality that the "pacification" of Atjeh was an ongoing process rather than a completed achievement. Like his fellow military-civil administrator [[Th. J. Veltman]], who served as *Civiel Gezaghebber* of the XXIII en XXIV Moekims, Watrin represented the intertwining of military and civilian authority that was a hallmark of the Atjeh administrative system.

## The Bestuursreorganisatie and the Question of Militarization

The *bestuursvergaderingen* at Koeta-Radja on [[19 January]] and [[20 January]] [[1914]] took place against the backdrop of a fundamental debate about the future direction of governance in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Government of Atjeh and Dependencies]]. The *bestuursreorganisatie* (administrative reorganization) proposed by the central government in Batavia envisioned a standardization of administrative structures across the Netherlands Indies, with a movement toward civilian governance and the gradual elimination of the special military-civil hybrid arrangements that characterized the Atjeh administration. Governor [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. N. A. Swart]] pushed back against this vision, arguing that the conditions in Atjeh — particularly the continued need for military oversight in the *moekim* districts — justified the retention of the *Civiel en Militair Gouverneur* (Civil and Military Governor) system and the practice of appointing military officers to civil administrative positions. For officers like Watrin, the outcome of this debate would determine whether their dual military-civil roles would be maintained, reformed, or abolished, with significant implications for their career trajectories and the governance of the territories they administered. The presence of military-civil administrators at the meetings served as a visible reminder of the distinctive governance model that the reorganization threatened to dismantle.

## Significance

Watrin's career, taken in its two-source totality, exemplifies the trajectory of the Dutch officer class in Atjeh from the *Marechaussee* patrols of the war years to the *civiel gezaghebber* posts of the post-war administration. As one of the *Marechaussee* commanders whose name "rang through the Indies like carillon music," he belongs to the colonial memory of the Atjeh-oorlog preserved in Merac's *Oost-Indisch Journael*; as *Kapitein der Infanterie* and *Civiel Gezaghebber* of the XXII Moekims, he was a working officer of the post-war administration whose presence at the 1914 *bestuursvergaderingen* places him at the centre of the debate over the future of the military-civil hybrid that had defined Atjeh's governance since the conquest.

## In the Maréchaussée Jubileum-nummer ([[1930]])

The *[[Overzicht van Onderscheidingen toegekend aan het Korps Maréchaussée in Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden (1890-1930)|Overzicht van Onderscheidingen]]* printed in the [[1930]] jubilee number of *[[Het Nieuws van den Dag voor Nederlandsch-Indië]]* records two awards to Watrin:

- [[1898]] — the *[[Militaire Willemsorde|Militaire Willems-Orde]]* 4th class, as *1ste Luits. Mar.*, together with [[Darlang|F. Darlang]]
- [[1906]] — the *[[Eeresabel]]*, as *1ste Luit. Mar.*

### Watrin's own account

The same issue printed a short statement Watrin had sent to the paper: "Ik verbleef in Atjeh van primo 1900 tot medio 1915. Het was de mooiste tijd van mijn loopbaan, bekroond met een Diner ten Hove op [[20 November]] [[1913]], gedurende mijn Europeesch verlof. Ik had het voorrecht links naast H. M. de Koningin aan tafel te zitten" — I stayed in Atjeh from the beginning of [[1900]] to the middle of [[1915]]. It was the finest time of my career, crowned by a Dinner at Court on 20 November 1913, during my European leave. I had the privilege of sitting at table on the left of Her Majesty the Queen.

Colonel [[C. A. Snell|L. A. Snell]] named Watrin among the old *vuurvreters* he remembered from the *soos* and the *rimboe*.
