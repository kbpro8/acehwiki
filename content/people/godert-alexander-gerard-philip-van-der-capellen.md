**Godert Alexander Gerard Philip, Baron van der Capellen** (1778–1848) served as Governor-General of the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]] from [[1816]] to [[1826]], presiding over the re-establishment of Dutch colonial rule after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|Anglo-Dutch Convention of 1814]] and the British interregnum associated with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and [[Lord Minto|Lord Minto]]. Although he is mentioned only briefly in P. H. van der Kemp's 1900 article, the passage in which he appears is a pointed one: Van der Kemp uses a single letter from Van der Capellen to [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]] to call into question the Governor-General's own firmness in dealing with the British.

## The Buitenzorg Letter of 9 January 1819

The episode Van der Kemp singles out concerns the complaints of [[Timmerman Thyssen|Governor Thyssen]] of [[Malacca]] about the conduct of certain officials at that port — most notably the harbourmaster [[Carnegy|Carnegy]], whom Thyssen accused of hypocrisy and of imposing a tariff favourable to Penang traders at Malacca's expense. Having taken cognisance of Thyssen's complaints, Van der Capellen wrote privately from Buitenzorg on 9 January [[1819]] to [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]] at [[Batavia]]. "The conduct of some officials at Malacca," he said, "I have learned with displeasure. T[hissen] must stand firm. Every Government in India in general must do so." Van der Kemp, having quoted this injunction, adds the laconic question: "Has Mr. Van der Capellen himself always put this into practice?" The implication is that the Governor-General's own record, in his dealings with the British in the years 1818–1824, hardly answered to the maxim of firmness he had laid down for Thyssen.

## The Wider Context

Van der Capellen's Governor-Generalship coincided with a sequence of humiliations for the Dutch colonial position: the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]] by Raffles in January 1819, the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] between Raffles and [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Jauhar Alum]] later the same year, the [[Wilhelmina mutiny and murder of Bouwens|Wilhelmina mutiny]] and the resulting [[Paper War|paper war]] with Penang over [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]], and finally the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London of 17 March 1824]], under which the Netherlands ceded its Indian factories, [[Malacca|Malacca]], and all claim to [[Singapore]] in exchange for a free hand on [[Sumatra]]. Van der Kemp's narrative of these events, while focused on the agents on the spot — Wolterbeek, Thyssen, [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]], [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]], [[Elout|Elout]] — repeatedly returns to the question of how firm the central administration at Buitenzorg and Buitenzorg's superior at The Hague actually were.

## Historical Standing

Outside Van der Kemp's article, Van der Capellen is remembered as a reforming Governor-General whose tenure saw the suppression of regional revolts and the early organisation of the colonial administration on its post-1815 footing. He was the recipient of numerous deputations from the outer possessions and the author of the celebrated *Rapport* of 1824 on the state of the Indies. His reputation, however, was long shadowed by thecatastrophes of his final years in office — above all the [[Java War (1825-1830)|Java War]] which broke out in 1825 and which he was accused of having done too little to prevent. Van der Kemp's barbed aside about firmness in 1819 thus aligns him with a longer Dutch historiographical tradition that has measured Van der Capellen's administration against the standard set by his more assertive successors.
