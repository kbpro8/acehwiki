**The moekim and imeum in Atjeh**, as described by [[R. L. Mellema|R. L. Mellema]] in *[[De Islaam in Indonesië (Mellema, 1947)|De Islaam in Indonesië (in het bijzonder op Java)]]* (Amsterdam, [[1947]]), are the Atjehnese forms of, respectively, the territorial congregation formed by several *desa*'s uniting to maintain a single Friday mosque, and the head of that congregation — the *imeum*, which Mellema glosses as "Atjeh's voor imaam" ("Atjeh's word for imaam"). Mellema's account is distinctive in that it gives an *etiology* of the moekim as a territorial parish: the canonical requirement that a Friday service be held only where at least forty adult male Muslims (*moekim* in the sense of "fixed resident") are settled could be met, in Atjeh, by several villages combining; and out of this combination there arose "nieuwe territoriale gemeenten" — new territorial communes — likewise called *moekim*, each placed under an *imeum*.

## The canonical requirement and the Javanese contrast

Mellema introduces the moekim–imeum system in the course of his discussion of the distinction between the *moskee* (*masdjid*, the mosque in which the Friday service is held) and the *langgar* (the prayer-house in which it is not). The Muslim law, he writes, requires that the Friday service (*Vrijdagdienst*) may be held only where at least "een veertigtal mannelijke, meerderjarige Moslims" — some forty adult male Muslims — are settled as permanent residents (*moekim*). This requirement is "uiteraard steeds het geval op de residentsschapshoofdplaats" — naturally always satisfied at the seat of a Residency — and it is for this reason that one finds a mosque, properly so called, only in such places. Where the requirement is not met, the building in which the *salaat* is performed is called not *moskee* but *langgar*.

In [[Java|Java]] the contrast is thus between the mosque-town (where forty resident men suffice) and the village with only a *langgar*. In [[Acheh|Atjeh]], however, Mellema records a distinctively different arrangement: "In Atjeh kon het gebeuren, dat verschillende desa's zich aaneensloten om gezamenlijk een moskee met Vrijdagsdienst te kunnen houden" — in Atjeh it could happen that several *desa*'s joined together in order to be able, jointly, to maintain a mosque with a Friday service. The canonical number of forty resident men was thus achieved not within a single settlement but across a cluster of settlements; and the cluster, once formed, became a new territorial unit.

## The territorial moekim

From this cooperation of several *desa*'s, Mellema writes, "zijn nieuwe territoriale gemeenten ontstaan, eveneens met de naam moekim aangeduid" — new territorial communes arose, likewise designated by the name *moekim*. The term *moekim* thus undergoes, in Atjeh, a semantic extension: from its canonical sense of "permanent resident" (the man whose presence, in numbers of forty, authorises the Friday service) it comes to denote the *territorial congregation* itself — the commune formed by the union of several villages for the purpose of maintaining the Friday mosque. This extension is, in Mellema's comparative framing, the distinctive Atjehnese contribution to the institutional geography of Indonesian Islam: where Java has the single-settlement mosque-town, Atjeh has the multi-village parish.

## The imeum

At the head of each such territorial moekim, Mellema continues, there stands an *imeum* — "Atjeh's voor imaam" — that is, the Atjehnese form of the Arabic *imām*. The *imeum* is thus, in Mellema's account, the head of the moekim conceived as a territorial parish, and his office is the institutional consequence of the canonical requirement that the Friday service have a leader. The Atjehnese *imeum* is to be distinguished from the Javanese *penghoeloe* (the head of the mosque, treated by Mellema in the immediately preceding pages of his survey) by virtue of the territorial character of the moekim over which the imeum presides: the penghoeloe is the head of a single mosque at a single place, while the imeum is the head of a parish comprising several villages.

## Relation to Snouck Hurgronje's account

Mellema's account is to be read alongside the more fully developed treatment of the same institutions in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *[[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|De Atjehers]]* (1893–94), where the [[Imeum Mukim|imeum mukim]] is described as the head of the [[Mukim|mukim]] (Acehnese parish) occupying the middle tier of the Acehnese administrative hierarchy between the [[Keuchi'|keuchi']] (village headman) below and the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] (territorial chief) above. Snouck Hurgronje further describes the historical "drift" of the imeum's office away from its religious foundation toward adat chieftaincy — a drift that Mellema, in his briefer comparative survey, does not address. What Mellema adds, by contrast, is the *etiological* observation that the territorial moekim arose from the practical need of several villages to combine in order to satisfy the canonical quorum for the Friday service: an observation that frames the Atjehnese parish as an institutional solution to a canonical requirement, rather than (as in Snouck's account) as a historical formation whose religious origin has been overlaid by adat. The two accounts are complementary: Snouck gives the full anatomy of the office, Mellema gives the canonical-genetic framing.

The [[De Buitenbezittingen|*De Buitenbezittingen*]] handbook of [[1917]] likewise draws on Snouck Hurgronje for its account of the imeum as the head of the moekim, and records the Shafiʿite requirement that the Friday service be held with a quorum of forty-four "mannelijke, vrije, meerderjarige moekims" within walking distance of the mosque — the same canonical requirement that Mellema cites as the origin of the territorial moekim.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, Mellema's account is significant as an independent comparative source for the moekim–imeum system, and as the source that most clearly articulates the canonical-genetic relation between the Friday-service quorum and the territorial parish. The account also illustrates the comparative method of Mellema's survey: the Atjehnese institution is introduced not for its own sake but as a counterpoint to the Javanese, and the comparison brings out the distinctive Atjehnese extension of the term *moekim* from "resident" to "parish." The entry thus complements the fuller Snouckian account of the [[Imeum Mukim|imeum mukim]] and the [[Mukim|mukim]] preserved elsewhere in the corpus, and it furnishes a mid-twentieth-century colonial confirmation of the canonical foundation of the Atjehnese territorial parish.
