**Boeröng** was the most feared of all spirits in Atjèhnese folk imagination — the ghost of a woman who died in childbirth (*maté didapoe*). Imagined as a woman with a hole in her back through which intestines protrude, the *boeröng* preyed on pregnant women and women in childbed. Comparable to *poentiana* (Malays), *koenti* (Sundanese), and *soendël bölöng* (Javanese), the *boeröng* had named graves worshipped like saints.

## Appearance and behaviour

The *boeröng* was imagined as a woman with a hole in her back through which her intestines protrude. She preyed on pregnant women and women in childbed (*kraamvrouw*), causing delirium and threatening their lives. When a *kraamvrouw* fell into delirium and asked for specific foods, she was identified as possessed by a particular named *boeröng*.

## Named boeröng's

The two most famous named *boeröng*'s were:
1. **Pötjoet Siti** — whose grave is at **Lambada** in moekim Bramöë (sagi [[XXVI Moekim's|XXVI Moekim's]]). When a *kraamvrouw* falls into delirium and asks for nangka fruits, *eumpiëng*, *idja plangi* or *keumbaj boendi*, she is said to be possessed by Pötjoet Siti.
2. **Srabi Tandjong** (also *Boeröng Srabi* / *Boeröng Tandjong*) — whose grave is at **Tandjong** in moekim Pagarajé. When the delirious *kraamvrouw* asks for *moeröng*-bladeren, dried buffalo meat (*sië baloe*), and salted duck eggs, Srabi Tandjong is identified as possessing her.

## Peutrön boeröng

The exorcism ritual was called **peutrön boeröng** ("bringing the boeröng downstairs"), performed by an expert who drove the *boeröng* from the possessed *kraamvrouw*, leading it out of the inner room, through the hall, down the house stairs, and onto the gampöng-path (*djoeröng*).

## Maté didapoe

A woman who died in childbed was called *maté didapoe* — considered a great misfortune for the survivors. She was believed to become a *boeröng*. Special burial practices existed to prevent her ghost from rising, including loosening the winds around the *kaphan* (shroud) before burial — failure to do so would produce a *boeröng poendjöt* ("wrapping-cloth ghost").

## The 44 herbs

The *kraamvrouw* carried a pouch of **aweuëh peuët plöh peuët** ("the 44 herbs") against the pudendum as protection against the *boeröng* and other evil spirits during the 44-day postpartum period.

## Connection to saints

The named *boeröng*'s had graves (*koeboe kramat*) that were worshipped like saints' graves, reflecting the ambiguous position of the *boeröng* in Atjèhnese folk belief — both feared as a predatory spirit and venerated as a being with supernatural power.
