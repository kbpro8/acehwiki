**Rimbaga** is the indigenous Indonesian technical term for the wax-resin-fat compound used in the *[[cire perdue|à cire perdue]]* (lost-wax) casting process to form the wax model from which a metal object is subsequently cast. The term is recorded — and the composition of the compound specified — in J. E. [[J. E. Jasper|Jasper]] en Mas [[Mas Pirngadie|Pirngadie]]'s *[[De Inlandsche Kunstnijverheid in Nederlandsch-Indië|De Inlandsche Kunstnijverheid in Nederlandsch-Indië]]*, Volume IV (the volume on gold- and silverwork), pp. 25–26, in a passage that Prof. [[T. J. Bezemer|T. J. Bezemer]] quotes verbatim in the [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|1933 plate-atlas]] to describe the casting process as practised by a Toba-Batak goldsmith on [[Noord-Sumatra]]. The passage is the immediate technical frame within which Bezemer then defines the [[Atjeh goldsmithing (soeasa technique)|Atjeh goldsmiths' alternative *soeasa* wrapping technique]], and *rimbaga* is accordingly one of the indigenous technical vocabularies through which the Atjeh goldsmithing context is documented in the late colonial Dutch scholarship.

## Composition

The Jasper-Pirngadie passage, as quoted by Bezemer, defines *rimbaga* as "*een soort was, mengsel van hars en vet*" — "a kind of wax, mixture of resin and fat." The compound is therefore a three-component mixture: wax (*was*), resin (*hars*), and fat (*vet*). The function of the three components is conventional in lost-wax casting: the wax provides the body and workability of the modelling material; the resin provides hardness and structural rigidity (so that the wax model does not deform under its own weight or under the weight of the clay mould); and the fat provides plasticity (so that the compound can be modelled and re-modelled in the hand and on the core). The specific proportions of the three components are not given by Jasper and Pirngadie, and presumably varied from goldsmith to goldsmith; but the three-component composition is the defining feature of *rimbaga* as an Indonesian lost-wax modelling compound.

## Use in the cire perdue process

The Jasper-Pirngadie passage quoted by Bezemer describes the use of *rimbaga* in the *[[cire perdue|à cire perdue]]* casting process as follows. The goldsmith first builds the *kern* (the core) of the object to be cast — a clay-and-burnt-rice-bran mixture, formed and baked to shape. This core serves as a *leest* (a last or form) on which the wax model is built up. The goldsmith then covers the core evenly with *rimbaga* — "*welke gelijkmatig met rimbaga … belegd wordt*" — and so obtains the *wassen voorvorm* (the wax fore-form), the wax model that is the exact shape of the metal object to be cast. On this wax fore-form, the goldsmith models the relief decoration in further *rimbaga* — "*waarop men eveneens met rimbaga de reliefvormen boetseert*" — building up the figures, ornaments, and surface decoration in the wax-resin-fat compound. Once the wax model is complete, the goldsmith attaches *giettuitjes* (pouring-channels) of *rimbaga* to it, coats the whole with dry clay powder, then encloses it in a thicker layer of clay mixed with burnt rice-bran. The mould is then dried, the wax melted out (leaving a hollow clay mould with the negative of the original wax model, including the relief decoration, on its inner surface), and molten metal poured in through the pouring-channels to replace the wax. When the metal has cooled, the clay mould is broken away — destroying the mould but releasing the cast-metal object.

The use of *rimbaga* in this process is, in two respects, distinctive. First, the same compound is used both for the wax fore-form (the body of the model) and for the relief decoration (the surface modelling): this means that the relief is not carved into the wax but built up on it, in the same material as the body. Second, the use of a wax-resin-fat compound — rather than pure beeswax — gives the model a working hardness that allows it to be handled, coated with clay, and enclosed in the mould without losing its shape; pure beeswax would be too soft for this purpose in the tropical Indonesian climate.

## Atjeh connection

The *rimbaga* passage of Jasper and Pirngadie describes the casting process as practised by a Toba-Batak goldsmith on [[Noord-Sumatra]]; it does not describe Atjeh practice directly. The Atjeh goldsmiths documented by Bezemer in the same chapter of the 1933 atlas did not, in fact, use the *rimbaga*-based *[[cire perdue|cire perdue]]* process for the [[Atjeh goldsmithing (soeasa technique)|*soeasa* wrapping technique]] that is the Atjeh-specific contribution to the chapter. The Atjeh goldsmiths wrapped a copper core with [[spinsbek|*soeasa*]] sheet rather than casting the ornament in one piece. The contrast is documented by Bezemer precisely because Jasper and Pirngadie had already documented the Toba-Batak alternative — and *rimbaga* is the technical vocabulary in which that alternative is described.

The Atjeh connection of *rimbaga* is therefore indirect: *rimbaga* is the technical term for the modelling compound used in the *cire perdue* process that frames, by analogy, the Atjeh goldsmiths' alternative wrapping technique. The term belongs to the same technical vocabulary as *[[spinsbek|spinsbek]]* / *[[spinsbek|soeasa]]* (the alloy used in the Atjeh wrapping), *[[cire perdue|cire perdue]]* (the casting process used by the Toba-Batak goldsmiths), *[[granuleeren|granuleeren]]* (the granulation technique used by the Batak, Dyak, and Timorese silversmiths), and *[[filigraanwerk|filigraanwerk]]* (the filigree technique used in the Padangsche Bovenlanden) — a vocabulary that, taken together, constitutes the late-colonial Dutch technical lexicon for Sumatran metalwork, within which Atjeh metalwork is described as a regional variant.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, *rimbaga* is significant principally as a piece of the technical vocabulary in which Atjeh goldsmithing is documented in the late colonial period. The term is recorded in Jasper and Pirngadie's handbook — the standard Dutch colonial reference on Indonesian craft — and is preserved in Bezemer's [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|1933 atlas]] as the indigenous name for the wax-resin-fat compound of the Sumatran *[[cire perdue|cire perdue]]* process. The survival of the indigenous term in a Dutch colonial handbook — rather than its replacement by a Dutch descriptive term like *was-hars-vet mengsel* — is itself notable: it is the kind of preservation of indigenous craft vocabulary that, in the colonial-craft-scholarship convention, was the contribution of an indigenous expert like Mas [[Mas Pirngadie|Pirngadie]] to the collaborative Jasper-Pirngadie handbook. The term *rimbaga* is, in this sense, a small piece of indigenous Sumatran craft knowledge preserved in the Dutch colonial record, and a marker of the collaborative character of the scholarship on which the AcehWiki's Atjeh goldsmithing entries draw.
