The **[[Sawah|sawah]]** (irrigated rice field, rice paddy) was the characteristic terrain of the Atjehnese lowlands — a flooded or marshy field, crossed only by narrow *galangs-an* (dikes), on which the Atjehneezen cultivated their padi (rice). The sawah was, in Borel's account, both the economic foundation of the Atjehnese war effort (the padi fed the warriors) and the most dangerous tactical terrain on which the Dutch could operate: a column marching across a sawah lost all order, exposed itself to Atjehnese fire from the boschrand, and could be cut to pieces by a *charge* of [[Klewang|klewang]]-armed warriors.

## Description and Tactical Significance

The sawah was, in Borel's account, an irrigated rice field — flooded or marshy for much of the year, crossed only by narrow *galangs-an* (dikes) that served as pathways. The sawah's tactical significance was double-edged. On the one hand, the sawah was open ground — the Dutch could see and be seen across it, and the *salvoes* of Beaumont-geweer fire could be brought to bear on an enemy advancing across it. The [[Acehnese attack on Penajoeng (11 April 1874)|Acehnese massed attack on Penajoeng]] of [[11 April]] [[1874]] — when a band of 400–500 Atjehneezen "zeer net uitgedoste" approached the omheining, intending to spring with the klewang, and were stopped by the Dutch artillerie and a half bataljon that flanked them — was a typical example of an enemy advance across open sawah being broken by Dutch fire. On the other hand, the sawah was marshy — a column marching across it lost all order, its men sank into the mud, and its formations could not be maintained. The [[Battle of Lemboe (25–26 December 1873)|battle of Lemboe]] of [[25 December|25]]–[[26 December]] [[1873]] — when Dutch troops were "exposed on the open sawah to moordend vuur" for hours — was a typical example of the danger of advancing across sawah under enemy fire.

## The Padi and the War

The sawah was, in Borel's account, the economic foundation of the Atjehnese war effort. The padi (rice plant) grown on the sawah fed the warriors; the cutting of standing padi was, in Borel's doctrine, one of the two principal means (*kappen en branden*) of overcoming Atjeh's resistance. [[Jan van Swieten|van Swieten]] had prohibited the cutting of standing padi as a humane measure, and had driven the gemoedelijkheid so far as to grant the [[Padi-snijden on the Langkroek sawah (1874)|padi-snijden]] concession to [[Langkroek]] — allowing the bevolking to cut their ripe padi provided they "de geweren aflegden." The result was that "zoolang het padi-snijden duurde, werd werkelijk geen schot op het bivouak gelost" — but as soon as the last bundle was harvested, the Atjehneezen "weder van alle zijden kwamen opdagen, ditmaal behoorlijk met hunne zware geweren gewapend" and opened a "levendig vuur." [[Generaal Pel|Pel]] reversed the policy: he harvested the padi for Dutch use, cutting the enemy's food supply and feeding the bezettingskorps. The sawah was thus both the terrain on which the war was fought and the economic resource for which it was fought.

## The Sawah and the Longbatta Disaster

The most damaging single tactical episode involving the sawah in Borel's account is the [[Battle of Longbatta (31 December 1874)|Longbatta disaster]] of [[31 December]] [[1874]]. [[Majoor van der Meer|van der Meer]]'s column, ordered to investigate the nearest kampong-rand to the south-east of the captured Mesigit benteng, misinterpreted the order: he abandoned the captured benteng without leaving a garrison, and marched his whole force southward over open, marshy sawah. The Atjehneezen, who had streamed back in, occupied the abandoned benteng and cut off the column's retreat. Van der Meer's column, "marching calmly across sawah, did not notice." Heavy firing broke out on the far side; Pel's staff tried in vain to recall van der Meer by signals. The retreat across the marshy sawah, under furious pursuit and cold-steel attacks, became a rout; the battalion lost cohesion; orders could not be given. The wounded and exhausted who fell behind were mercilessly killed by the Atjehneezen. The lesson Borel draws is categorical: marching in dispersed order over soaked sawah along wooded edges exposed a column to massacre — the lesson of the [[Atoeh catastrophe (13 February 1876)|Atoeh catastrophe]] of [[13 February]] [[1876]], when [[Jan van Swieten (Kapitein)|kapitein van Swieten]]'s detachment of 2 officers and 57 men was cut to pieces on the drassige sawah near [[Lamsajoen]].
