*village*, *wet-rice field*, *swamp*, **Paja**, **Alang-alang**, **Galangan**, **Nipah-bosch** and **Kreupelbosch** were the principal [[Achehnese]] / Malay geographical terms employed throughout Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* to describe the terrain of the [[Groot-Atjeh]] theatre of operations. The village was the basic unit of settlement, typically a cluster of houses surrounded by irrigated rice paddy. The *sawah* — often wet and marshy — impeded military movement and provided cover for Achehnese defenders. The marsh/swamp and marsh/swamp were waterlogged terrain features that channelled Dutch columns onto raised paths and embankments. The cogon grass — described as "verzengende" (scorching) on [[25 May]] [[1896]] — covered fallow *sawahs* and could itself be set alight. The *galangan* / rice paddy embankments served as cover for infantry and as footbridges across marsh. The nipah palm grove and thicket were difficult terrain that sheltered Achehnese ambushes.

## Etymology and Meaning

The Achehnese/Malay geographical vocabulary is largely descriptive. also *kampoeng* is Malay for "village", "hamlet" — the basic unit of settlement. *Sawah* is Malay/Indonesian for "irrigated rice paddy" — a flooded field for wet-rice cultivation, typically bounded by embankments. *Rawah* is Malay for "marsh", "swamp" — waterlogged ground. *Paja* is Acehnese/Malay for "marsh", "swamp" — a near-synonym of *rawah*. *Alang-alang* is Malay for cogon grass, a tall coarse grass that colonises fallow land; the reduplication is typical of Malay botanical terms. also *galang-galangan* is Malay for "embankment", "bund" — the raised earth balks that separate flooded rice plots; the term also denotes a footbridge over marsh. *Nipah-bosch* is a Dutch compound: the nipah palm, *Nypa fruticans* + Dutch for "forest", "grove" — a mangrove-palm swamp forest, characteristic of coastal Aceh. *Kreupelbosch* is Dutch: crippled, scrubby + *bosch* — a low thicket or scrub. *Kampoeng rand* is Dutch-Acehnese: *kampoeng* + edge — the village edge.

## Historical Context

In the first semester of [[1896]] the geographical terms were not merely descriptive but tactically determinative. The Dutch mobile columns operated in a landscape of *sawah*, *rawah*, *paja*, *nipah-bosch* and *kreupelbosch* that channelled them onto raised paths and embankments, where Achehnese defenders could ambush them. The *sawah* — wet, muddy, and crossed by *galangan* embankments — impeded the movement of European infantry in particular; the Dutch soldier in heavy boots floundered where the barefoot Achehnese moved freely. The *alang-alang* — "verzengende" (scorching) on [[25 May]] [[1896]] — could be set alight as a defensive measure, smoking out Dutch positions. The *nipah-bosch* along the coast — a mangrove-palm swamp — sheltered Achehnese ambush positions and was traversed only with difficulty. The *kreupelbosch* provided concealment for retreating Achehnese fighters. The *kampoeng rand* — the village edge — was the typical line of contact between Dutch columns and Achehnese defenders.

## Significance

The Achehnese geographical vocabulary is the indispensable key to understanding the tactical problem of the [[Aceh War]]. The Dutch soldier, encumbered by heavy boots, webbing and rifle, was at a severe disadvantage in the *sawah*, *rawah*, *paja* and *nipah-bosch* that characterised the theatre of operations, where the barefoot Achehnese defender moved freely and could choose the moment of engagement. The *alang-alang* — set alight as a defensive measure — and the *kreupelbosch* — providing concealment for retreating fighters — were active elements of the Achehnese tactical repertoire. The Dutch response — raised paths, railway embankments, and the systematic digging away of features such as the *grafheuvels* at [[Lamdjamoe]] — was a sustained attempt to render the Achehnese landscape militarily legible. The survival of these geographical terms in modern Indonesian and Acehnese usage testifies to their enduring descriptive power.

## Account from *Het recht op den grond in Atjeh* ([[D. W. N. de Boer]], [[1931]]) — the ateueng in Atjehnese land law

The *galangan* of the general Malay vocabulary corresponds to the Atjehnese *ateueng*, the small dike separating one wet rice field from another. In [[D. W. N. de Boer]]'s account of Atjehnese land law the *ateueng* was not merely a terrain feature but a legal one, serving at once as a mark of prior cultivation and as a metaphor for the limits of a right.

### As evidence of cultivation

In [[Groot-Atjeh]], a plaintiff asserting an earlier possessory right over wet rice fields (*[[Oemong|oemòng]]*) that had been left fallow had in particular to point to visible traces of *ateueng*; a former watercourse (*[[Loeeng|loeëng]]*) or the *[[Oendja ateueng|oendja]]* — the boundary post buried at the corners of a *sawah*, of old custom in the *landschap* [[Pidië|Pidië-proper]] — could serve as further proof. Witnesses alone did not suffice.

### As the limit of the priority right

The possessor of a *[[Sawah|sawah]]* could assert a priority right over adjoining ground at the *oeloe* (head-end) of his field, extending over one *[[Thirhōm idja|thirhōm idja]]* — the distance of a single throw of the tightly rolled shoulder-cloth, reckoned at about twenty-five metres — so as to reserve a place for a field-hut or watch-house. But the right applied only to ground not destined for rice cultivation, and never to ground suitable for extending a *sawah* complex. The Atjehnese expressed the limit in a saying: "dit recht wordt niet toegekend buiten de *ateueng*" — this right is not granted beyond the little rice-field dikes. De Boer explained that the phrase was self-evident to an Atjehnese, because an *ateueng* is a separation between a rice field and adjoining land of like kind, whereas no *ateuengs* are used where the *sawah* passes over into a mountain slope, hilly terrain or *[[Ladang|ladang]]* ground. In some districts the reserve was revised and restated in an ordinary measure of length: in [[Sawang]] (*onderafdeeling* [[Lho' Seumawe|Lho' Seumaweh]]) it was fixed from [[1917]] at only eight metres.
