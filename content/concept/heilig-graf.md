*holy grave* — Dutch for "holy grave", the [[Achehnese]] *keramat* — was the term for an Islamic shrine or holy grave venerated by the Achehnese as the burial place of a saint (*wali*) or other religious notable. Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* identifies several *heilige graven* in the [[Groot-Atjeh]] theatre of operations: the at the foot of the *gebergte* west of [[Kenoï|Kenoi]]; the at the top of the [[Tjot Tjako]] mountain, a simple stone grave; the a *steenen graf* — stone grave — on the road Lamtih–Lam Manjang; and the *heilige graf van Teungkoe Lam-Bira*. The *heilig graf* was the institutional counterpart of the [[masdjid]] (mosque): where the masdjid was the centre of the living religious community, the heilig graf was the centre of veneration of a dead saint. The two were often physically adjacent, and the grave was frequently the site of [[kaloet]] (ascetic meditation).

## Etymology and Meaning

The phrase *heilig graf* is Dutch: holy + grave. The semantic field covers any venerated burial site, but in the colonial Indies context the term specifically denoted the indigenous *keramat* — a shrine, typically a grave, venerated for the blessing power of the saint buried there. The word *keramat* is from Arabic pl. *karāmāt*, the miraculous gift of a saint. In Achehnese usage the *keramat* was a fixed sacred site, distinct from but related to the practice of ascetic retreat and the institutional hierarchy of the [[oelama]]s. The stone grave of *Teungkoe Mesir* indicates a built structure rather than a simple earthen mound. The term grave mounds is used in a more secular archaeological sense for pre-Islamic mounds.

## Historical Context

In the first semester of [[1896]] the *heilige graven* were nodes of religious geography that the Dutch campaigns traversed or targeted. The *Toean di Boh*, at the foot of the mountains west of [[Kenoï|Kenoi]], was a landmark in the [[XXII Moekims|sagi XXII]]. The *Toean Tjot Tjako*, at the top of the [[Tjot Tjako]] mountain, marked a strategic high point. The *Teungkoe Mesir* — "steenen graf on road Lamtih–Lam Manjang" — was on the line of march between two contested kampongs. The *heilige graf van Teungkoe Lam-Bira* marked the resistance position near Lam-Bira. The *grafheuvels* at 225 m west of [[Lamdjamoe]] — described as the positions behind which Achehnese opponents "zich vroeger steeds nestelden if zij den post beschoten" (always entrenched themselves when they fired on the post) — show the strategic overlap of grave-topography and tactical position. The Dutch decision to "afgraven" (dig away) these grafheuvels in the spring of [[1896]] was an explicit attack on the Achehnese religious infrastructure.

## Examples in the Source

Kruisheer's principal *heilig graf* references include: the *Toean di Boh* — at the foot of the *gebergte* west of [[Kenoï|Kenoi]]. The *Toean Tjot Tjako* — at the top of the [[Tjot Tjako]] mountain, a simple stone grave. The *Teungkoe Mesir* — a *steenen graf* on the road Lamtih–Lam Manjang. The *heilige graf van Teungkoe Lam-Bira* — near the resistance positions at [[Lam-Bira]]. The *grafheuvels* at 225 m west of [[Lamdjamoe]] — the grave mounds behind which Achehnese opponents entrenched themselves when firing on the post; these were dug away in the spring of [[1896]]. The honourific prefix Acehnese *tuan*, "lord", "master" applied to *Toean di Boh* and *Toean Tjot Tjako* marks the veneration of the buried figure.

## Significance

The *heilig graf* is the institutional expression of the saint-veneration that grounded Achehnese religious geography. The network of graves — each marking the burial of a saint, an oelama, or a teungkoe — defined the sacred topography of Groot-Atjeh and provided the religious infrastructure within which the [[kaloet]] practice and the [[sabil Allah]] doctrine were sustained. The Dutch campaigns against the religious hierarchy in the spring of [[1896]] implicitly targeted this infrastructure: the digging away of the [[Lamdjamoe]] grafheuvels was a tactical operation with religious-symbolic significance. The survival of the *keramat* institution in post-war Acehnese religious life — most famously at [[Teungkoe di Tiro|Samalanga]] and elsewhere — testifies to its enduring role in the religious geography of the region.
