**Oemòng** was the Atjehnese term for the wet or irrigated rice field — the *natte rijstveld*, the *[[Sawah|sawah]]* proper — and the object of the most closely regulated part of Atjehnese land law.

## Fiscal treatment

Within the *[[Gampong|gampong]]* boundaries the *oemòng* bore the *[[Boeët oemòng|boeët oemòng]]*, the Atjehnese land rent levied on the yield at one *[[Naleh|naleh]]* of *padi* per *naleh* of seed, roughly two to three per cent; outside the *gampong* it bore an identical levy as part of the *[[Adat glé|adat glé]]*.

## Law of proof

In [[Groot-Atjeh]], a plaintiff asserting a prior possessory right over *oemòng* left fallow (*[[Roh|roh]]*) had to point to visible traces of *[[Ateueng|ateueng]]*, and might strengthen his case with a former *[[Loeeng|loeëng]]* or a buried *[[Oendja ateueng|oendja]]*.

## Alienation and social opinion

Complete alienation of rice fields was regarded as a disgrace. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]], quoted by [[D. W. N. de Boer]] from *[[De Atjehers (Snouck Hurgronje)|De Atjehers]]* Vol. I, p. 312, wrote that although the sale of land in Atjeh could not be called exactly rare, "geldt hij, die zijn geerfde rijstveld geheel of gedeeltelijk verkoopt in de publieke opinie als een verkwister" — he who sells his inherited rice field wholly or in part passes in public opinion for a spendthrift. The reprehensible practice was called *[[Peupia oemòng|peupia oemòng]]*, "the breaking up of one's rice field," for by it a man ruined the *[[Pangoelee hareukat|pangoeléé hareukat]]*, the "master of all breadwinning." Sale (*[[Poeblee|poebléë]]*) of rice fields accordingly hardly occurred.

## Measurement

The size of a rice field in Atjeh was expressed not in surface but in the quantity of *[[Bibit|bibit]]* (seed padi) needed on average to plant it, measured in *[[Goentja|goentja]]*, *[[Naleh|naleh]]*, *[[Gantang|gantang]]* and *[[Areh|areh]]*.
