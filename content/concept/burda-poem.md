**Burda (poem)**, formally titled *Qasīdat al-Burdah* ("Poem of the Cloak") and known in Arabic as *al-Burdah* ("the mantle") — also called *al-Burdah al-Muḥammadiyya* — is a celebrated thirteenth-century Arabic ode composed by the Egyptian Sufi poet and scholar [[Al-Busiri|al-Būṣīrī]] in praise of the Prophet [[Muhammad]]. The poem became one of the most widely recited, copied, and commented-upon devotional texts in the Islamic world, and reached the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Acheh]] by the early modern period, where a sixteenth-century Malay translation circulated among scholars, mystics, and the Muslim elite. The Malay version — preserved in the digitised manuscript DLP-54, *Een 16de eeuwse Maleise vertaling van de Burda van al-Busiri* — became a principal vehicle for the diffusion of Arabic religious terminology into Acehnese literary culture and remains a central text of [[Acehnese religious poetry]].

## Authorship and Composition

[[Al-Busiri|Al-Būṣīrī]] (d. [[1295]]) — a Berber-origin calligrapher and scholar living in [[Bilbis]], [[Egypt]] — composed the poem in the mid-thirteenth century, most likely around [[1265]]–[[1270]] (the exact date is uncertain). Modern Arabic literary scholarship identifies him unequivocally as the author, although traditional Acehnese accounts sometimes attribute the composition instead to the Sufi scholar [[Imam al-Bukhari|Imam al-Bukhari]] — a confusion that reflects the poem's absorption into local devotional genealogies rather than a separate tradition.

The poem is a *qaṣīda* of approximately [[160]] verses, organised in classical Arabic poetic sections — *nasīb* (amatory prelude), *raḥīl* (journey), and *madīḥ* (praise) — that mirror the format of the Acehnese *kasida*, reinforcing its suitability for local devotional use. Sources disagree on the number of internal divisions: some describe ten sections, others eight, while the Acehnese devotional tradition sometimes speaks of thirteen stanzas, the discrepancies reflecting different manuscript recensions rather than conflicting authorship.

## Themes and Imagery

The poem's central themes are the spiritual perfection of the Prophet, the miraculous nature of the [[Qurʾān]], and the believer's yearning for divine mercy and intercession. Key theological concepts such as *achirat* (the afterlife) and *rasulullah* (the messenger of God) are prominently featured, alongside invocations of the protective *[[Kawthar]]* (the heavenly pool of the Prophet). The Prophet is famously described as "a flower so tender, as the full moon so great, as the sea so lofty" — imagery that reappears in the Malay rendition — and the [[Qurʾān|Qurʾānic]] word is figured as "pearls in the shell of a speaking mouth."

Other recurring motifs, as reflected in the Malay translation, include the Prophet's role as "the lord of both worlds" and "the master of jinn and humanity"; the contrast between *hunger* (*kelaparan*) and *satiety* as metaphors for spiritual longing; the struggle against *satan* (Arabic: *shaitan*) and satanic desires; and the admonition to seek Allah's forgiveness. The defeat of *kāfir* (non-believers) and *jinn* (supernatural beings) provides the poem's polemical counterpoint to its devotional core.

## The Sixteenth-Century Malay Translation

A Malay version of the *Burda*, dated to the sixteenth century, survives in the manuscript DLP-54 (*Een 16de eeuwse Maleise vertaling van de Burda van al-Busiri*). The translation renders the Arabic theological vocabulary into Malay while preserving key Arabic words in italics (e.g., *shaitan*, *sunnat*), and is accompanied by linguistic and exegetical footnotes that clarify archaic Malay terms and Arabic roots. Often referred to in Dutch sources as the *Maleise tekst van de Burda*, the translation incorporates a dense inter-lingual glossary in which Arabic terms are rendered with Acehnese-Malay equivalents — for example, [[A. 'ahd|A. 'ahd]] ("setia, afspraak, avereenkomst"; "loyalty, agreement, compact"). This glossary demonstrates the deep penetration of Arabic theological concepts into Acehnese religious discourse and provides a valuable source for the study of Arabic loanwords in the Acehnese Malay dialect.

The Malay *Burda* was likely used in Acehnese *madrasahs* and *khalwat* (spiritual retreats) as a didactic text for memorisation and recitation. The translation preserves the theological concepts of the Arabic original while integrating Acehnese idiom — translators rendered Arabic metaphors such as the *necklace* of *spot, shame and disgrace* into Malay poetic forms. Through trade and scholarly exchange, the poem had entered Aceh by the [[1500s]], and local scribes produced the Malay rendition that adapted its language to the Acehnese context.

## Ritual and Cultural Reception in Aceh

In Aceh the *Burda* was recited during *maulid* (Mawlid) celebrations — known locally as *dikië jadjandji* (the dhikr of [[Barzanji|al-Barzanjī]]) — as well as during *tahlilan* gatherings and as part of *ta'ziyah* rituals. Its verses were memorised by *ulama* and incorporated into *nyanyian* (chant) repertoires, and Acehnese *bakāsidah* often rendered the poem in Acehnese translation or chanted it in Arabic, linking the classical Arabic literary form to local devotional practice. The poem was traditionally sung on the fifteenth day of [[Ramadan]] and used as an amulet against illness, fire, and shipwreck; according to one source, the Ottoman custodians of the Prophet's cloak would present a water-soaked cloth to distinguished members of the Acehnese royal household after the annual unveiling, and the *Burda* itself was also distributed in bottled form for consumption during Ramadan meals — a direct material link between the poem and Acehnese courtly practice.

Its verses were used to inspire resistance against colonial incursions and to affirm the sultanate's Islamic identity. Acehnese scholars incorporated the *Burda* into *tazkirah* (biographical) literature and *qasidah* recitals, and the poem's emphasis on the Prophet's intercessory power resonated with the Acehnese emphasis on *wali* (saints) and the *sulṭān*'s claim to divine legitimacy. Manuscripts of the *Al-Burda* appear in the collections of the [[Aceh Museum]] and the [[Leiden University Library]], evidencing its long-standing presence in the region. The translation's extensive list of Arabic-Malay lexical items illustrates how the poem functioned as a conduit for Arabic religious concepts entering the local cultural sphere.

## Legacy

The *Burda* inspired a vast body of commentaries, translations, and *takhmīs* (expansions) across the Muslim world, including the Malay version examined here. Its verses were treated as a powerful amulet in [[North Africa]] and in Aceh, where they were believed to protect houses from fire and ships from wreckage. The translation of the *Burda* facilitated the incorporation of Arabic theological vocabulary into the Acehnese language, shaping the theological lexicon that underlies subsequent Acehnese religious literature, legal texts, and oral tradition. Its role in the diffusion of Arabic religious terminology highlights the broader processes of cultural and linguistic exchange between the Arab-Islamic world and the [[Malay Archipelago]] during the early modern period.
