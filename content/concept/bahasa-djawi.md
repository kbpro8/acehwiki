**Bahasa Djawi** ("the Jawi language") is the name given in the religious literature of [[Acheh]] and of the wider Malay-Indonesian world to the Malay language in its written, literary form — the language of the court, of religious scholarship, and of the *kitab*. It was the language in which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] composed his Malay treatises, the language in which the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]] were written, and the language that Raniri knew — as [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] records — before he ever set foot in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]].

## The term

The term *Djawi* (modern *Jawi*) is, in its widest sense, a designation for the Malay of the Muslim Malay-Indonesian world, written in the Arabic-based *Jawi* script and used as the lingua franca of religion, commerce, and diplomacy from the harbours of [[Gudjarat|Gujarat]] to the Straits of Malacca and the Spice Islands. In the narrower sense in which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Raniri]] uses the term — the *bahasa Djawi* of the Achehnese court — it designates the specific literary Malay that was the language of the court of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] and his successors, and that served as the vehicle for the production of Malay religious prose in the golden age and the orthodox reaction of the seventeenth century.

## Bahasa Djawi in Gujarat

[[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]]'s introduction to the [[1955]] edition of Raniri's two Malay treatises makes clear that the *bahasa Djawi* was not unfamiliar in the harbour towns of [[Gudjarat|Gujarat]] in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The coastal towns of Gujarat had at this period a very international population; ships regularly sailed thence to the Strait of Malaka; among the mercenary troops of the sixteenth century were "Javanese" — that is, in this context, Indonesians. To learn Malay, Raniri had no need of distant travel: "there were Malays enough in Gujarat, and his uncle [[Muhammad Djailani Hamid]] will have spoken that language too." Whether Raniri wrote his first Malay books in Gujarat or elsewhere is, however, an open question.

## Bahasa Djawi at the Achehnese court

In [[Acheh]], the *bahasa Djawi* was the language of the court. Raniri, on his arrival in [[1637]], knew the language and had already written two Malay works in it, while a third was in progress. His position in Acheh was therefore that of an immigrant scholar who entered a court in which his own literary language was already the language of religious and political life — a position that gave him, with the favour of [[Iskandar Thani]] and [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Taj al-Ālam]], an immediate authority in the religious controversies of the day.

## The witness of the *Tibjān* and *Ḥujjat*

The [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]] are written in the *bahasa Djawi* of the Achehnese court. The Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 3291 of the *Tibjān* — the only complete manuscript, copied about [[1855]] for [[Herman Neubronner van der Tuuk|Van der Tuuk]] from a good older exemplar of Achehnese provenance — preserves Achehnese linguistic forms, such as *sidjuk* (Ach. *sidjueʾ*) for *sedjuk*, and the consistent replacement of *kesembilan* with *kesemilan* (cf. Ach. *seumah*, Mal. *sembah*). The Moluccan manuscript Cod. Or. 1255 of the *[[Ma'rifat al-madhahib|Maʿrifat al-madhāhib*]] with interlinear Malay translation — which Voorhoeve identifies as Raniri's source for the second part of the *Tibjān* — preserves an "old-fashioned" Malay in which, for instance, *pulang* is used for *pula*.

## The play of *kuphō* and *kuphu*

A remarkable trace of the *bahasa Djawi* in the religious literature of Acheh is the play on the words *kuphō* (Arabic *kufr*, "unbelief") and *kuphu* (Arabic *kufʾ*, "equal, like") cited by [[Hamzah Pansuri]] in his [[Kitab al-Muntahi|*Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] — a play that depends on the fact that *pada* in Achehnese means both "equal" (Javanese *pepada*) and "sufficient" (Malay *pada*). [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] notes that "the play is in the language of Pasai — that is, in the Achehnese Malay of Pasai" — and that it cannot be reproduced in standard Malay. The play is itself a document of the linguistic specificity of the *bahasa Djawi* of the Achehnese court: a Malay in which Achehnese forms and meanings were still audible beneath the surface of the Arabic script.

## Significance

The *bahasa Djawi* of the Achehnese court is the language of the golden age of Achehnese letters — the language of [[Hamzah Pansuri]]'s [[Asrar al-'arifin|*Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and [[Kitab al-Muntahi|*Kitāb al-Muntahī*]], of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]'s [[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|*Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn*]] and [[Chirkah|*Chirkah*]], and of [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Raniri]]'s [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]], [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]], [[Sirat al-Mustaqim|*Ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*]], and [[Bustan al-Salatin|*Bustān as-Salāṭīn*]]. It is also the language by which the scholastic apparatus of Arabic and Persian Islam was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world: Raniri's Malay translations from the [[Tamhid fi bayan at-tawhid|*Tamhīd*]] of [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|as-Sālimī]], the [[al-Insan al-Kamil|*al-Insān al-kāmil*]] of [[Abd al-Karim al-Jili|al-Jīlī]], and the [[Ma'rifat al-madhahib|*Maʿrifat al-madhāhib*]] are among the principal vehicles of this transmission.
