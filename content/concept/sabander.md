**Sabander** — the Malay and Achehnese *shahbandar*, the port-official responsible for the regulation of foreign trade at the ports of the Malay-Indonesian archipelago — was the principal commercial intermediary between the European East India companies and the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s court, the official who set the prices at which the Dutch, English and French could buy pepper, and the official whose rearrest of [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] on a fabricated theft charge in December [[1600]] provoked [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]]'s seizure of the Arab junks and the resulting armed clash with the Atjeh shore batteries. The sabander is the principal named Achehnese office in Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The office

The sabander — Persian *shah-bandar*, "king of the port" — was the principal commercial officer of the early-modern Malay-Indonesian port-kingdom. The office was responsible for the reception of foreign merchants, the regulation of their trade, the assessment and collection of port dues, the settlement of commercial disputes, and the setting of prices at which foreign merchants could buy local produce. At [[Acheh|Atjeh]], the sabander was the principal intermediary between the European East India companies and the sultan's court: he negotiated the contracts, set the prices, and supervised the delivery of the pepper.

## The 1600 pepper contract

The sabander of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] was the official with whom [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] negotiated the pepper contract of [[28 December]] [[28 December]] [[1600]] — the contract that fixed the price, quantity, delivery time and export duties, and that provided that "geen koopluijden ofte schippers van watt natie die ook zouden mogen zijn en zullen geen peper mogen koopen offte laeden, voor en all eer de Hollanders hare ladinge bekomen hebben". The sabander's role was central to the negotiation: the sultan at first signed a contract, then tore it up when the Dutch had Hans Decker translate the text and discovered discrepancies with what had been agreed. The contract of [[28 December]] [[1600]] was the result of the sabander's renewed negotiation.

## The rearrest of Frederik de Houtman

The sabander of [[Pedir|Pedir]] was the official who, on the sultan's authority, rearrested [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] on a fabricated theft charge in December [[1600]] — the rearrest that provoked [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]]'s seizure of the Arab junks on [[12 January]] [[1601]] and the resulting armed clash with the Atjeh shore batteries. Van der Woude records: "aan land werd hij echter onverhoeds op last van den sabander van Pedir op een verzonnen beschuldiging van diefstal gevangen genomen, en met toestemming van den vorst tezamen met een scheepsgezel (...) naar Pedir vervoerd; daar werden zij in de boeien geslagen". The sabander of Pedir's role was the proximate cause of the breakdown of the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit]] and the principal instance of the sabander's power to obstruct the European trade at Atjeh.

## Significance

The sabander is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal named Achehnese office of the early-modern period and the principal commercial intermediary between the European East India companies and the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s court. The sabander's role in the [[Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit (1600-1601)|Van Caerden visit of 1600-1601]] — both in the negotiation of the pepper contract of [[28 December]] [[1600]] and in the rearrest of [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] that provoked the armed clash — frames the office as the central commercial-institutional instrument of the sultan's balancing policy, and the principal instance of the early-modern Malay-Indonesian practice of delegating commercial regulation to a port-official. The sabander's role at Atjeh also frames the early-modern Malay-Indonesian port as a regulated commercial space — not a free market — and the European trade at Atjeh as a regulated trade conducted through the sabander rather than directly with the pepper producers.
