**The djinamée** — the Atjehnese *huwelijksgift* (marriage gift) — is the marriage payment that, in the adatrecht of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], is made by the man to the woman at the marriage, and that has become a validity requirement (*geldigheidsvereiste*) of the marriage — so that, without the *djinamée*, the marriage is not validly concluded. The institution is cited by [[B. ter Haar BzN|B. ter Haar BzN]] in *[[Beginselen en Stelsel van het Adatrecht (Ter Haar, 4th ed. 1950)|Beginselen en Stelsel van het Adatrecht]]* (first ed. [[1939]]; 4th ed. [[1950]]) as a typical example of a marriage payment that has the character of a *huwelijksgift* — distinguished from the *bruidschat* (bride-price) proper — and as an Atjehnese instance of the wider Indonesian (and Muslim) category of the marriage gift.

## The marriage gift distinguished from the bride-price

[[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]] distinguishes, in his systematic account of Indonesian marriage payments, between the *bruidschat* (bride-price) — the payment that has the characteristic effect of transferring the woman and the children to the husband's kin-group — and the *huwelijksgift* (marriage gift) — the payment made to the woman personally, which has become a validity requirement of the marriage without the transfer-effect of the bride-price. The *djinamée* of Atjeh is cited as a typical example of the latter category, alongside the *soenrang* of South Celebes and the Muslim *mas kawin*. "De djinamée van Atjeh," Ter Haar writes, "de soenrang in Zuid-Celebes en de moslemse mas kawin" are "geldigheidsvereiste voor het huwelijk" (validity requirement for the marriage) — "al kan in Zuid-Celebes door het lenen van de soenrang (soenrang niinrang) de betaling fictief gemaakt worden" (although in South Celebes the payment can be made fictive by the lending of the *soenrang*).

The distinction is significant for the legal characterisation of the Atjehnese marriage. Where the *bruidschat* has the effect of transferring the woman and the children to the husband's kin-group — the "losmakende kracht" (loosening force) that detaches the woman from her kin-group and attaches her to the husband's — the *huwelijksgift* does not have this effect. The *djinamée* of Atjeh is thus, in [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]]'s account, a payment that validates the marriage without effecting the transfer of the woman; it is a payment *to the woman personally*, not *for the woman* to her kin-group.

## The djinamée and the mas kawin

The *djinamée* of Atjeh is noted by [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]] as having "samengesmolten" (coalesced) with the Muslim *mas kawin* — the small payment (often five guilders) of the man to the woman that is the validity requirement of the Muslim marriage. "De huwelijksgift, mas kawin (mahr) is een kleine betaling (veelal vijf gulden) van den man aan de vrouw," Ter Haar writes; "zij is soms samengesmolten met andere betalingen, op Java bijvoorbeeld met de toekon, op Zuid-Celebes met de soenrang, in Atjeh met de djinamée, en is dan ook een hoger bedrag." The coalescence of the *djinamée* with the *mas kawin* in Atjeh is thus an instance of the wider Indonesian pattern by which the Muslim marriage gift is absorbed into, and identified with, a pre-existing or co-existing indigenous marriage payment — a pattern that gives the combined payment a higher amount than the nominal Muslim *mas kawin* alone.

## The djinamée as personal property of the wife

The *djinamée*, as a marriage gift made to the woman personally, remains — in [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]]'s account — the personal property of the wife. "Waar onder de huwelijksbetalingen voorkomt een huwelijksgift aan de vrouw," Ter Haar writes, "zoals de djinamée in Atjeh, de hoko in de Minahasa en gelijk de soenrang in het grootste deel van Zuid-Celebes blijft dat goed of geld, evenals de mas kawin en de daarmee versmolten betalingen elders, aan de vrouw persoonlijk." The *djinamée* thus does not enter the *huwelijksboedel* (marital community of goods) but remains the wife's personal property — a rule that places the Atjehnese marriage gift within the wider Indonesian category of the *barang asal* (goods of origin), the goods that remain tied to the spouse's family of origin rather than entering the marital community.

## The return of a portion upon dissolution

Upon the dissolution of the marriage, [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]] notes, a portion of the *djinamée* is returned — a practice cited alongside similar practices in the Pasemah and Bali. "Het teruggeven van een gedeelte van het betaalde (bijvoorbeeld uit de Pasemah en Bali vermeld van een deel van den bruidschat, in Atjeh gebruikelijk bij de huwelijksgift, ook in de Minahasa en elders bekend)" — the return of a portion of the paid (for example, recorded in the Pasemah and Bali of a portion of the bride-price, in Atjeh customary for the marriage gift, also known in the Minahasa and elsewhere). The return of a portion of the *djinamée* is thus an Atjehnese instance of the wider Indonesian practice by which the return of a portion of the marriage payment upon dissolution maintains a certain, post-marital relation of the wife to her family of origin — a relation that is, in [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]]'s account, "hoe wezenlijker de bruidschat als zodanig begrepen wordt, hoe wezenlijker die functie uiteraard is; zij kan ook tot inhoudsloze traditie zijn geworden" (the more essentially the bride-price is understood as such, the more essential that function naturally is; it can also have become an empty tradition).

## Significance

For AcehWiki, the *djinamée* is significant as the Atjehnese marriage gift — the payment that validates the Atjehnese marriage and that has coalesced with the Muslim *mas kawin* — and as a key example in [[B. ter Haar BzN|Ter Haar]]'s systematic account of Indonesian marriage payments. The institution illustrates the interplay of adat and Islam in the Atjehnese marriage: the *djinamée* is an indigenous marriage gift that has absorbed the Muslim *mas kawin*, and it remains the wife's personal property, distinct from the marital community of goods. The institution complements the corpus's existing entries on the Atjehnese marriage — drawn from [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *[[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|De Atjehers]]* and from *[[De Buitenbezittingen|De Buitenbezittingen]]* — and it furnishes the doctrinal-legal characterisation of the Atjehnese marriage gift within the colonial legal science of the late Netherlands Indies.
