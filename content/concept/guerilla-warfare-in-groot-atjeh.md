**The guerilla war in Groot-Atjeh** was the characteristic form of warfare which the [[Atjeh]]nese enemy waged against the Dutch occupation army in [[Groot-Atjeh]] — replacing battle in the open field by surprise, ambush, night assault, murder of stragglers, cutting of communications, fire, flood and sleeplessness. It was described and analysed with a precision no earlier Dutch work had reached by [[Kolonel Meijer|H. F. Meijer]] in his *[[Atjeh van 26 December 1875 tot 4 September 1876]]* ([[1883]]), whose subtitle, *De offensieve handelingen der guerilla* (the offensive operations of the guerilla), was meant literally: the enemy, everywhere the Dutch believed themselves on the defensive, was in fact the offensive party.

## The enemy's method

Meijer's portrait of the enemy's method, spread through his chapters, includes:

- **Use of the terrain.** From the mountain tops the guerilla watched all daytime movements of the Dutch troops with his practised eye; he descended when a weak patrol or a badly guarded detachment offered itself, and lay *"as a tiger in ambush"* for his prey; the sawahs, kampong hedges, nipah bushes, pagars and ditches were his covered approaches, often to within ten paces of a post.
- **Surprise attacks by night.** Posts like Lemboeq N.O. ([[Attack on Lemboeq N.O. (23-24 January 1876)|January]]), Lamprit ([[Night attack on Lamprit (21-22 April 1876)|April]]), Lampagar ([[Night attack on Lampagar (2-3 May 1876)|May]]) and the west hill-post ([[Surprise of the west hill-post at Biloel Zuid (2-3 June 1876)|June]]) were assaulted after careful spying, usually a few hours before dawn, at the posts' weakest hour and point — sometimes with the help of information drawn from friendly kampongs.
- **Ambushes by day.** Patrols, escorts and convoys on the sunken footpaths between posts were his daily object: February ([[Atoeh catastrophe (13 February 1876)|13]] and [[Lamkoenjit ambush (17 February 1876)|17 February]]), March ([[Ambush of the Buijs detachment (4 March 1876)|4]] and [[Convoy ambush near Atoë (21 March 1876)|21 March]]), April ([[Koewala Pantjoer patrol ambush (7 April 1876)|7]] and [[Oleh Karang convoy ambush (16 April 1876)|16 April]]), June ([[Ambush between Oleh Karang and Kota Alam (18 June 1876)|18 June]]), July ([[Ambush between Pango and Kota Alam (14 July 1876)|14]], [[Ambush between Boekit Daroe and Ketapan Doewa (17 July 1876)|17]] and [[Ambush between Kota Alam and Oleh Karang (28 July 1876)|28 July]]) and August ([[Lo-oe ambush (28 August 1876)|28 August]]) each produced their characteristic ambush.
- **Weapons of circumstance.** Grass fire in the dry monsoon ([[Grass-fire attack on Lamprit (8 February 1876)|Lamprit, 8 February]]), the damming of channels to direct flood water over a work, and the continuous night firing that withheld the garrisons' sleep (*afmatting* — the methodical tiring-out) were part of the same warfare.
- **Local coalitions.** Repeatedly the resistance was helped by the *two-faced* neutrality of the submitted population, which kept up its trade with the enemy as by ancient exchange — the betel-leaf and rice trade through the Dutch [[Passenstelsel|passenstelsel]] (pass system), in which passes of friends covered the passages of enemies.

## The Dutch answer

The Dutch answer, as Meijer documents it, was never to try to break this war by a single battle — the guerilla never showed himself to a strong column — but by: careful march and escort discipline; the system of fortified posts (the *bentingstelsel*) with storm-proof palisades, obstacle belts of bamboo-thorn, *randjoes* and broken glass, and lighting by pole lanterns; the clearance of the terrain around the works; beaten roads and fixed convoy times; vivres depots for two months in every post, and counter-ambushes (*hinderlagen*) and night patrols. Meijer showed the price of their daily neglect, and the rarity of properly trained troops in [[1876]], when the greater part of the infantry were young recruits and the enemy always knew of every deficiency in advance.

## Significance

Meijer's book became the Indies army's own standard text on the guerilla war; the standing comparison between the Atjeh war and the French Vendée and the Calabrian guerilla war of the Napoleonic era — which he quotes from an eye-witness of the latter — served the same purpose: a warfare in which every hedge, bush and hut might be hostile.
