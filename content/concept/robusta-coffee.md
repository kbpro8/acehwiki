**Robusta Coffee (Aceh)** (Dutch: *Robusta-koffie*, Latin: *Coffea canephora*) was the coffee variety favoured by the European plantation enterprises of the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the early [[20th century|twentieth century]], cultivated on the lower-lying East Coast plains where the climate was too warm for the more delicate [[Arabica Coffee (Aceh)|arabica]]. Although of lower market quality than arabica, robusta proved substantially more disease-resistant and was therefore adopted as the principal coffee crop of the European estates established under the [[Agrarische Wet 1870]] regime of long-lease erfpacht concessions around [[Langsa|Langsa]] and the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]]. Robusta was typically planted as a "catch crop" (Dutch: *vanggewas*) in the early years of rubber estates to provide income while the slower-maturing *Hevea* trees were reaching productive age, and the crop became a steady, if secondary, contributor to the Atjeh plantation economy of the [[1910s]] and [[1920s]].

## The robusta variety and its advantages

Robusta coffee — *Coffea canephora* — was identified as a distinct species in the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]], after the coffee leaf disease (Dutch: *koffiebladziekte*) caused by *Hemileia vastatrix* had devastated the *Coffea arabica* plantations of [[Java|Java]], [[Ceylon|Ceylon]] and the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]] in the [[1870s]] and [[1880s]]. Robusta was found to be naturally resistant to *Hemileia* and to several other coffee pathogens, and although its bean was smaller, rounder and less aromatic than that of arabica, its vigour, yield and adaptability to lowland conditions made it an attractive commercial proposition.

The variety was introduced to the Atjeh East Coast in the late [[1890s]] and rapidly established itself as the preferred plantation coffee of the residency. It tolerated the warm, humid climate of the alluvial plain — where arabica would have succumbed to leaf disease within a year or two — and produced its first commercial crop at three to four years of age, two years earlier than arabica. The bean's high caffeine content (about 2.7 per cent versus 1.5 per cent for arabica) made it particularly suitable for the manufacture of instant coffee and espresso blends, although these markets did not develop until well into the twentieth century.

## Cultivation on the European estates

Plantations were established on the higher elevations of the East Coast interior, employing European capital and local labour. The principal robusta district was the under-subdivision of [[Langsa|Langsa]], where the [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber boom]] of the early twentieth century had assembled large contiguous blocks of leasehold land suitable for combined rubber-and-coffee cultivation. Robusta was typically planted as a "catch crop" between the rows of young *Hevea* seedlings, providing a steady income from the third year onward while the rubber trees — which would not begin to yield latex until their fourth or fifth year — were reaching maturity. As the rubber canopy closed, the coffee bushes were gradually shaded out and removed; the system thus combined short-term cash flow with long-term plantation development.

Cultivation was intensive and systematic: the bushes were planted in rows 2.5 to 3 metres apart, weeded by hand or by horse-drawn cultivator, and fertilised with green manure and occasional applications of guano. The ripe red cherries (Dutch: *koffiebessen*) were hand-picked by Javanese and Acehnese contract labourers (Dutch: *contractkoelies*) in the main harvest season, processed through the estate's wet-mill (Dutch: *koffiebereiding*) to remove the skin and pulp, fermented in water for 12 to 24 hours to loosen the mucilage, washed, sun-dried on paved drying-yards, and finally hulled (Dutch: *gepeld*) and sorted for export.

## Market position and colonial policy

Robusta fetched lower prices than [[Arabica Coffee (Aceh)|arabica]] but ensured a steady supply for local consumption and modest export. The bean was graded by size and defect count, and the best Atjeh robusta — known in the trade as "Easte Coast Robusta" or "Atjeh Robusta" — was sold principally to the Dutch coffee roasters of Amsterdam and Rotterdam, with smaller consignments going to the American and German markets. The bean was a staple ingredient of the cheaper European coffee blends and was also widely consumed locally as the *kopi* of the urban Atjeh population.

The European estates were the principal producers, but smallholder robusta cultivation also developed in the East Coast villages, where Acehnese farmers planted the bushes in their home gardens and sold the cherry to the nearest estate or to Chinese dealers. The colonial agricultural service encouraged this smallholder robusta through extension work and seed distribution, and the smallholder crop formed the basis of the post-independence Acehnese coffee economy that has since made Indonesia one of the world's leading robusta producers.

## Decline and legacy

By the [[1930s]] the great expansion of the Atjeh coffee economy had passed its peak: the depression had driven coffee prices sharply down, the catch-crop function of robusta had largely been fulfilled as the rubber estates matured, and the surviving coffee gardens were increasingly left untended. The Japanese occupation of [[1942]] and the subsequent Indonesian revolution disrupted the trade further, but the robusta gardens of the East Coast survived and were the foundation of the post-independence Acehnese coffee economy. The crop remains a major smallholder resource of lowland Aceh today, and the choice of robusta over arabica — driven by the leaf-disease constraint that shaped colonial plantation agriculture in the [[1900s]] — continues to define the Atjeh coffee landscape.
