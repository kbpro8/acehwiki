**Spinsbek** is a distinctive metalworking technique prominently featured in the gold and silver objects of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] catalogued in the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society's]] 1928 treasure-room guide. The term appears dozens of times across the three Atjeh cabinets (Kast 3, 4, and 5), applied to an extraordinary range of object types including rings, chains, clasps, boxes, lime containers, belt buckles, revolvers, and ornamental plaques.

## The term

The etymology and exact technical definition of *spinsbek* are not entirely clear from the available sources. The word appears to be a Dutch colonial-period term, possibly derived from a local (Acehnese or Malay) word or a descriptive coinage. The element *spins-* may relate to "spinning" or "spun," suggesting a technique involving the creation of tiny metal beads, granules, or wire elements, while *-bek* may refer to the beak-like or pointed form of the individual granules. An alternative interpretation connects the term to the Malay word *sinabek* (to tear, to separate), possibly referring to a technique of creating a rough or granulated surface texture.

## Technical characteristics

From the catalogue descriptions, *spinsbek* appears to have been a surface-decoration technique applied to objects of gold, silver, and copper. It is listed alongside other techniques such as filigree (*filigraan*), chasing (*gedreven*), and enamelling (*geëmailleerd*), suggesting it was a distinct method rather than a synonym for any of these. The fact that *spinsbek* is listed as a material in its own right — e.g., "ringen, zilver, koper en spinsbek" (rings, silver, copper and spinsbek) — implies that it was recognised as a specific kind of metalwork product or surface treatment, possibly sold or worked by specialist craftsmen.

## Prevalence in the Atjeh collection

The dominance of *spinsbek* in the Atjeh cabinets is striking. While filigree, chasing, and enamel appear in the collections of many regions, *spinsbek* is overwhelmingly concentrated in the Atjeh section, appearing only sporadically elsewhere in the catalogue. This suggests that *spinsbek* was a characteristically or even uniquely Acehnese technique, or at least one that was practised with particular intensity and skill in Atjeh.

Notable *spinsbek* objects from the Atjeh cabinets include:
- **Rings**: Multiple rings described simply as "spinsbek" or "spinsbek cantille-werk"
- **Ankle rings** (*enkelring*): Several pairs of *spinsbek* ankle rings
- **Revolver** with *spinsbek* decoration (Cabinet 5, items 47) — an unusual application of traditional metalwork to a modern firearm
- **Betel-lime boxes** (*kalkdoosje*): Several examples, including one hanging from thirteen united thin chains
- **Belt chains** with *spinsbek* screw clasps
- **Boxes** (*doosje*) of *spinsbek* for amulets and sirih lime
- **Ornamental plates** for belly bands (*buikband*)

## Significance

The widespread use of *spinsbek* across all categories of Acehnese metalwork — from the humblest ring to the most elaborate weapon fitting — suggests that it was a fundamental and ubiquitous element of the Acehnese gold- and silversmith's repertoire. Its near-absence from the collections of other regions in the Batavian Society catalogue further suggests that it was a distinctive marker of Acehnese identity in the decorative arts.
