**Adat** (from Arabic *ʿādah*, "custom") was the unwritten customary law of [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] — the body of indigenous legal tradition governing social, family, and community life. It coexisted with [[Hikum|Islamic law]] (*hukom*, from Arabic *ḥukm*), with each domain having its own guardians: the [[Keuchi'|keuchi']] and [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] for adat, and the [[Teungku|teungku]] for hukom.

## Nature of Acehnese Adat

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] emphasised that the Acehnese adat was genuinely indigenous and wholly suitable to the state of civilisation in which the Acehnese had moved as long as they had been known. He disputed the claim — made by some Dutch officials — that the Acehnese had once possessed an orderly past resting on the basis of Mohammedan law, with a disorderly present caused by a creeping in of adat. This comparison was "entirely chimerical and rests on false or inexactly stated data." The *Adat Meukuta Alam* — the legendary fundamental law of the kingdom — belonged, he argued, to the realms of legend.

The wholly unlettered [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade|Teuku Malikön Adé]] as supreme judge of the kingdom was cited as an example of the ignorance of the chiefs in regard to Mohammedan law — an ignorance shared with the rulers of most Mohammedan countries.

## Scope

Adat governed:
- **Blood feuds and vengeance** (*[[Bila|bila]]* — blood money)
- **Land rights and transactions** (sale of rice fields, sugarcane gardens, court yards)
- **Village governance** (the powers and duties of the [[Keuchi']])
- **Marriage, family, and inheritance** (covered in detail in Chapter III of *The Achehnese*)
- **Commercial and fishing customs** (pantang rules at sea)
- **Clan relationships** ([[Kawom|kawom]] solidarity and liability)

## Adat and Islam

The relationship between adat and Islam in Acheh was one of the central questions addressed by Snouck Hurgronje. While Acehnese political adventurers and teungkus sometimes sought to mobilise Islam against the existing order, the everyday adat was in most respects prior to and independent of Mohammedan law, with Islamic provisions overlaid in specific domains such as inheritance, marriage, and prayer.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Adat

**Adat** is the customary law of the [[Atjehers|Acehnese]] — and, more broadly, of the peoples of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] — that, alongside the *hoeköm* (Islamic law), governed the civil and criminal affairs of the native population and that the Dutch colonial administration, following the principle of *herstelling c.q. handhaving der eigenlijke volkshoofden, gebaseerd op de instellingen des lands* — restoration or maintenance of the genuine popular chiefs, based on the institutions of the land — undertook to preserve and to incorporate into the structure of indirect rule. *De Buitenbezittingen* treats *adat* as one of the two constitutive legal orders of Atjehsche society, alongside *hoeköm*, and traces its variations across the regions of the Gouvernement.

## Adat and hoeköm

The relationship of *adat* and *hoeköm* — customary law and Islamic law — is the foundational legal polarity of Atjehsche society. The [[Gampong|gampong]] administration embodies it: the [[Keutjhï|Keutjhï]], the "vader" (father) of the gampong, is the keeper of *adat*, while the [[Teungkoe|Teungkoe]], the "moeder" (mother), is the keeper of *hoeköm*. *De Buitenbezittingen*, following *C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje*'s *De Atjehers*, records that "hoewel adat en hoeköm onafscheidelijk van elkaar heeten" — although adat and hoeköm are called inseparable — "heeft de eerste toch steeds de overhand" — the first, however, always prevails. The same polarity is replicated at the level of the [[Moekim|moekim]], where the *imeum* — originally a religious functionary — became in time an *adat* chief, the *oelèëbalang tjoet* (small oelèëbalang).

## Adat in the colonial legal system

The colonial legal system of the Gouvernement, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, preserved the *voorrecht der eigen rechtspleging* — the privilege of native jurisdiction — for "verreweg het grootste deel der inlandsche bevolking" (by far the greater part of the native population), by virtue of *St. 1881 No. 83* and the *akten van verband* (acts of binding) of the *landschapshoofden*. The native courts — the *moesapat* (in the Onderhoorigheden, the *rapat* in Singkel) — followed, "zooveel mogelijk de adat," *modified and softened according to our legal concepts* in many cases, since the Dutch penal code, police regulations, and other ordinances served as the "richtsnoer" (guideline). *Godsoordeelen* (ordeal) and *mutileerende straffen* (mutilating punishments) were wholly forbidden. In civil cases — inheritances, estates, land disputes, sawahs, plantations — the *adat* was followed "zuiver" (purely).

## Regional variations

The *adat* varied considerably across the regions of the Gouvernement. The Groot-Atjehsche *adat*, described in detail by Snouck Hurgronje, was the most fully developed; it was found "vrijwel ongeschonden" (virtually intact) in [[Pedir|Pedir]] and, in modified form, in the Atjehsche parts of the Westkust. The Menangkabau *adat* — strongly modified but still recognisable — prevailed in the Malay colonies of the Westkust south of [[Meulabôh|Meulabôh]], with its *soekoe* (clan) organisation and its *kamanakan* (matrilineal) inheritance. The *adat kamanakan* of Tapai Toean, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, was "opgeheven in 1913 op verzoek van de hoofden der Maleische bevolking" — abolished in 1913 at the request of the chiefs of the Malay population — by the then Governor. The Gayo and Alas *adats* — based on genealogical *kawöm* and *méréga* (clan) organisation — were again distinct, as were the *adats* of Tamiang, which preserved the *Datoeq Ampat Soekoe* council of Radja Mengkoeta.
