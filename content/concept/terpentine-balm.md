**Terpentine Balm (Aceh)** (Dutch: *terpentijn-balsem*) was the resinous balsam obtained by tapping the Sumatra pine (Dutch: *Sumatra-den*, *Pinus merkusii*) of the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the early [[20th century|twentieth century]], and was the source of two distinct commercial products: terpentine oil (Dutch: *terpentijnolie*), distilled from the raw resin and used in paints and solvents, and the residual solid balsam (Dutch: *balsem* or *colofonium*), sold as flat cakes for use in the batik industry and paper manufacture. The tapping of the Sumatra pine was one of the small-scale forest-based industries of the Atjeh interior, and the trade complemented the broader resin and forest-product economy of [[Resin (Aceh)|damar]], [[Bakau Bast|bakau bast]] and [[Kapok Fiber|kapok]].

## The Sumatra pine and its range

The Sumatra pine — *Pinus merkusii*, Dutch: *Sumatra-den* — is the only pine species native to the Southern Hemisphere and the only one indigenous to the Indonesian archipelago. Its natural range in the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] extended from the [[Gajoland|Gayo uplands]] of northern Sumatra through the Tapanoeli highlands to the Kerintji and Barisan mountains of central Sumatra, with a separate population in the northern Philippines. The species thrives on the well-drained volcanic soils of the highland slopes at altitudes of 900 to 1,500 metres, where the cool climate and well-marked dry season favour its growth, and the natural stands of the Gayo uplands around [[Takèngon|Takèngön]] formed the principal Atjeh resource for the terpentine-balsam trade.

The tree was first identified and described by the German-Dutch botanist Junghuhn in the [[1840s]], and was subsequently recognised as a distinct species of considerable economic potential for its resin yield. The colonial forest service (Dutch: *boschwezen*) began experimental tapping of the Sumatra pine in the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]] and developed the systematic exploitation of the resource in the early twentieth century, both in the natural stands of the Gayo uplands and in the experimental pine plantations (Dutch: *dennenplantages*) established on degraded grasslands at various highland sites in Sumatra.

## Tapping and distillation

The production of terpentine balsam followed a three-stage process:

1. **Tapping** (Dutch: *tappen*): The bark of the pine was stripped in a series of horizontal V-shaped cuts (Dutch: *tapsneden*) about 5 to 8 centimetres wide and 1 to 2 centimetres deep, descending the trunk in successive years. A galvanised-iron gutter (Dutch: *tapgoot*) and a small earthenware cup (Dutch: *tapbakje*) were fixed below each cut, and the milky resin (Dutch: *gom* or *hars*) that exuded from the wound was collected weekly during the tapping season, which extended through the cooler months of the year. A mature tree could yield 2 to 4 kilograms of crude resin per season, and a tree could be tapped for 15 to 20 years before the panel reached the base of the trunk.
2. **Distillation** (Dutch: *destillatie*): The collected resin was conveyed to the distillery (Dutch: *destilleerderij*) and heated in a copper still (Dutch: *koperen distilleerketel*) with a small quantity of water. The volatile terpentine oil (Dutch: *terpentijnolie*) vaporised at about 156 degrees Celsius, was condensed in a coiled condenser (Dutch: *koeler*), and was collected as a clear, colourless, highly flammable liquid with the characteristic sharp aroma of turpentine. The yield of terpentine oil was typically 15 to 20 per cent of the crude resin by weight.
3. **Solid balsam** (Dutch: *balsem* or *colofonium*): The residual solid left after distillation was a hard, pale, glassy resin — rosin or colophony (Dutch: *colofonium*) — which was poured into flat moulds (Dutch: *vormen*) and cooled into flat cakes (Dutch: *platte koeken*) of approximately 1 to 2 kilograms each, ready for sale.

## Uses and economic role

The two products of the Sumatra pine served distinct industrial markets:

- **Terpentine oil** (Dutch: *terpentijnolie*): used principally as a solvent in the manufacture of paints, varnishes, and lacquers, and as a raw material for the synthesis of camphor, synthetic perfumes, and a range of pharmaceutical preparations. The oil was exported in tinned drums to the paint and chemical factories of the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and [[Java|Batavia]].
- **Balsam cakes** (Dutch: *balsem-koeken*): used in the batik industry (Dutch: *batik-industrie*) of Java, where the rosin was dissolved and applied to the textile to resist the dye in the batik wax-resist process; and in paper manufacture (Dutch: *papierfabriekatie*), where the rosin was added to the pulp to provide the "size" that gives writing paper its resistance to ink-bleed. The balsam cakes were exported principally to the Javanese batik centres of Yogyakarta and Surakarta and to the European paper mills.

The trade was small in absolute terms but locally important, providing cash income to the forest-edge villages of the Gayo uplands and employing a small workforce of tappers and distillery operators at each processing site. The [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] and the [[Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen|Atjeh tram]] made it easier to move the packaged oil and balsam cakes from the highlands to the export ports of [[Belawan]] and [[Sabang]].

## Decline and legacy

By the [[1930s]] the Atjeh terpentine-balsam trade had contracted under the combined pressure of the depression, competition from cheaper American and French turpentine, and the gradual substitution of synthetic solvents for natural terpentine oil in the European paint industry. The surviving Gayo pine stands continued to be tapped on a reduced scale through the [[1930s]], and the trade limped through the Japanese occupation of [[1942]] and the Indonesian revolution before being absorbed into the state forest corporation of the post-independence period. The Sumatra pine itself has since been widely planted as a plantation timber species across the Indonesian highlands, and the surviving natural stands of the Gayo uplands remain a protected forest resource.
