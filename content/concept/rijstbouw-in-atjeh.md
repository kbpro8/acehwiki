**Rijstbouw in Atjeh** (Rice cultivation in Atjeh) is the system of wet-rice and dry-rice agriculture practised across the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* — the colonial administrative unit comprising [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], the [[Noordkust van Atjeh|Noordkust]], the [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust]], the [[Alasland|Alasland]] and the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust]] — as documented in detail by [[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]] in his [[Kreemer's De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden (1918)|*De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* (1918)]]. The Atjeh rice-economy is the indispensable baseline of Atjehnese subsistence and of the early-twentieth-century colonial policy debate on the [[Rijstvraagstuk (1916)|"Rice Question" of 1916]].

## The Atjehnese Sayings on Rice

[[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]] opens his 1918 monograph with two Atjehnese sayings that frame the place of rice in Atjehnese life:

- *Bèreën that nyan keuh meujân broë-pulôh* — Agriculture is the master of all livelihoods. This saying, Kreemer notes, applies equally to the Atjehnese, the Gayo and the Alas peoples of the interior.
- *Kaja meunèh hana meusampé, kaja padé meusampôeuna* — Money alone is not sufficient, but where there is abundance of rice, nothing is lacking.

These sayings express the central place of rice cultivation in the Atjehnese conception of well-being: rice is not merely a subsistence crop but the precondition of any other prosperity.

## The Typology of Rice Fields

The Atjeh rice-economy rests on four principal types of rice field, each with its own Acehnese, Gayo and Alas terminology:

### Sawah (Irrigated and Rain-Dependent Wet Fields)

The [[sawah|sawah]] — the wet rice field — is the principal type of Atjeh rice field. Kreemer distinguishes three sub-types:

- **Kunstmatig bevloeide velden** (artificially irrigated fields) — *oemòng ie peunieuëh* in Acehnese; *oemo mòc-woih* in Gayo; *djoemo mèlawe* in Alas. These fields are irrigated by water led from rivers and streams through dug channels (*leueng*) or through bamboo-and-wooden flumes (*peueucò* or *palòng*).
- **Van regen afhankelijke velden** (rain-dependent fields) — *oemòng preh ie oedjèn*. These fields depend solely on rainfall, retained on the field by the small enclosing dikes (*ateuëng*). They are the most common type in regions without irrigation, especially in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]].
- **Moerassawah's** (swamp-rice fields) — *oemòng paja*; *oemo paja* or *oemo rölöm*; *djoema rawang*. These are fields in swampy low-lying land, where the water is too deep for ordinary sawah cultivation.

### Ladang (Dry Upland Fields)

The *rijstladang* — the dry upland rice field — is the secondary type of Atjeh rice field. *Ladang* cultivation is practised in regions where the terrain does not permit sawah cultivation, especially in the upland areas of the interior and in the Témiang region of the [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust]]. Kreemer excludes *ladang* cultivation from the detailed treatment of his 1918 monograph, which is concerned principally with sawah cultivation.

### Abandoned Fields (Pòk Ròh)

A *pòk ròh* — an abandoned sawah, formerly cultivated but long uncultivated — is a common feature of the Atjeh landscape, especially in the lowlands of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. Kreemer notes that many such abandoned fields are found on the [[Blang Bintang|Blang Bintang]] in the [[Sagi der XXVI Moekims|Sagi der XXVI Moekims]], where they are often given out for three-year cultivation without counter-performance — a practice called *padjól asé*.

### Terraced Sawahs

[[Takengon|Terraced sawahs]] — *oemòng teurata* — are found on the river terraces of the interior, where the rivers of the [[Bukit Barisan|Bukit Barisan]] mountains have cut broad valleys with stepped terraces. The Atjehnese sometimes convert these terraces into sawahs.

## The Annual Cycle of Cultivation

The Atjeh rice year follows a cycle of fourteen named stages, from the making of the seedbed to the ripening of the grain. The cycle is organised around the two principal sowing methods: the *poela* method (transplanting from a seedbed) and the *taboe* method (direct broadcasting on the field).

### The Two Sowing Methods

The *poela* method is the transplanting method: the seed is first sown on a seedbed (*teubò seuneulòng*, or *nendòe* on the Oostkust; *pénjémen* in Gayo; *pénămén* in Alas), and the seedlings are transplanted to the field after about 44 days. The *taboe* method is the direct-broadcasting method: the seed is sown directly on the field, without a seedbed. Kreemer notes that the *taboe* method is generally preferred in regions of unreliable rainfall (such as Groot-Atjeh), because it avoids the dependence on wet conditions at the transplanting moment; while the *poela* method is generally preferred in regions of reliable rainfall (such as the irrigated Pidie valley), because it produces a more regular stand and reduces the need for weeding.

### The Thirteen Ripening Stages

Kreemer records thirteen named stages of rice ripening, from the first development of new leaves to the full hardening of the grain:

1. *manja'* — new young leaves develop; plants about 14 days on the field.
2. *dara* (*doro* in Gayo, *daro* in Alas) — leaves begin to tiller; the yellow young plants take on the green colour.
3. *meu cuntéën* — rings of hair appear on the stem at the points where the leaves branch.
4. *dòelat bae* — the previously flat stem takes on a round form.
5. *òe* or *boenténg* — the blossom sets.
6. *siròk* (*sròh* or *ampar* in Gayo, *ampar* in Alas) — the blossom emerges from the top of the plant.
7. *meu ie tjòt* — the grains become milk-ripe.
8. *meu ikòë mandè* — the ears bend like the hackle of a cock; also called *teukneë*.
9. *meu asèe* — the grain-setting takes place.
10. *bòeleuën peungeuh* — the rice is not yet hard but turns yellow (like the full moon).
11. *kòenèng* — the rice is gold-yellow.
12. *òelèe mandè* — the grains become hard.
13. *masak* — the grains are fully hard; the rice is now ready for harvest.

If the harvest is delayed beyond the *masak* stage, the rice becomes *reuntah* — that is, the grains fall off at cutting.

## Rice Varieties

Kreemer records the named varieties of rice grown in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], grouped into three classes:

### Late-ripening varieties (*padé rajeu'*)

These require 6 to 12 months to ripen, and because they can tolerate abundant water they are planted in swamps. Varieties include: *padé keudah*, *padé keudah gloempang*, *padé keudah mandé*, *padé poetéh rajeu'*, *padé reunti paja*, *padé radén*, *padé lansajé*, *padé poetrë banan*, *padé bàh sàmboh*, *padé peunjaman*.

### Early-ripening varieties (*padé cut*)

These ripen in about 3 months. Varieties include: *padé aka*, *padé poetéh cut*, *padé seudoët*.

### Glutinous varieties (*leukat*)

These also ripen in about 3 months. Varieties include: *leukat adang*, *leukat djeuët*, *leukat bring*, *leukat oendé*, *leukat dadéh*, *leukat lidah kendéui*, *leukat boengong seulimèng*, *leukat bin*, *leukat koemoenjan*.

The Atjehnese planter, Kreemer notes, habitually plants several varieties (early- and late-ripening) on the same field, to hedge against the uncertain rainfall. If he planted only one late-ripening variety and the rain failed at the moment of transplanting, the seedlings would be lost.

## Labour Systems

The Atjeh rice crop is grown under five principal labour arrangements:

- **Own cultivation** — *kòh keudòe* — the owner of the field works it himself.
- **Wage-labour** — *kòh peung-oepah* — the field is worked for a wage, with an advance (*tjaram* or *tjinggram*) paid before the harvest.
- **Sharecropping** — *mawaih* (Gayo *mawah*, Alas *blah dudé* or *boeloeing dudé*) — the sharecropper works the field for an indefinite period in exchange for half the harvest. The field-owner provides the seed and pays half the threshing cost; other costs (buffalo hire, etc.) fall on the sharecropper. Variants include *bagi lhèë* (one-third to the owner), *bagi peuet* (one-quarter to the owner — common on the [[Blang Bintang|Blang Bintang]] in the [[Sagi der XXVI Moekims|Sagi der XXVI Moekims]], where much abandoned sawah is available).
- **Voluntary collective labour** — *toempoh* or *bantoe* (Gayo *timpoh* or *bantoe*; collective *meuseuraja*, Gayo *djamoe tëë*, Alas *nërajo*) — many persons work together out of free choice, typically repaid with rice, sugarcane and other refreshments.
- **Reciprocal labour** — *taelòng-moclòng* or *meugantché* or *meunoerdé* (Gayo *panggèlò*, Alas *plingari*) — labour is exchanged with the obligation of return.

## Measurement and Taxation

### The Volume Measures

The Atjeh rice-economy uses a hierarchy of volume measures, of which the principal are:

- **The *kating*** — a basket, used for measuring the harvest. A *kating* may contain 20 *gantang* (= 40 *arè*) or 10 *gantang* (= 20 *arè*).
- **The *tòng*** — a box, typically of 1 *naléh* (= 8 *gantang* = 16 *arè*) or 1½ *naléh*.
- **The *naléh*** — the standard small measure, of 8 *gantang* = 16 *arè*.
- **The *goentja*** — the standard large measure, of 10 *naléh* = 160 *arè* in Groot-Atjeh. In Gayo, *koentjo's* of 10, 20 and 30 *naléh* are distinguished; in the Alasland, a *koentjo* = 4 *naléh*.
- **The *lèngkar*** — an Alas cylindrical measure, of 4 *naléh* = 44 *säloep*.

### The Djakeuet (Rice Tithe)

The [[djakeuet|djakeuet]] is the Islamic rice tithe (*zakat al-fitr*) levied on the rice harvest. The minimum harvest on which the djakeuet is due is, in Groot-Atjeh, 7½ *goentja* (= 75 *naléh* = 1200 *arè*); on the Oostkust, 6 *goentja*; in Pidie, differently again. The rate is 1/10 of the harvest — that is, one *naléh* on every *goentja* harvested. In Gayo the minimum is 3 *koentjo* for the Meergebied and 4½ *koentjo* elsewhere, with the rate 1/10 — that is, one *gantang* on every *koentjo*. In the Alasland the minimum is 10 *parah* and the rate 1/10 (1 *parah* = 1 *koentjo*). Kreemer notes, however, that this tithe is "volstrekt niet regelmatig opgebracht" — by no means regularly paid — and is in practice left to the generosity of the payer.

## The Regional Survey

Kreemer walks systematically through each of the five major divisions of the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* and gives the area under rice, the type of cultivation, the average yield, and the local consumption-and-export balance:

- [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] — ~20,000 ha cultivated, mostly rain-dependent, average yield 8 *pikoel*/ha.
- [[Pidie|Pidie]] (Noordkust) — ~16,000 ha irrigated in the Pidie valley, "the rice-granary of Atjeh"; average yield 36 *pikoel*/ha.
- [[Meureudu|Meureudu]] — ~3,000 ha, partly irrigated; average yield 30 *pikoel*/ha.
- [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] — irrigated around Samalanga, II Moekim Toenòng and Keudé Peudada; rain-dependent eastward to the Peusangan delta.
- [[Lho Seumawè|Lho Seumawè]] — almost all rain-dependent.
- [[Lho Soekon|Lho Soekon]] — about three-quarters rain-dependent; ~30,000 ha still to be opened; average yield 24 *pikoel*/ha.
- [[Takengon|Takengon]] — only living-water irrigated fields, around the [[Laut Tawar|Tawar-meer]] and the [[Pegasing|Pegasing]] valley.
- [[Peureula|Peureula]] (Oostkust) — ~6,000 ha in cultivation and ~6,000 ha more suitable; *ladang* cultivation important in Témiang.
- [[Blang Kedjérén|Blang Kedjérén]] — usually a surplus in good years.
- [[Lokop|Lokop]] — produces only about three-quarters of local consumption.
- [[Alasland|Alasland]] — only living-water fields; surplus exported to Singkel and the Karo highland.
- [[Simaloer|Simaloer]] — almost all rain-dependent, regular rice imports (e.g. 1,394,285 kg in [[1915]]).
- [[Singkel|Singkel]] — Benèeten-Singkelsche has some fields but suffers flooding; Simpang Kanan better; Simpang Kiri worse; Troemon largely self-sufficient.
- [[Tapa Toean|Tapa Toean]] — Laboh Hadji the best rice land; then Meuké and Manggéng; Kloeët often short.
- [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]] — much *ladang* cultivation in the upper regions; much swamp-sawah in the lowlands.
- [[Tjalang|Tjalang]] — mostly *ladang* and swamp rice; the Daja landschappen a favourable exception.
- [[Daja|Daja]] — the historic rice-storehouse of the Westkust.
- [[Panté Tjeureumèn|Panté Tjeureumèn]] — the only landschap on the Westkust with artificial irrigation.

## The Rice Question (Rijstvraagstuk)

The closing section of Kreemer's 1918 monograph addresses the contemporary policy question of the [[Rijstvraagstuk (1916)|"Rice Question" of 1916]]: could Atjeh, with improved irrigation, become the rice-storehouse of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra's East Coast]] plantation belt? Kreemer cites the calculation of [[J. H. Heyl|Landbouwadviseur J. H. Heyl]] that of the 1.6 million *pikoel* of rice required by Deli, "nearly half" could come from Atjeh, more specifically from [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], [[Pidie|Pidie]], [[Meureudu|Meureudu]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]], [[Lho Soekon|Lho Soekon]] and [[Peureula|Peureula]].

Kreemer notes the paradox that, despite producing enough rice for the indigenous population, Atjeh imported roughly 200,000 *pikoel* of rice annually — chiefly *Rangoon-rijst* imported by the [[Atjehsche Handelmaatschappij|Atjehsche Handelmaatschappij]] and a few European firms — because Atjeh-grown rice was not preferred by the non-indigenous population (the plantation workers of [[Langsa|Langsa]], the timber-workers of [[Sinabang|Sinabang]], the naval-coal station at [[Sabang|Sabang]], and the military garrison, which alone consumed ~14,000 *pikoel* of foreign rice per year).

## Significance

The *Rijstbouw in Atjeh* is the indispensable baseline of the Atjehnese subsistence economy and of the early-twentieth-century colonial policy debate on the [[Rijstvraagstuk (1916)|"Rice Question" of 1916]]. The detailed technical-and-ethnographic documentation of the Atjeh rice-economy in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s [[Kreemer's De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden (1918)|1918 monograph]] preserves the dense Acehnese-Gayo-Alas agricultural vocabulary of highland Sumatra rice cultivation at the close of the long nineteenth century, and the systematic regional survey of the rice-economy of each of the five major divisions of the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* is the indispensable quantitative baseline for any historical study of the Atjeh rice-economy in the colonial period.
