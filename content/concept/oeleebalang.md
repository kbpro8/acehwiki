**The Oeleebalang** — also written *Uleëbalang*, *Uleebalang* — was the Atjèhse territorial chief or district head, the principal Atjèhse administrative institution of the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Atjèh] and of the post-Dutch-conquest Atjèhse administration. The *oeleebalang* was the chief of a *mukim* (the Atjèhse territorial unit) or of a group of *mukim*s, and exercised in his territory the principal administrative, judicial, and fiscal functions. The institution was, with the Sultan and the religious leaders (*teungku*s), one of the three principal Atjèhse political institutions, and its relative power — vis-à-vis the Sultan — was, in the Snouck Hurgronje diagnosis, one of the principal causes of the decline of the Sultanate.

## The Office

The *oeleebalang* was the chief of a *mukim* (the Atjèhse territorial unit) or of a group of *mukim*s, and exercised in his territory the principal administrative, judicial, and fiscal functions. The office was hereditary in the *oeleebalang* family, but the confirmation of the succession was, in the pre-Dutch period, in the gift of the Sultan; in the post-Dutch-conquest period, it was in the gift of the Dutch administration. The *oeleebalang* was responsible for the collection of the taxes, the maintenance of the peace, the administration of justice, and — in the pre-Dutch period — the construction and maintenance of the irrigation works.

## The Pre-Dutch Irrigation Functions

In the pre-Dutch period, the construction of irrigation works was, in the Atjeh-Instituut brochure's account, done "*in betaalden of onbetaalden gedwongen arbeid der bevolking, al naar gelang een oeléébalang over voldoende geldmiddelen kon beschikken of niet, om in dit laatste geval een bron van inkomsten te verkrijgen.*" (in paid or unpaid forced labour of the population, according as an oeleebalang had sufficient money means or not, to obtain in this latter case a source of income.) When users made use of the water from such a *loeëng* (irrigation channel), they had to — according as they had sown — annually after the harvest pay the same sown quantity of padi in kind to the constructor of the irrigation work. The execution of irrigation works, also in paid labour, was for the *oeleebalang*s an important source of income.

## The Decline of the Sultanate

It is also known, the Atjeh-Instituut brochure notes, how the Atjèhse Sultanate — bearer of the wealth built on the [[pepercultuur|pepercultuur]] — gradually declined in significance, "*doordat de volkshoofden steeds grooter macht ontwikkelden en verschillende onderhoorigheden zich losscheurden.*" (because the popular chiefs developed ever greater power and various dependencies tore themselves loose.) The *oeleebalang*s were the principal "popular chiefs" whose growing power, vis-à-vis the Sultan, was the principal cause of the decline of the Sultanate. The Snouck Hurgronje diagnosis of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]] would, in the [[Verslag omtrent de religieus-politieke toestanden in Atjeh|Verslag]] of [[23 May]] [[23 May]] [[1892]], take account of the relative power of the *oeleebalang* in the prescription of the three principal rules (Keumala to be left alone; the actively hostile party — the bands organised by oelama's and adventurers — to be beaten; trust to be restored by promoting agriculture, industry, and trade).

## The Keudjroen-loeëng

The *oeleebalang* was, in the pre-Dutch period, the appointing authority for the *[[Keudjroen-loeeng|keudjroen-loeëng]]* — the Atjèhse irrigation official charged with the supervision of the irrigation water distribution. The revival of the office of *keudjroen-loeëng* in the post-war period — recommended by the Atjeh-Instituut brochure of [[1923]] — was, in Pidië, the rice-land par excellence, to be done by the appointment of younger brothers or close relatives of landscape-heads (i.e. of *oeleebalang*s) to the office.

## Significance

The *oeleebalang* is the principal Atjèhse territorial chief or district head, and the principal Atjèhse administrative institution of the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Atjèh] and of the post-Dutch-conquest Atjèhse administration. The office — hereditary in the *oeleebalang* family, with confirmation in the gift of the Sultan (pre-Dutch) or of the Dutch administration (post-Dutch) — was the principal vehicle of Atjèhse local administration, and the *oeleebalang*'s growing power, vis-à-vis the Sultan, was the principal cause of the decline of the Sultanate.
