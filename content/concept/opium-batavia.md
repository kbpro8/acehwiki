**Batavia Opium Company** (Dutch: *'s Lands-opiumbedrijf te Batavia*) was the state-run monopoly that packaged prepared opium (Dutch: *bereide opium*) in standardised tubes (Dutch: *tubes*) and distributed it to licensed opium-holders (Dutch: *opium-houders*) throughout the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]]. Operating from central factories in Batavia (present-day Jakarta), the Company was the production-and-packaging arm of the colonial opium regie and the successor to the older, much-criticised *pacht* system of private opium farms. Its operations supplied every residency of the Indies, including [[Atjeh|Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]], where the [[Opiumregie in Atjeh|Atjeh regie]] began receiving its tubes from Batavia on [[1 April]] [[1913]].

## Background and establishment

For centuries the Dutch colonial government had farmed out the right to sell opium at retail to private entrepreneurs — predominantly Chinese — in the *pacht* system. The pachters bought raw opium (Dutch: *ruwe opium*) from the government, prepared it in their own establishments with their own secret recipes, and sold it to consumers. The system incentivised the farmers to maximise consumption through aggressive marketing and adulteration, and produced widespread addiction, smuggling, and corruption among colonial officials.

Reform pressure mounted in the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]], driven both by the moral criticisms of the anti-opium movement in the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and by the practical difficulty of policing the pacht in the partially-pacified outer territories. A government-run system, the *regie*, was introduced on [[Madoera|Madura]] as a trial in [[1893]] and was then gradually rolled out across the Buitengewesten: Lombok in [[1897]], the Lampongsche Districten in [[1902]], Sumatra's Westkust, Tapanoeli, Benkoelen and Ternate in [[1905]], Palembang, Djambi and Banka in [[1906]], and so on through to Atjeh in [[1913]]. The establishment of the *'s Lands-opiumbedrijf te Batavia* as the central production facility was the indispensable precondition of the regie: only by controlling production could the government guarantee the standardised product that the regie's licensed distributors would sell.

## Production and packaging

Raw opium was imported from British India and — after the collapse of the Levant trade — from Persia and Yugoslavia, and was landed at the customs warehouses of Batavia. From there it was conveyed to the *opiumbereiding* — the central preparation works of the *'s Lands-opiumbedrijf* — where it was boiled, refined, blended with water and a small quantity of starch and spices to a uniform consistency, and tested for morphine content (Dutch: *morfine-gehalte*). The resulting prepared opium (Dutch: *bereide opium*) was a dark, sticky paste of standardised strength, quite different from the variable and often adulterated product of the old pacht trade.

The prepared opium was then weighed out into standardised lead-foil tubes (Dutch: *tubes*) of approximately one tahil (about 37.8 grams) each, sealed, stamped with the government seal (Dutch: *rijkswapen*), and packed into wooden chests for shipment. Each chest carried a serial number and a manifest that allowed the colonial audit office (Dutch: *algemeene rekenkamer*) to trace every tube from the Batavia factory to the licensed opium-holder who finally sold it.

## Distribution network

The packed tubes were shipped from Batavia to every residency of the Indies, including [[Atjeh|Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]] after the introduction of the [[Opiumregie in Atjeh|Atjeh regie]] on [[1 April]] [[1913]]. At the residency level the tubes were received by the resident's opium office (Dutch: *opiumkantoor*) and distributed to the licensed opium-holders — typically Chinese shopkeepers — who sold them on to registered consumers under tight governmental control. The licensed holders were required to keep detailed ledgers of every sale and were subject to unannounced inspections by government *controleurs*.

Strict regulations (Dutch: *streng bepalingen*) were enforced at every stage to limit opium-related danger and to curb smuggling. The standardised packaging, the uniform morphine content, and the serial-number traceability of every tube made it far more difficult for illicit dealers to pass off contraband as government opium — a major advance over the *pacht* era, when the variable quality of pachter-prepared opium had provided easy cover for smugglers.

## Regulatory context and significance

The Company enforced restrictive provisions to mitigate the public health impact of opium, reflecting broader colonial attempts to manage narcotics. The regie system of which it was the productive core was in this sense a compromise: by centralising production and distribution it eliminated the worst abuses of the pacht — adulteration, over-supply, sales to minors — while continuing to draw substantial fiscal revenue from a trade that the colonial state was unwilling to suppress outright. For [[1913]] the total revenue from the opium regie across the Indies reached 10.729 million guilders, one of the largest single revenue lines of the colonial budget.

The *'s Lands-opiumbedrijf te Batavia* continued in operation through the [[1920s]] and [[1930s]], gradually reducing the number of sales points and raising the retail price as part of a deliberate policy of suppressing demand. The Japanese occupation of [[1942]] interrupted the trade, and the post-independence Indonesian government formally abolished the opium regie in 1949, ending a centuries-long chapter in the fiscal and moral history of the Netherlands Indies.
