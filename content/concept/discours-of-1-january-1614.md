**The Discours of 1 January 1614** — the long memorandum addressed by **[[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Jan Pieterszoon Coen]]**, in his capacity as director-general of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]]'s Bantam-Jacatra factories, to the [[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]] in Amsterdam on [[1 January]] [[1614]] — is the foundational text of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force, the working-out of the bewindhebbers' secret instructions of [[11 April]] [[1608]] and [[4 May]] [[1609]], and the proximate intellectual cause of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]]'s coercive monopoly policy at [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] and elsewhere in the Indies. The Discours is the principal document of Van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]] — the title of the book refers to the body to which the Discours was addressed.

## The institutional context

[[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] had been appointed director-general of the Bantam-Jacatra factories in [[1613]] — the post in which all the threads of the Company's Asian business converged. The [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]]'s [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] expedition of March [[1613]] — in which Both was forced to restore discipline among the troops with drastic sentences and to confront the growing Dutch-English rivalry at Ai and Run — was the immediate strategic context. The Discours was [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s response, written on [[1 January]] [[1614]] and addressed to the [[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]] in Amsterdam. Van der Woude notes that [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s appointment was the institutional foundation of the Discours: "voor het eerst liepen daar alle draden uit Indië en patria in diens handen samen en onder zijn toezicht werden alle gegevens in cijfers in het grootboek uitgedrukt".

## The argument

The Discours's argument was the working-out of the bewindhebbers' secret instructions of [[11 April]] [[1608]] and [[4 May]] [[1609]]. The central complaint was that the bewindhebbers were sending "slechte, onkloeckmoedige menschen" to staff the Indies, and that no amount of commercial skill could compensate for the absence of "deuchtsaem van leven, cloeckmoedich ende groot van verstande". The Discours explicitly cited Atjeh and Banda as the two theatres in which the policy of free competition had demonstrably failed: at Banda "moet 't ondergebracht worden ende moeten aldaer in 't velt domineren", and at Atjeh the sultan's balancing policy had kept Dutch pepper prices volatile and the factory precarious.

The Discours's most-quoted passage is the Banda prescription: "Van Banda connen ons meester maken, dezelvige eylanden met macht aantasten; sulcx moeten nootelijcken doen ende de plaetse populeren, ofte het landt verlaten, want 't en sal anders met Banda nyet zijn". The "plaetse populeren" — populate the place with imported settlers — was the policy that [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] would execute four years later, after his conquest of Banda in [[1621]]: the depopulation of the Bandanese, the importation of Dutch settlers and slaves, and the establishment of the *perkenier* system of nutmeg plantations.

## The argument for "Indië onafhankelijk van Holland"

The Discours also addressed the wider strategic question of the Company's relationship to the Dutch state. [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s argument — anticipated by [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]]'s *Discours* of [[12 November]] [[1608]] — was that "Indië onafhankelijk van Holland" must be — that the Asian trade must generate its own capital, run its own books, and feed its own reserve fund, free from the quarterly pressures of Amsterdam shareholders. The argument was the administrative counterpart of the strategic argument: the Company in Asia could not be run as a branch of an Amsterdam export house. Van der Woude treats this as the institutional foundation of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme: the Indies administration must be free from Amsterdam's quarterly pressures, the Asian trade must generate its own capital, and the monopoly-by-force policy must be executed without interference from patria.

## The legacy

The Discours's argument would define VOC policy for the next quarter-century. [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s conquest of Banda in [[1621]], his founding of [[Batavia|Batavia]] in [[1619]], and his hardline policy against the English at [[Ambon|Ambon]] (culminating in the [[Ambonese murder|Ambonese murder of 1623]]) were the working-out of the Discours's prescriptions. The Discours's argument for "Indië onafhankelijk van Holland" would also shape the institutional development of the VOC: the dual bookkeeping (the *grootboek* and the *rekeningen-courant* of the Indies versus the *retouren-generaal* and *equipage-generaal* of patria) was the institutional expression of the Discours's argument, though Van der Woude treats it as the principal institutional failure of the VOC.

## Significance

The Discours of [[1 January]] [[1614]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the foundational text of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force and the proximate intellectual cause of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]]'s coercive monopoly policy at Banda and elsewhere in the Indies. The Discours's working-out of the bewindhebbers' secret instructions of [[11 April]] [[1608]] and [[4 May]] [[1609]] — and its argument for "Indië onafhankelijk van Holland" — defined VOC policy for the next quarter-century and provided the intellectual foundation for [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s conquest of Banda in [[1621]], his founding of [[Batavia|Batavia]] in [[1619]], and his hardline policy against the English at [[Ambon|Ambon]]. The Discours's role in the Dutch-Atjeh relationship was indirect: by [[1614]] the Atjeh trade had lost most of its strategic significance for the Dutch, and the Discours's prescription was for Banda, not for Atjeh. But the Discours's framing of the Atjeh experience — as the principal instance of the failure of free competition at an Asian court — was the proximate intellectual cause of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme that would define VOC policy for the next two centuries.
