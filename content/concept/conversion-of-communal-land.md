The **Conversion of Communal Land** (Conversie) was a significant agrarian reform in the [[Dutch East Indies]] during the late [[19th century]], aimed at replacing communal land tenure with individual ownership on [[Java]] and [[Madoera]]. The policy was a key element of liberal colonial ideology, but its implementation was highly controversial and ultimately limited in its effects.

## Background

Under traditional Javanese law, land was held communally by the *desa* (village), with periodic redistribution to household heads. The liberal colonial reformers, influenced by European concepts of private property, sought to replace this system with individual ownership, believing it would promote economic development and agricultural improvement.

## The Debate

### Liberal Position

The liberal reformers argued that:
- Communal land tenure was a "cancer" that hindered agricultural development.
- Individual ownership would provide security and incentives for investment.
- The "conversion" would bring the Javanese into the modern economy.

Fransen van de Putte, a key liberal figure, stated: "The present law aims not only to establish the principle that the Javanese is the owner of the land he has reclaimed and cultivated, but also to assure him that ownership individually, by confirming it wherever individual ownership already exists on Java, and by further introducing it in those areas where the cultivated lands are communally held."

### Conservative Position

The conservatives, including [[W. van Goltstein]], argued that:
- The Javanese were not ready for such radical change.
- The change would disrupt social order and harm the indigenous aristocracy.
- Conversion should be gradual and only when desired by the population.
- The system of "solidarity" in the villages was an important social bond.

## Implementation Attempts

### Van de Putte's Efforts ([[1864]]-[[1866]])

Van de Putte proposed a law to regulate land ownership on Java, which would have introduced individual ownership. The proposal was blocked by the [[Poortman Amendment]], which would have preserved communal rights.

### The Agrarische Wet ([[1870]])

The [[De Waal]] Agrarian Law ([[1870]]) did not address conversion directly but left it as a matter for the future. De Waal stated: "The solution of the main feature of the agrarian commune system... be left to time."

### Levyssohn Norman's Reis ([[1874]]-[[1876]])

[[H.D. Levyssohn Norman]], a liberal reformer, traveled across Java promoting conversion and witnessing the first conversions. His activities led to conflict with [[W. van Goltstein]], who viewed his efforts as premature and coercive.

### The Kabinetscirculaire ([[1880]])

Van Lansberge's circular of [[15 August]] [[1880]] attempted to clarify the government's position:
- It stated that conversion should be gradual.
- Officials should not actively promote conversion.
- The government would maintain a "neutral" position.

This circular was criticized by liberal members of the [[Second Chamber]], who viewed it as a "obstacle to free exercise of the right of the native population to choose the form of land tenure they prefer."

### The [[1885]] Regulation

A regulation on conversion was finally issued in [[1885]], but its implementation was limited. Most Javanese villages reverted to communal tenure after experimenting with individual ownership.

## Outcomes and Legacy

The conversion policy had limited practical impact:
- Most villages that adopted individual ownership later returned to communal systems.
- The Javanese social order proved resistant to imposed European concepts of property.
- The liberal reformers' vision of a transformed Javanese peasantry did not materialize.

However, the debate over conversion reflected deeper tensions in colonial policy:
- The conflict between liberal ideology and conservative pragmatism.
- The tension between Western concepts of property and Javanese social norms.
- The question of whether the colony should be reformed according to European models or preserve indigenous systems.
