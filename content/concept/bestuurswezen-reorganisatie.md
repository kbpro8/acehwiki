**Bestuurswezen Reorganisatie** ("reorganization of the governance system") refers to the comprehensive reform of the administrative structure of the Dutch East Indies that was developed, discussed, and partially implemented beginning in [[1913]]–[[1914]]. The reform initiative, led by Government Commissioner **S. de Graaf** and Chief Official **J. de Groot**, aimed to restructure the entire colonial administrative apparatus through the consolidation of provincial units, the transfer of administrative responsibilities to lower-level authorities, and the introduction of provincial councils with mixed European and indigenous membership.

## Historical Impetus

The reform initiatives emerged from developments occurring over approximately a decade (beginning around [[1902]]). Key factors driving the reorganization included:

1. **The [[Aceh War]] aftermath** ([[1873]]–[[1903]]): The prolonged and expensive military campaign to pacify [[Aceh]] had strained colonial resources and exposed the limitations of existing governance structures. The transition to civilian administration in [[Aceh]] in [[1904]] raised questions about how the pacified territory could be efficiently administered and integrated into the wider colonial system.

2. **Fiscal pressures**: The central colonial government faced growing budgetary burdens as the administrative apparatus expanded. Devolving certain functions to lower levels was seen as a potential way to distribute costs and improve efficiency.

3. **Administrative modernization**: Colonial theorists and administrators increasingly recognized that modern governance required decentralized decision-making, provincial specialization, and local participation rather than centralized bureaucratic control.

4. **Indigenous participation demands**: Growing nationalist and reformist sentiment in the [[Buitenbezittingen]] (Outer Possessions) and elsewhere suggested that some degree of indigenous participation in governance might help build legitimacy and stability.

## Development Process

Unlike the popular misconception that the reorganization proposals emerged suddenly from a single mind, the reform process involved gradual development over approximately a decade:

- **[[1902]]**: Initial descriptions and analyses prepared by the General Secretariat began the conceptual foundation
- **[[1902]]–[[1913]]**: Extended period of discussion and refinement within the Department of Domestic Affairs (*Departement van Binnenlandsch Bestuur*) and consultations with senior colonial officials
- **[[August 1913]]–[[January 1914]]**: The [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] conducted across Sumatra representing the first systematic field consultation with administrators in the colonies
- **[[1914]] onward**: Gradual implementation through individual ordinances and administrative orders

## Core Principles

The reorganization rested on several fundamental principles:

### 1. **Administrative Decentralization**
The transfer of authority and responsibility from the central colonial government in [[Batavia]] to regional and local governmental organs. This took two forms:

- **Autonomy** (*autonomie*): delegation of management of local affairs to local bodies
- **Self-governance** (*zelfbestuur*): transfer of responsibility for specific state functions to lower organs

### 2. **Provincial Consolidation**
Consolidation of existing small provinces (*gewesten*) into fewer, larger provincial units capable of supporting more sophisticated administrative structures and specialized bureaus. On Java and Madura, this meant reducing to approximately three major provinces. In the [[Buitenbezittingen]], it meant mergers such as the proposed union of [[Aceh]] with the [[Sumatra's Oostkust|East Coast]].

### 3. **Provincial Self-Governance**
Establishment of provincial councils (*Gewestelijke Raden*) with responsibility for:
- Approving provincial budgets
- Exercising limited legislative authority within colonial legal frameworks
- Oversight of provincial self-governing functions

The councils would include European and indigenous officials and non-officials, though executive authority would remain with the provincial governor (*Gewestelijk Bestuurder*).

### 4. **Indigenous Administrative Development**
Expansion of the role and authority of indigenous administrative structures, particularly on Java and Madura through regency councils (*regentschapsraden*) and in the [[Buitenbezittingen]] through recognition of indigenous self-governing territories (*zelfbesturen*).

## Application to Aceh

The reorganization proposals had important implications for [[Aceh]], which was entering a new phase of colonial governance following the [[Aceh War]]. The discussions at the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] in [[Medan]] and [[Koeota-Radja]] specifically addressed:

1. **Proposed merger with East Coast**: The possibility of combining [[Aceh]] with the [[Sumatra's Oostkust|East Coast of Sumatra]] into a single enlarged provincial unit, a proposal that drew criticism from Controller [[J. Tideman]], who argued that merging regions with different historical traditions and economic structures would create administrative complications.

2. **Provincial council composition**: The question of how an enlarged Aceh-East Coast province would structure its provincial council with adequate representation of indigenous leaders and officials alongside European administrators.

3. **Transfer of functions**: Which state functions—education, police, public works—would be transferred from central colonial control to provincial authority.

4. **Integration of indigenous hierarchy**: How Aceh's existing indigenous administrative structures (the [[uléëbalang|uléëbalangs]] and their hierarchies) would be integrated into the new self-governing provincial structure.

## Outcomes and Implementation

The [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] produced detailed records (*notulen*) documenting the state of reform thinking. However, full implementation of the comprehensive reorganization proved complex, and the system evolved through incremental changes rather than wholesale restructuring:

- The [[Zelfbestuurs-ordonnantie]] ([[1914]]) formalized the legal framework for self-governing territories in the [[Buitenbezittingen]]
- Provincial consolidations proceeded unevenly, with mergers such as the Aceh-East Coast union implemented but also generating ongoing administrative complications
- The degree of autonomous and self-governing authority actually transferred often proved more limited in practice than the reform theory had envisioned

## Significance

The Bestuurswezen Reorganisatie represented a critical juncture in Dutch colonial administrative thought and practice. It marked the transition from highly centralized nineteenth-century colonial governance toward a more differentiated system recognizing provincial variation and indigenous participation. For [[Aceh]] specifically, the reorganization placed the post-war transition to civilian administration within a broader framework of administrative modernization and decentralization—a framework that would continue shaping Aceh's administrative relationship to the colonial system through the remainder of the colonial period.
