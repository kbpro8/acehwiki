The **pacification of Aceh** (Dutch: *De pacificatie van Atjeh*) refers to the transition from military conquest to stable colonial governance in [[Acheh|Atjèh]] (Aceh) — the eventual subjugation of the Achehnese sultanate by Dutch military and political means, beginning with the first Dutch expedition to Atjeh in [[1873]] and carried through to completion under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]] ([[1898]]–[[1904]]) and his subsequent tenure as Governor-General ([[1904]]–[[1909]]). The process involved a combination of military pressure, political co-optation of local chiefs, and economic development, built on the fundamental principle that the indigenous leadership — particularly the *oelèëbalangs* — should be supported and guided rather than suppressed. This principle was advised by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] and subsequently broken by [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]], whose deviation triggered the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] and his removal. It was commemorated in the colonial capital by the [[Atjeh Monument]] in the Wilhelminapark at [[Weltevreden]], and on the battlefield itself by the [[Peutjot Cemetery|cemetery at Peutjot]] near [[Kota Radja]].

## The Van Heutsz system

Van Heutsz's approach, developed in close collaboration with Snouck Hurgronje, rested on several pillars:

- **Unconditional recognition of Dutch sovereignty** through the [[Korte Verklaring|Korte Verklaring]], a standard compact by which native chiefs acknowledged Dutch suzerainty in exchange for protection of their territorial authority.
- **Support for the traditional chiefs** (*oelèëbalangs*) against the religiously motivated resistance of the *ulama*, rather than punishing and replacing them.
- **Economic incentives** such as the creation of *landschapskassen* (regional treasuries) that gave chiefs a direct stake in good governance.
- **Military mobility** using small, highly trained *marechaussée* units to deal with remaining pockets of resistance, rather than large fixed garrisons.
- **Gradual reduction** of forced labour and arbitrary fines to win over the population.

The intellectual foundation for this system was laid by Snouck Hurgronje's ethnographic study of Acehnese society, *De Atjehers* ([[1893]]–[[1894]]), which demonstrated that the [[Aceh War]] could not be won by military means alone, and that the chiefs — not the Sultan or the *ulama* — were the natural partners of colonial governance. Snouck's analysis of the [[Gampong|gampong]], the [[Moekim|moekim]], and the territorial *oelèëbalangschappen* provided the framework on which Van Heutsz's policies were built.

## The British experience confirmed

The difficulty of the task was already visible in the British experience of [[1786]] — that to secure order in a place like [[Penang|Pinang]] and Aceh it would be necessary "to subdue all the chiefs" and "to overawe the inhabitants." The Dutch, compelled during the second decennial period of the nineteenth century to take measures to ensure the safety of foreign trade in Aceh, discovered that the same lesson applied: military conquest alone was insufficient without the active cooperation of the local power-holders.

## The Gayo-Alas campaign and the final military phase

The last major military operation of the pacification was the [[Gajoland|Gajo]]-[[Alaslanden|Alas]] expedition of [[1904]], led by Van Daalen when still a lieutenant colonel. The campaign broke the last major centre of resistance in central Aceh, bringing the interior under Dutch control and setting the stage for the transition to civil administration. After [[1905]], with Van Heutsz as Governor-General overseeing from Batavia, the focus shifted to consolidation and administration.

## The Van Daalen deviation and its correction

The pacification policy was severely tested during the governorship of [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen]] ([[1905]]–[[1908]]), who abandoned the conciliatory approach in favour of punishment and coercion — arbitrary fines, internment of chiefs and their families at [[Sabang]], dismissal of *oelèëbalangs* without replacement, and general contempt for local institutions. His deviation produced the *vrij hevige opflikkering van het verzet* (fairly fierce flare-up of resistance) of [[1907]] and [[1908]] and triggered the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] of 1907–1908. The return of [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]] to the governorship in 1908 restored the original policy. Swart's *verzoenende en vertrouwenwekkende politiek* — conciliatory and confidence-building policy — drew the chiefs who had been held down by force "steeds meer ook uit overtuiging aan onze zijde" (ever more, from conviction, to the Dutch side) and inaugurated the *periode van rust* that allowed the economic and administrative development documented in *De Buitenbezittingen*.

## Results

By [[1909]], when Van Heutsz left office, Aceh was largely peaceful. The economic infrastructure — roads, markets, handicraft schools, and agricultural projects — was developing; the population, while still scarred by decades of war, was increasingly accepting Dutch rule; and the cautious replacement of military by civil administrators had begun. *De Buitenbezittingen* treats this consolidation as "een krachtig bewijs" — powerful proof — of the success of the pacification, which had come "betrekkelijk snel en onverwacht" (relatively quickly and unexpectedly). The pacification of Aceh became a model for the subsequent expansion of Dutch control over the outer islands.

## Account from *Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas* ([[1926]]) — The Pacification

The *Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas* refers to the pacification of Atjeh in three distinct contexts, each of which attests to its central place in the official colonial narrative:

1. **Governor Van Heutsz's achievement**: As Governor of Atjeh from [[1897]], Van Heutsz "bewerkte de onderwerping van dat gewest" (accomplished the subjugation of that territory).

2. **The Atjeh Monument**: The monument in the Wilhelminapark at [[Weltevreden]], [[Batavia]], "werd opgericht ter gedachtenis aan de pacificatie van Atjeh" (was erected in memory of the pacification of Atjeh).

3. **The Peutjot Cemetery**: Near [[Kota Radja]], the cemetery "werd voornamelijk bestemd voor de op de verschillende expedities in Atjeh gesneuvelden" (was primarily intended for those who fell on the various expeditions in Atjeh).

## The Policy Shift

The atlas also provides crucial context for understanding how the pacification was achieved. It notes that the [[Buitengewesten]] had previously suffered from neglect under the [[Onthoudingspolitiek|policy of abstention]] (*onthoudingspolitiek*), but that at the end of the previous century this was replaced "onder den Gouverneur-Generaal Van Heutsz door een krachtige en doelbewuste politiek van het onder ons gezag brengen er van" (under the Governor-General Van Heutsz by an energetic and purposeful policy of bringing [the territories] under our authority). The pacification was not merely a military event but was accompanied by — and made possible by — the construction of roads, railways, and other infrastructure that integrated the formerly independent territories into the colonial state. By [[1926]], the year of the atlas's publication, the war in Atjeh — which had begun in [[1873]] — was fully in the past, commemorated by monuments rather than fought on battlefields.

## Significance

The pacification of Aceh was a turning point in Dutch colonial history. It demonstrated that a combination of military force, political acumen, and economic development could succeed where pure coercion had failed for decades. It also set the stage for the "ethical policy" of the twentieth century, which sought to balance imperial control with the welfare of the colonised population. In the broader history of the Aceh War, the pacification is often cited as demonstrating that the critical variable in colonial governance is the relationship between the administration and indigenous institutions — a lesson the Van Daalen episode taught at great cost. The atlas's commemoration of the pacification in [[1926]] — through the [[Atjeh Monument]], the [[Peutjot Cemetery|cemetery at Peutjot]], and the portrait gallery of "Groote Mannen" — fixed the pacification as the centrepiece of the official colonial reckoning of the late Dutch East Indies.
