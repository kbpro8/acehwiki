The **Manté's** are legendary forest-dwelling people said to inhabit the mountains of the **XXII Mukims** in [[Aceh]], described in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *De Atjehers* and later discussed in the chapter *Jagerslatijn* (Hunters' Latin) of [[Oost-Indisch Journael|*Oost-Indisch Journael*]] by Merac ([[1947]]). Said to wear no clothing and to be densely hairy over the whole body, the manté's served in colonial-era writing on [[Aceh]] as the archetypal "people of the interior" — known to all informants only by hearsay, and always located one day's journey further inland than wherever the investigator happened to be.

## The Snouck Hurgronje account

In *De Atjehers*, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] describes the manté's as unclothed, heavily hairy inhabitants of the mountains of the XXII Mukims. He notes that every informant knew of them only by reputation rather than personal experience. As Merac's text summarises the passage:

> "In his well-known work *De Atjehers*, Snouck Hurgronje already makes mention of inlanders who are called *manté's*. These *manté's* would wear no clothing and would be densely hairy over the whole body. They would live in the mountains of the XXII Moekims, and all informants knew them only by hearsay. One informant knew to tell that, in the time of his grandfather, a *manté* married couple was brought captive to the [[Sultan of Aceh|Sultan of Atjeh]]. Despite all the efforts that were employed to persuade them, those forest-people refused to speak or to eat and finally died the voluntary death by starvation."

The captive couple's voluntary death by starvation is the single most-cited episode involving the manté's in the colonial literature on [[Aceh]].

## Acehnese writings and the "one day's journey" motif

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] also observed that Acehnese writings occasionally mention the manté's. However, he remarked that they were always said to live *one day's journey farther into the interior* than wherever the investigator happened to be. As Merac frames the observation:

> "Also in Acehnese writings these *manté's* are sometimes mentioned, but Snouck Hurgronje already remarked that these beings always lived one day's journey further inland than the researcher who wished to see them."

This recurring motif reinforces the manté's role as elusive inhabitants of the wilderness — always reported nearby, yet never actually encountered. Their location remains perpetually beyond the reach of direct observation.

## The orang pendek and the koekoeman

In *Jagerslatijn*, the manté's are discussed alongside the **orang pendek**, the legendary "short man" of the Acehnese forests. An elderly hunter named [[Datoek]] claims to have personally seen such a creature. During the conversation, the district court chairman interrupts Datoek's account by citing [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s description of the manté's as an ethnographic framework for interpreting the story. In Datoek's region, the orang pendek is known as the **koekoeman**.

Unlike the manté's, whose existence rests entirely on hearsay, Datoek insists he saw the koekoeman himself — from behind, in broad daylight at around 12:30 p.m., from a distance of only twenty metres.

## Significance

The manté's occupy an important place in colonial-era writing about [[Aceh]] as the archetypal "people of the interior." In both *De Atjehers* and [[Oost-Indisch Journael|*Oost-Indisch Journael*]], they represent a population that is consistently described but never directly observed. Their defining characteristics are not only their supposed appearance but also their persistent elusiveness. Every account is second-hand, and they are always said to live just beyond the limits of exploration. As such, the manté's became a colonial literary and ethnographic symbol of the unknown forests and mountains of [[Aceh]] rather than a documented people.
