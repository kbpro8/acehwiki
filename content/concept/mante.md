The **manté's** are legendary forest-dwelling people said to inhabit the mountains of the **XXII Mukims** in Aceh. They are described in **Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje**'s *De Atjehers* and later discussed in the chapter *Jagerslatijn* of *Oost-Indisch Journael* by Merac (1947).

According to these accounts, the manté's wore no clothing and were covered in dense body hair. They were believed to live deep in the mountains of the XXII Mukims, yet none of the reported informants had encountered them directly; all knew of them only through hearsay. As a result, the manté's became a literary and ethnographic symbol of the mysterious "people of the interior" in colonial descriptions of Aceh.

## The Snouck Hurgronje account

In *De Atjehers*, Snouck Hurgronje describes the manté's as unclothed, heavily hairy inhabitants of the mountains of the XXII Mukims. He notes that every informant knew of them only by reputation rather than personal experience.

One informant recounted a story from his grandfather's time in which a manté couple was captured and brought before the Sultan of Aceh. Despite repeated attempts to persuade them to speak or eat, the pair remained silent and ultimately died from voluntary starvation.

## Acehnese writings and the "one day's journey" motif

Snouck Hurgronje also observed that Acehnese writings occasionally mention the manté's. However, he remarked that they were always said to live *one day's journey farther into the interior* than wherever the investigator happened to be.

This recurring motif reinforces the manté's role as elusive inhabitants of the wilderness—always reported nearby, yet never actually encountered. Their location remains perpetually beyond the reach of direct observation.

## The orang pendek and the koekoeman

In *Jagerslatijn*, the manté's are discussed alongside the **orang pendek**, the legendary "short man" of the Acehnese forests. An elderly hunter named **Datoek** claims to have personally seen such a creature.

During the conversation, the district court chairman interrupts Datoek's account by citing Snouck Hurgronje's description of the manté's as an ethnographic framework for interpreting the story. In Datoek's region, the orang pendek is known as the **koekoeman**.

Unlike the manté's, whose existence rests entirely on hearsay, Datoek insists he saw the koekoeman himself—from behind, in broad daylight at around 12:30 p.m., from a distance of only twenty metres.

## Significance

The manté's occupy an important place in colonial-era writing about Aceh as the archetypal "people of the interior." In both *De Atjehers* and *Oost-Indisch Journael*, they represent a population that is consistently described but never directly observed.

Their defining characteristics are not only their supposed appearance but also their persistent elusiveness. Every account is second-hand, and they are always said to live just beyond the limits of exploration. As such, the manté's became a colonial literary and ethnographic symbol of the unknown forests and mountains of Aceh rather than a documented people.
