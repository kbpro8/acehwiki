**Pinus Merkusii** — the Sumatra pine, in Dutch *Sumatra-den* — is a species of pine native to the [[Gajo-landen|Gajo-Landen]]-[[Atjeh]] border region of northern [[Sumatra]], and the principal source of the colonial-era Atjeh-turpentine industry. The species — named *Pinus Merkusii Jungh et de Vr.* after the nineteenth-century botanists Junghuhn and de Vriese — is the only pine native to Sumatra, and the only pine native to the Southern Hemisphere; its stands in the *Semnenboschcomplexen* of [[Paja Djorok]] and [[Blangtekoer]] were the principal raw material of the [[Baleq]] enterprise of *'s Lands Caoutchouchedrijf* from [[1924]] onward.

## Distribution and silviculture

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet of 1930]] records that the *Semnenboschcomplexen* of Paja Djorok and Blangtekoer — both *betrekkelijk dicht gelegen bij den grooten weg van Takengon naar Bireuen (Atjeh)* — were the principal sites of the trial exploitation that began on [[14 September]] [[1924]]. The pamphlet's Fig. I — captioned *Bosch van Pinus Merkusii in Atjeh* — is a photograph from the Koloniaal Instituut collection, taken by O. Brandts Buys, that illustrates the silvicultural condition of the Pinus Merkusii stands in the late [[1920s]]. The same stands had earlier been the subject of the [[C. Brandts Buys|Brandts Buys]] monograph *Bijdrage tot de kennis van Pinus Merkusii Jungh et de Vr., meer in het bijzonder in de Gajo-landen* — the principal early scientific study of the species.

## The *quarre* tapping method

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the Pinus Merkusii of the [[Baleq]] enterprise was tapped using the *[[quarre]]* method — a French system borrowed from the turpentine industry of the Landes. The tapping wounds — 10 cm wide and at greatest depth 2½ cm deep — were refreshed every three days in an upward direction with a slightly curved carpenter's chisel. The number of tapping wounds per tree depended on the thickness of the trunk: at a circumference of 1–1½ m, one wound; at 1½–2 m, two wounds; above that, three. The balsam — the raw resinous exudate of the tapped pines — was collected and distilled to yield approximately 75% colophonium (resin) and 20% turpentine.

## The chemical composition of the turpentine

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the chemical composition of Pinus Merkusii turpentine — as established by Dr. [[D. B. Spoelstra]]'s fractional distillation of [[1930]] — was 85–90% α-pinene and approximately 4% Δ3-carene, against approximately 75% α-pinene and 15% carene in the [[1924]] trial samples. The fourfold reduction in carene content — from 15% to 4% — was identified as the chemical basis of the improved odour of the factory-scale product. The high α-pinene content of Pinus Merkusii turpentine — 85–90%, against 60–70% in the *Pinus palustris*-derived American and *Pinus Pinaster*-derived French turpentines — is the distinctive chemical feature of the species, and the source of the marginal shortfall of the Atjeh product from the draft *Ontwerp-normaalblad V 597* refractive-index standard.

## The Pinus Merkusii forests in the colonial economy

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the [[Baleq]] enterprise — the principal colonial-era turpentine factory in the Netherlands Indies — was operated by *'s Lands Caoutchouchedrijf* on behalf of the *Dienst van het Boschwezen*, and that by [[1930]] the area under exploitation was approximately 1600 ha, entirely in production. The colophonium (resin) was packed in cases and sold principally to the batik workshops on [[Java]] and, secondarily, to soap factories on the [[Oostkust van Sumatra]]. The turpentine was packed in petroleum tins of 18 litres or 15½ kg per tin, and was sold partly in the Netherlands Indies and partly — through the [[Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij]] — in Europe. The Pinus Merkusii forests of the [[Gajo-landen|Gajo-Landen]]-[[Atjeh]] border region thus supplied both the metropolitan Dutch varnish and paint industry and the colonial Java batik industry.

## Significance

Pinus Merkusii's significance in the historiography of colonial Aceh is as the principal raw material of the [[Baleq]] enterprise — the only pine native to Sumatra, and the only pine native to the Southern Hemisphere. The species' high α-pinene content — 85–90%, against 60–70% in the American and French turpentines — is the distinctive chemical feature that both gave the Atjeh-turpentine its improved odour and produced the marginal shortfall from the draft *Ontwerp-normaalblad V 597* refractive-index standard. The species' stands in the *Semnenboschcomplexen* of Paja Djorok and Blangtekoer were the principal raw material of the colonial-era Atjeh-turpentine industry, and the [[C. Brandts Buys|Brandts Buys]] monograph on the species is the principal early scientific study of Sumatran pine silviculture.
