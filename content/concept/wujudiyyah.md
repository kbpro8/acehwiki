**The Wujūdiyyah** — also called *Ittiḥādiyya*, *Mulhid*, or *Zindīq* — is the doctrine of the unity of being (*waḥdat al-wujūd*) in the form in which it was developed at the court of [[Acheh]] by [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and his successor [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, and against which [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] directed his polemic after his arrival in Acheh in [[1637]]. The doctrine is set out in the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq*]] of Raniri, where it is divided into the upper-wind (*bovenwindse*) and lower-wind (*benedenwindse*) Wujūdiyyah; to the latter belong Hamzah Pansuri and his school.

## The doctrine

The doctrine of the Wujūdiyyah, as set out by Raniri in the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]], is that "being is one, namely the being of God; this is not a self-subsistent existent distinct from creation, but is enclosed in all that is created; so that the world = God, God = the world." The Wujūdiyyah say: "nothing exists but God; *lā ilāha illā Allāh* means 'there is no being of mine, only the being of God'" — by which they intend: "God's being is this my being." They further teach that "God and we are of one genus and of one being," and that "the essence of God is knowable, and his quality and quantity are manifest, insofar as he exists outwardly in time and place."

## The distinction from orthodox Sufism

Raniri distinguishes sharply between the orthodox Sufi position and that of the heretical Wujūdiyyah. The Sufis, Raniri argues, accept the theologians' doctrine of two beings — the necessary being of God and the contingent being of the created world — but employ a different terminology in which "being" signifies only the divine essence, while the world is mere manifestation (*maẓhar*) and shadow (*ẓill*). In the Sufi's saying "God and the world are one," the meaning is that the world is an accident of God, not counted, in the same way that a mirror-image is not a second sun. The Wujūdiyyah, by contrast, mean by the same formula that God and the world are one in essence — a position Raniri judges to be that "of an unbeliever and an atheist." The Sufi position is, in Raniri's argument, that of the orthodox *mutakallimūn* in different terminology; the Wujūdiyyah position is a heresy that conflates shadow and substance.

## The Achehnese Wujūdiyyah

The Achehnese Wujūdiyyah of the school of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] is the principal object of Raniri's polemic. Hamzah's *[[Asrar al-'arifin|Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] expound the doctrine of the unity of being in its most extreme Achehnese form, drawing on the Sufi tradition of [[Ibn Arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] and on Persian Sufi poetry. [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] (d. [[1630]]) continued Hamzah's school at the court of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]], and his *[[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn]]* and *[[Chirkah|Chirkah]]* (or *Ḥarakah*) developed the metaphysics of the unity of being in Malay. The Wujūdiyyah flourished at the Achehnese court until the death of Iskandar Muda in [[1636]].

## The orthodox reaction

The arrival of [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]] in [[1637]], in the first year of the reign of [[Iskandar Thani]], opened the orthodox reaction against the Wujūdiyyah. Raniri [[Raniri's public debate against the Wujudiyyah in Acheh|refuted the adherents of the extreme Wujūdiyyah mysticism in a public debate]] in the presence of the sultan; the disciples of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] were put to death and his books burned. The composition of the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]] — both directed against the Wujūdiyyah — followed at the command of [[Iskandar Thani]] and his successor [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Taj al-Ālam]]. Raniri continued to enjoy royal favour under both sultans; his authority replaced that of the Wujūdiyyah mystics at the Achehnese court, and the production of Malay religious prose in the new orthodox dispensation — the [[Sirat al-Mustaqim|*Ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*]], the [[Bustan al-Salatin|*Bustān as-Salāṭīn*]], the [[Hall al-zill|*Ḥall aẓ-ẓill*]], the [[Djawahir al-'ulum|*Jawāhir al-ʿulūm*]] — replaced the Wujūdiyyah literature of the age of Iskandar Muda.

## Sources and authorities

In his refutation of the Wujūdiyyah Raniri cites a number of authorities. The principal among them is [[Ibn Arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] in the *Futūḥāt Makkīya*, who is quoted as rejecting those who identify the visible world with God. The Indian mystic [[Ahmad al-Mahaimi|ʿAlī b. Aḥmad al-Mahāʾimī]] (d. 835 H / [[1432]]) is cited in the *Ithbād an-naṣīḥa* and the *Irāʾat ad-daqāʾiq* to the effect that "whoever teaches that the Reason, the Soul, etc., are the Highest Reality is as wrong as one who says 'Zaid's hand is Zaid,' and is an unbeliever and atheist." [[Muhammad b. Fadl Allah|Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh]] is cited in the *Tuḥfa al-mursala*. The lost [[Lata'if al-asrar|*Laṭāʾif al-iʿlām*]] is cited on the unity of being. Raniri also distinguishes the orthodox Wujūdiyyah (*Wujūdiyya al-muwaḥḥid*) — who are the Sufis — from the heretical Wujūdiyyah (*Wujūdiyya al-mulḥid*), who are the atheists.

## Significance

The controversy over the Wujūdiyyah is the central event of the religious history of [[Acheh]] in the seventeenth century. It marks the transition from the golden age of Achehnese mystical poetry — the age of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] — to the age of the orthodox reaction under [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]], and the composition of the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]] is the principal literary deposit of the controversy. The polemic against the Wujūdiyyah also preserves, in the form of citations and refutations, the only extended fragments of the works of Hamzah Pansuri and Shamsuddin of Pasai — works whose own manuscripts have survived only in fragments and which are known to us chiefly through the writings of their opponent.
