**Islam in Atjeh** — [[Acheh]] was one of the earliest territories in the Indonesian Archipelago to adopt Islam, a fact attested by both archaeological evidence and the literary tradition as noted in the *Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas* ([[1926]]).

## Account from *Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas* (1926) — Early Islamisation

The atlas, in its chapter on religion, addresses the early spread of Islam in the Archipelago: "Uit gegevens uit reisverhalen en Mohammedaansche grafsteenen met opschriften uit de 15e eeuw valt af te leiden dat de Islam destijds reeds vasten voet had gevat, zoowel op de N.O. kust van Sumatra (de Moslimsche rijkjes Perlak en Pasé in omstreeks 1500) als kort daarna op de N.O. kust van Java. Dat de streken, die reeds vroegtijdig werden bekeerd, behooren o.a. de Molukken, Atjeh en Palembang, terwijl ook de Menangkabausche Maleiers reeds in oude tijden tot den Islam moeten zijn overgegaan."

(From data in travel accounts and Mohammedan tombstones with inscriptions from the 15th century, it can be deduced that Islam had by then already gained a firm footing, both on the N.E. coast of Sumatra (the Muslim principalities of Perlak and Pasé around 1500) and shortly thereafter on the N.E. coast of Java. Among the regions that were converted at an early date are, inter alia, the Moluccas, Atjeh, and Palembang, while the Minangkabau Malays must also have converted to Islam in ancient times.)

## Significance

The atlas's classification of Atjeh among the earliest Islamic regions of the Archipelago is consistent with the scholarly consensus that North Sumatra was the gateway through which Islam entered the Indonesian world. The specific mention of [[Perlak]] and [[Pasé]] as Muslim principalities (*Moslimsche rijkjes*) on the north-east coast of Sumatra around [[1500]] provides a terminus ante quem for the Islamisation of the broader region. The presence of Muslims in Perlak and Pasé by that date suggests that Atjeh itself, lying adjacent to these sultanates, was already in the process of conversion or had recently converted.

The atlas also notes that the Islam that reached the Archipelago came via Persia and Hindustan and was already modified by the customs and thought-patterns of the peoples of India, with which the Indonesians were already partly familiar through the earlier influence of Hinduism. This "adaptability" (*aanpassingsvermogen*) of Islam is cited as a reason for its rapid spread.
