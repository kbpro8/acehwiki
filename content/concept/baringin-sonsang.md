**Baringin sonsang** — Malay "inverted waringin (banyan)" — is the allegorical image of a banyan tree whose roots stand in the air and whose leaves hang downwards, used in Malay, Minangkabau and Javanese folk tradition as a *bedekte taal* form for the inverted ancestral land of [[Taroema]] on the Sumatran east coast. The image is the principal *bedekte taal* sign of the dispersal of the [[Djawaka|Djawaka]] of [[Aroe|Aru]] by the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjehnese conquest of Aroe]] of [[1539]], and the central image of [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]]'s chapter *Melasaké pampang*.

## The image

The ***baringin sonsang***, [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] records, is described in Minangkabau tradition (citing [[Dr. H. N. van der Tuuk|Van der Toorn]] in *BKI*, 5e Volgr. V, p. 88) as "a wholly imaginary tree, the *baringin songrang*, i.e. an inverted waringin, whose leaves with the rough surface turned upward, growing on the Moon, serves as the pleasure-garden of children deceased in youth, who are brought thither by *bidadari*'s and laid in golden cradles hung beneath the rich foliage". In [[Perak|Perak]] tradition (citing [[Walter William Skeat|Skeat]]'s *Malay Magic*), the same image survives as the ***beringin sonsang***, "probably also a mythological explanation of the enigmatic black drawing on the lunar disc, the heavenly abode of the Heavenly Queen [[Doerga|Doerga]] of [[Taroema]]".

## Decoding: Taroema inverted

The image, in [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]]'s reading, is a *bedekte taal* form for the inverted ancestral land of [[Taroema]] — the place whose "tree" (*taroe*) is its own name (*na-ma*), and whose "inversion" by the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjehnese conquest of Aroe]] of [[1539]] marks the dispersal of the [[Djawaka]] and the close of the Hindu-Javanese east-coast age. The same image is, in De Roo de la Faille's reading, the *Melasaké pampang|Melasaké pampang]] of South Sumatran tradition — the "wonder-tree of Belalau" whose roots stood in the air, and whose leaves hung downwards — and the ***boom Melasaké pampang*** of the Lampong-Semangka origin legend.

## The Bocheltje and the mouse

Beneath the inverted waringin, [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] records, sits an old hunchback (*Bocheltje*), ever fishing with a long line that hangs down to the Earth; if his line ever reached the Earth, the world would end — but a mouse ever gnaws the line through. The Bocheltje, De Roo de la Faille decodes, is the *bong-kok* of Taroema — a wordplay on *hong* ("Chinese cemetery", i.e. *soe-tji boewa-na*, "the dead Taroema"); the line, of *koelit terap* bast, is the *koe-ta a-lit Soengei Tarab*; the mouse is *ti-koes*, the islet *sak-ti koes-ta* that prevents Taroema from carrying off the people of the Earth; and the cat that watches is *koetjing*, the *Koetjing* colony of [[Cochin|Cochin]] on the [[Coromandel Coast]] — "in perfect agreement with the Bocheltje, whose wicked business is nonetheless prevented".

## Significance

For AcehWiki, the *baringin sonsang* is the principal allegorical sign of the dispersal of the [[Djawaka]] by the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Atjehnese conquest of Aroe]] of [[1539]] — the *bedekte taal* form by which Malay, Minangkabau and Javanese folk tradition recorded the "inversion" of the ancestral land of [[Taroema]] and the close of the Hindu-Javanese east-coast age. Its survival in three regional traditions — Minangkabau, Perak, and Lampong-Semangka — also marks it as the most widely-diffused single allegory of the [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] corpus.
