**Prang sabi** (Atjèhnese, from the Arabic *fi sabil Allah*, "in the path of God"; English "holy war") is the Atjèhnese term for the religious duty, incumbent on every Muslim in time of conflict with the infidel, of taking up arms against the *[[kaphé|kaphé]]* (unbeliever) — a duty that, in Atjèh, attained a prominence and an intensity "to a higher degree inherent in the Acehnese than in most of their co-religionists elsewhere, who, taught by experience, have set it aside as no longer of this time." Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats *prang sabi* as the central religious-political idea of Atjèhnese resistance — the thread that runs from the [[Atjèh War|Atjèh War]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]] through the [[Bakóngan uprising (1925–1927)|Bakóngan uprising]] and the [[Lhóng action (1933)|Lhóng action of 1933]] to the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion]] of [[1942]].

## The hikajat prang sabi

The religious duty of *prang sabi* was inculcated through the *[[hikajat prang sabi|hikajat prang sabi]]* — the prose-poetic "incitement to holy war so notorious from the Aceh War period." Piekaar records a characteristic verse from the *hikajat*: "If we do not reconcile with the unbelieving Dutchmen, they are our hereditary enemies for all time." The text, "published by Damsté and translated in the *Bijdragen tot de taal-, land- en volkenkunde van Nederlandsch-Indië* (volume 84, p. 545 ff.), had apparently lost none of its old persuasive power and still formed the primary means to incite the believers to become fighters on God's path." Its recitation was a capital instrument of religious mobilisation: "Following in the footsteps of their Dutch predecessors, the recitation of the 'hikajat prang sabi,' the incitement to holy war so notorious from the Aceh War period, was therefore forbidden" by the Japanese from [[1942]].

## The duty and the sabi

For the Atjèhnese, *prang sabi* was "a pious duty imposed by Islamic law." [[Teukoe Panglima Pólém|Teukoe Panglima Pólém]] of [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] — the great resistance leader — considered "the acceptance of the authority of the infidel (Atj. *[[kaphé|kaphé]]*) … an abomination and the conduct of the holy war (Atj. *prang sabi*) a religious duty." The fighter who fell in its performance became a *sjahid* (Arabic *shahīd*, "witness of faith"): "Teungkoe Tjhi' di Tiró and his sons … all fell as 'sjahid' (Ar. witness of faith) for the defense of religion and fatherland." The duty could also be invoked as the badge of an internal revolution: an anonymous writer in the *[[Atjèh Shimbun|Atjèh Shimbun]]* of [[29 March]] [[1944]] called on his fellows to "gird ourselves for the *prang sabi*" in defence of the new *shilkyó-hóin* religious court.

## Prang sabi and the Japanese invasion

In [[1942]] the duty of *prang sabi* was reawakened across Atjèh. "How else is it to be explained," Piekaar asks of the older generation in the *sagi* of the [[XXV Moekims|XXV Moekims]], "that over this older generation, seemingly without any transition, the spirit of the 'prang sabi' again became prevalent?" The Japanese occupation was hailed in a saying of the period: "*kaphé ploeëng, daja taniOng*" — "the unbelievers have fled, the anti-Christ has arrived." Yet the Japanese, "completely unfamiliar with the country and people … would come into conflict with the same Atjèhnese religious fanaticism that had been their ally in driving out the Dutch infidels." Already in [[August]] [[1942]] a recitation of the *[[hikajat prang sabi|hikajat prang sabi]]* in the subdistrict [[Lhó' Soekón|Lila' Soekón]] triggered the first serious encounter between Japanese authority and Atjèhnese religious fanaticism — the precursor of the [[Bajoe|Bajoe]] rising suppressed in blood in [[November]] [[1942]].

## The Japanese prohibition

The occupation regime repeatedly attempted to suppress the *prang sabi* idea. The *[[hikajat prang sabi|hikajat prang sabi]]* was banned from [[1942]]; on [[13 July]] [[1945]] the head of the political intelligence service (*tokkei-ka*) again reminded the police that "possessing and reciting the 'hikajat prang sabi,' 'of which it is again heard,' is still forbidden," with the strictest action to be taken against recitation in public meetings. Yet, as Piekaar drily notes, "the Acehnese nature was not denying itself" the *hikajat* — and the duty it broadcast — would outlast both the Dutch and the Japanese occupations.
