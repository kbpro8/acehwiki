**Kapok Fiber** (Dutch: *kapokvezel*) was the fluffy seed-hair (Dutch: *zaadharen*) of the kapok fruit (Dutch: *kapokvrucht*), harvested from the *Ceiba pentandra* tree cultivated in the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]]. After de-seeding (Dutch: *ontpitten*) and removal of the seed-coats (Dutch: *zaadhuiden*), the cleaned fibres were used as stuffing for mattresses and cushions, as thermal and acoustic insulation, and — on account of their natural buoyancy — as the filling for life-jackets and life-belts. Kapok fibre contributed modestly to the export basket of [[Aceh]], often bundled with other forest products such as [[Rattan (Aceh)|rattan]] and [[Resin (Aceh)|resin]] for shipment through [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]] and [[Belawan]].

## The kapok tree and its cultivation

The kapok tree (*Ceiba pentandra*, Dutch: *kapokboom* or *wolboom*) was widely planted across the Acehnese lowlands, both as a village shade tree and as a managed cash crop. Mature trees, reaching 25 to 35 metres in height, produced their first commercial crop at eight to ten years of age and continued to yield for fifty years or more. The trees flowered and fruited annually, producing elongated woody pods (Dutch: *kapokcapsules*) up to fifteen centimetres long that split open when ripe to release the mass of silky white fibre surrounding the small black seeds. Harvesting was carried out by climbing the tree or by knocking the pods off with long bamboo poles (Dutch: *stokken*), a labour typically performed by young men in the dry season between February and April.

After the harvest the pods were sun-dried for several days, then threshed to release the fibre and seed mass. This crude mixture was bagged and either processed at the village level or sold to a dealer for cleaning at a mechanical cleaning plant (Dutch: *kapokontpittingsinrichting*).

## Processing

The raw kapok was purified by removing the seeds (Dutch: *pitten*), the seed-linings (Dutch: *zaadvliezen*), and the broken fruit walls (Dutch: *vruchtwanden*), yielding a clean, buoyant, slightly waxy fibre of exceptional lightness — about one-eighth the weight of cotton by volume. At the village level this was done by hand, the fibre being tossed in a breeze that carried the light hairs away while the heavier seeds and debris fell to the ground. By the early twentieth century mechanical ginning machines (Dutch: *ontpitmachines*) driven by small diesel engines had been installed in cleaning plants at [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]], [[Langsa]], and [[Lhokseumawe|Lho' Seumawè]], and the cleaned fibre was hydraulically pressed into bales of approximately 100 kilograms for export.

The cleaned fibre graded into three categories: white prime kapok (Dutch: *witte prima kapok*), slightly yellowish second grade, and the short-fibred *kapok-afval* used for cheap stuffing. The prime grade was exported principally to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and to [[Singapore|Singapore]], where it was either made up into upholstery or shipped onward to European and American upholstery and life-saving-equipment manufacturers.

## Economic role and uses

Kapok fibre contributed modestly to the export basket of [[Aceh]], often bundled with other forest products like [[Rattan (Aceh)|rattan]] and [[Resin (Aceh)|resin]]. Its principal uses — mattress and cushion stuffing, insulation, and buoyant life-jacket filling — guaranteed a steady European demand through the [[1910s]] and [[1920s]], particularly during and after the First World War when the demand for military life-belts expanded. The colonial navy (Dutch: *Koninklijke Marine*) and merchant marine were important buyers of kapok for life-jacket filling on account of the fibre's natural buoyancy: a cubic metre of kapok could support approximately 30 kilograms of weight in water for several days without becoming waterlogged.

The Atjeh kapok trade was overshadowed in the [[1930s]] by competition from the more extensively cultivated kapok of [[Java]] — where the *Eriodendron anfractuosum* (synonym of *Ceiba pentandra*) was a major estate crop — and by the rise of synthetic stuffing materials. By the late colonial period kapok had become a marginal Atjeh export, though village-level use for stuffing pillows and mattresses continued through the Japanese occupation and into the post-independence period.
