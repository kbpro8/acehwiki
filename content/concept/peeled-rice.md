**Peeled Rice (Aceh)** (Dutch: *gepelde rijst*) was the main staple (Dutch: *stamvoedsel*) of the population of the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]], consisting of rice grains from which the outer husk had been removed but the inner silver-bran (Dutch: *zilvervlies*) was largely left intact. The retention of the silver-bran preserved the thiamine (vitamin B1) and other essential nutrients of the grain, in sharp contrast to fully milled white rice (Dutch: *witte rijst*), the consumption of which caused beriberi (Dutch: *beri-beri*) when it became the dietary cornerstone of communities with limited access to whole-grain processing technologies. Despite its health advantages, the partial-peeling method characteristic of Atjeh rice milling came under pressure in the early twentieth century from the spread of machine milling.

## The rice economy of Atjeh

Rice was the dietary and economic foundation of every Acehnese community. The sawahs (Dutch: *sawahs*) of the coastal plain and the rain-fed upland rice fields (Dutch: *ladangs*) of the interior together produced the bulk of the food consumed by the population, and rice was also the principal tax-and-tribute commodity of the pre-colonial [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] and the [[uleebalang|territorial chiefs]]. Rice was harvested, threshed by foot or by animal-treading, winnowed in the wind, and then stored as paddy (Dutch: *padi*) in the raised rice-barns (Dutch: *rimpé* or *lumbung*) that stood beside every Acehnese farmhouse.

The conversion of paddy to edible rice was carried out by the women of the household using the wooden mortar and pestle (Dutch: *rijstblok* and *stamper*) that had been the standard Indonesian rice-processing tool since antiquity. The paddy was pounded in the mortar to crack the outer husk (Dutch: *dop*), then winnowed to separate the husk from the brown rice (Dutch: *bruine rijst*), then pounded a second time to remove most — but not all — of the silver-bran (Dutch: *zilvervlies*), the thin, silvery inner skin that surrounds the white endosperm.

## The silver-bran and the beriberi question

The retention of the silver-bran was not merely an artefact of imperfect milling technology: it was, unintentionally, the principal public-health safeguard of the traditional Acehnese diet. The silver-bran contains essentially all of the thiamine (vitamin B1), niacin, and other B-complex vitamins of the rice grain. When the bran is removed — as occurs in fully milled white rice — the resulting product is nutritionally depleted, and a diet based on it, in the absence of other thiamine sources, leads to the deficiency disease beriberi (Dutch: *beri-beri*).

Beriberi was a major public-health problem of the early twentieth-century Netherlands Indies, particularly among the Javanese plantation labourers (Dutch: *contractkoelies*) whose rations consisted largely of machine-milled white rice. The colonial medical service — building on the work of Christiaan Eijkman, Gerrit Grijns and Adolphe Vorderman — established the thiamine-deficiency aetiology of beriberi in the [[1890s]] and [[1900s]], and from [[1910]] onward advocated the substitution of partially-peeled "silver-bran rice" (Dutch: *zilvervliesrijst*) for fully-milled white rice in institutional rations.

The Acehnese smallholder tradition of hand-pounding rice, by leaving most of the silver-bran intact, naturally produced a beriberi-resistant staple. The consumption of fully peeled white rice, by contrast, caused beriberi as the removal of the bran eliminated essential vitamins — a lesson that the colonial medical service learned from the Atjeh contrast.

## Cultural role and the threat of machine milling

Despite its health advantages, peeled rice remained the dietary cornerstone of the Atjeh population, and the traditional hand-pounding of rice was a daily domestic ritual of great cultural significance: the rhythmic sound of the pestle against the mortar marked the rhythm of village life, and the skill of efficient pounding was a competence expected of every adult woman. The early twentieth century brought a threat to this tradition in the form of the machine rice-mill (Dutch: *rijstpelmolen*), which produced fully milled white rice at a fraction of the labour cost of hand-pounding. Chinese entrepreneurs installed the first mechanical rice mills at [[Lhokseumawe|Lho' Seumawè]] and [[Langsa|Langsa]] in the [[1910s]] and [[1920s]], and the cheaper, whiter product rapidly displaced hand-pounded rice in the market.

The colonial medical service warned against the nutritional consequences of this shift and advocated for the use of the partially-dehulled "silver-bran rice" (Dutch: *zilvervliesrijst*) that could be produced by machine mills set to remove only the outer husk. The advice was only partially heeded: by the [[1930s]] machine-milled white rice had become the standard urban Atjeh staple, and beriberi appeared in the towns and plantation labour-lines where it had previously been absent. The traditional hand-pounded *gepelde rijst* survived in the villages of the interior, where it remains the preferred rice of older Acehnese households to this day.
