**Slavernij in Nederlandsch-Indië** (slavery in the Netherlands Indies) was the institution of chattel slavery that persisted in the outer provinces (*Buitengewesten*) of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Netherlands Indies]] throughout the nineteenth century, despite the undertaking given by the Dutch government in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|Verdrag van Londen]] of [[1814]] to combat it with vigour. The institution, together with the associated practices of *zeeroverij* (piracy) and *landroverij* (land-piracy), is the central subject of [[J. Frijling|Ir. J. Frijling]]'s [[1946]] sketch *Een stukje geschiedenis der Indische Buitengewesten*, which traces the failure of the Dutch to suppress slavery from [[1814]] to the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification of Atjeh]] around [[1900]] and identifies the institution as one of the principal causes of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].

## The Undertaking of 1814 and its Non-Fulfilment

By the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|Verdrag van Londen]] of [[1814]], by which the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] recovered most of the colonies that [[Britain|Britain]] had occupied during the Napoleonic period, the Dutch government promised "de slavernij in Ned.-Indië met kracht te zullen tegengaan." Frijling records that "het bleef bij een belofte" — the promise was not kept. The British government's irritation at this failure was, "althans de reden genoemd," the reason why London broke off the [[1820]] conversations with the Hague over the exchange of [[Billiton|Billiton]] for [[Singapore|Singapore]] and twice refused to resume them.

## The Cause of the Failure: *On-macht* rather than *On-wil*

Frijling defends the Dutch failure on the ground of *on-macht* (powerlessness) rather than *on-wil* (ill-will). The [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] of [[1814]], after the French occupation, was almost as poor as it would be in [[1945]] after the German. The territory over which it had recovered sovereignty extended from the 95th to the 142nd degree of longitude east — more than an eighth of the Earth's circumference measured along the equator. The [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie]], whose rights the Dutch state had inherited, had been able to enforce its authority over no more than ten per cent of the total area of the archipelago; over the whole of Java, but in the Buitengewesten only at a few widely separated posts. The army of officials needed for an actual administration was simply not to be had, quite apart from the enormous recurring costs.

## The *Niet-inmengingspolitiek* and its Consequences

Throughout the nineteenth century the Dutch government followed a policy of *[[Niet-inmengingspolitiek|niet-inmenging]]* — non-intervention — in the affairs of the native states of the Buitengewesten. In the absence of effective authority, the native princes and headmen fell into increasing mutual discord, "een strijd van allen tegen allen, leidende tot grenzenloze onderdrukking van bevolkingen." Slavery flourished, and both *zeeroverij* (piracy) and *landroverij* (land-piracy — rampok- and ketjoepartijen, plundering and extortion) assumed unheard-of proportions. Foreign merchant ships were also among the victims; the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]] were direct foreign reprisals against Atjehese piracy.

## The Atjeh War as the Resolution

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] gave the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] a free hand over the whole of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] but laid on it the obligation to suppress the Atjehese slave trade and piracy. The [[Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy (1872)|Dutch decision of 1872]] to take military measures led directly to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]] of [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]] and to the protracted [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]]. The final pacification of Atjeh by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] around the turn of the century, and the subsequent reforms by which Van Heutsz preserved native self-government under a thin layer of properly prepared Dutch administrators, gave the Netherlands for the first time the effective authority over the Buitengewesten that it had lacked since [[1814]] — and with that authority, the means to suppress slavery.
