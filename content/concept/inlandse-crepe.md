**Inlandse Crêpe** (Dutch: *inlandsche crêpe*, literally "indigenous crêpe") referred to the rubber sheets produced locally in the residency of [[Atjeh]] from the tapped [[Latex (Aceh)|latex]] of the *Hevea brasiliensis* rubber tree in the early [[20th century|twentieth century]], and was the characteristic product of the smallholder rubber economy that developed alongside the European plantation sector. The raw latex was coagulated, rolled into sheets, and then smoked to prevent fungal growth, yielding a creased, wrinkled product known as *crêpe* on account of its crinkled surface. Although of lower grade than the plantation rubber (Dutch: *ondernemings-rubber*) produced on the European estates of [[Langsa|Langsa]] and the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]], *inlandse crêpe* fetched a respectable price on the world market and contributed materially to the prosperity of smallholder rubber-growing regions of [[Borneo]], [[Djambi|Djambi]] and [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] in the boom years before [[1930]].

## Manufacturing process

Latex was collected in the small cups (Dutch: *latexbakjes*) hung at the base of each tapping cut on the rubber tree, then carried to the smallholder's processing shed. There the latex was poured into a coagulation vat (Dutch: *coagulatie-bak*) and mixed with a small quantity of formic or acetic acid (Dutch: *mierenzuur* or *azijnzuur*) to separate the rubber particles from the watery serum. After a few hours the coagulum floated to the surface as a soft white slab, which was then passed repeatedly through a hand-cranked sheeting roller (Dutch: *wals*) to expel the residual water and produce a thin, even sheet about two to three millimetres thick.

The freshly rolled sheets were hung on bamboo poles in a smoke-house (Dutch: *rookhuis*) for several days, where the smoke of slow-burning coconut husks and damp wood deposited antiseptic phenols and creosotes on the rubber surface. This smoking was the critical step that distinguished *inlandse crêpe* from the simpler, sun-dried *inlandsche droge vel*: without it, the warm and humid Atjeh climate would have caused the sheets to spoil with mould within days. After smoking, the sheets were folded, pressed into bales of approximately 100 kilograms, and shipped via [[Belawan]] or [[Sabang]] to the rubber brokers of Singapore, Amsterdam and Liverpool.

## Market role and smallholder economy

Although of lower quality than the European-produced plantation rubber — which was carefully washed, milled and baled under factory conditions — the *inlandse crêpe* of the Atjeh smallholder fetched a respectable price on the world market. In the boom year of [[1910]], when Brazilian supply disruptions drove smoked sheet rubber above ten shillings per pound, even low-grade smallholder crêpe from Atjeh earned smallholders their first significant cash windfalls. The trade sustained the prosperity of rubber-growing regions in [[Borneo|Borneo]], [[Djambi|Djambi]] and [[Atjeh]] through the price cycles of the [[1910s]] and [[1920s]], and was a primary driver of the consumer-goods boom that transformed Acehnese village life in those decades.

The Dutch colonial government's response to the smallholder rubber boom was ambiguous. The Stevenson restriction scheme of the [[1920s]], imposed by the British to support rubber prices, was extended to the Netherlands Indies through the [[International Rubber Regulation Agreement]] of [[1934]], which limited smallholder output. The colonial administration regarded the unregulated *inlandse crêpe* trade with suspicion, since it competed directly with the European estates and was believed to encourage tapping of immature trees. Yet it remained the principal smallholder income source of the Atjeh interior through the depression of the [[1930s]].

## Decline and legacy

By the late [[1930s]] the smallholder rubber economy of Atjeh was under pressure both from the international restriction scheme and from competition with the more efficient, factory-processed *pale crêpe* and *ribbed smoked sheet* of the European plantations. The Japanese occupation of [[1942]] and the subsequent Indonesian revolution disrupted the trade further, and many smallholder rubber gardens were abandoned or tapped only intermittently. The surviving smallholder groves nevertheless formed the basis of the post-independence Acehnese rubber economy, and the *inlandse crêpe* tradition — wrinkled, smoked, imperfect, but resilient — remains a symbol of the smallholder agriculture that the European plantation sector could never entirely displace.
