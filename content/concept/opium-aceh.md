**Opium (Aceh)** (Dutch: *opium*) was the prepared narcotic sold under government monopoly in the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]], supplied to Atjeh by the [[Batavia Opium Company|state opium enterprise at Batavia]] (Dutch: *'s Lands-opiumbedrijf te Batavia*) and packaged in tubes (Dutch: *tubes*) for distribution to licensed opium-holders (Dutch: *opium-houders*) across the archipelago. The colonial administration imposed stringent regulations (Dutch: *streng bepalingen*) to limit opium-related danger (Dutch: *opiumgevaar*) and to curb smuggling (Dutch: *smokkelhandel*), and opium sales formed one of the most important revenue lines of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]] residency.

## Background: the pacht system

For centuries the Dutch colonial government had farmed out the right to sell opium at retail to private entrepreneurs — predominantly Chinese — in a system known as the *pacht*. This system incentivised the farmers to maximise consumption through aggressive and often corrupt practices, leading to widespread addiction and smuggling. In Atjeh the *pacht* system was introduced in stages after the [[Aceh War|Aceh War of 1873]]: Stbl. [[1876]] N°. 151 brought Groot-Atjèh under the Sumatra's Oostkust *pachtreglement*, and Stbl. [[1884]] N°. 168 reorganised the *opiumpacht* for Groot-Atjèh. In the Onderhoorigheden, opium was managed through agreements with the indigenous headmen (*hoofden*).

The *pacht* system was notoriously difficult to control in the partially-pacified Atjeh interior. Smugglers moved contraband opium up the rivers and through the forest paths beyond the Dutch lines, and the pachters themselves were often complicit. The Westkust governors issued import licenses to the hoofden — General Van Teijn in the late [[1880s]] authorized 619 chests at f 300 each, and his successor Colonel Pompe van Meerdervoort a further 67 chests — but these legal imports barely dented the illicit traffic.

## The Opiumregie and licensed distribution

In [[1913]] the colonial government replaced the *pacht* system in Atjeh with the [[Opiumregie in Atjeh|Opiumregie]] (government opium monopoly), the last such transition in the Dutch East Indies. Opium was henceforth produced centrally in Batavia by the [[Batavia Opium Company|'s Lands-opiumbedrijf te Batavia]], packaged in standardized tubes (Dutch: *tubes*) of uniform weight and quality, and distributed to licensed opium-holders (Dutch: *opium-houders*) — typically Chinese shopkeepers — who sold it on to registered consumers under tight governmental control.

The regie imposed stringent regulations to limit opium-related danger and to curb smuggling. Licensed holders were required to keep detailed ledgers (Dutch: *opium-registers*) of every sale, recording the name, age and address of the purchaser, the quantity sold, and the date. Government inspectors (Dutch: *controleurs*) visited the sales points without warning to check the books against the actual stock. Smuggling was punished by heavy fines, confiscation of goods, and imprisonment; the colonial administration also maintained the *verboden kringen* (forbidden circles) of [[Gajoland|Gayo Lues]], the Alaslanden and Takèngön, where the Gayo and Alas populations — who did not use opium — were entirely protected from the trade.

## Consumption and control

Local consumption was monitored, and import restrictions were enforced to prevent illicit trade. The presence of opium reflects the broader colonial narcotics policy in the Dutch East Indies, in which the government sought simultaneously to maximise the fiscal revenue from opium and to limit the public health consequences of addiction. The regie system was in this sense a compromise: by centralising production and distribution it eliminated the worst abuses of the *pacht* — adulteration, over-supply, sales to minors — while continuing to draw substantial revenue from a trade that the colonial state was unwilling to suppress outright.

The price of regulated opium was raised progressively — from f 12.50 per thail in [[1913]] to f 20 in May [[1921]] — both to suppress demand and to capture the difference between the regulated price and the price that the illicit market would otherwise have charged. By [[1920]] there were 49 sales points in the Atjeh regie, down from 57 in [[1913]], a slow contraction that reflected both the contracting number of consumers and the gradual spread of the *verboden kringen* policy.

## Significance

Opium was a central commodity of the colonial fiscal system in Atjeh. The total revenue from the opium regie across the Indies reached 10.729 million guilders in [[1913]], and the Atjeh share was a substantial contributor to the [[Landschapskassen|landschapskas]] of the residency. The transition from pacht to regie in [[1913]] also marked the final consolidation of Dutch administrative control over Atjeh after decades of war, and the rigorous supervision of opium sales was one of the principal means by which the colonial state established its presence in the villages of the interior. The opium trade thus stands at the intersection of colonial fiscal policy, public health management, and the long process of pacification that followed the [[Aceh War]].
