**The Civiel Gezaghebber** (Civil Authority Holder) was a colonial administrative position in the Netherlands East Indies, particularly significant in the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] where military officers frequently served in this civil capacity. At the Koeta-Radja meetings ([[19 January]]–[[20 January]] [[1914]]), the position was discussed in the context of the broader bestuursreorganisatie (administrative reform), particularly regarding the proposed fusion of the bestuurskorpsen (administrative corps) of Java/Madoera and the Buitenbezittingen. The Civiel Gezaghebber occupied a distinctive place in the colonial administrative hierarchy, exercising civil authority in territories where the security situation was considered sufficiently uncertain to warrant a military presence in the civil administration. This arrangement was especially characteristic of Atjeh, where the legacy of prolonged military conflict had produced a governance structure in which the boundaries between military and civilian authority were persistently blurred.

The position of Civiel Gezaghebber thus embodied one of the central contradictions of Atjeh's colonial governance: the formal distinction between military and civil administration was maintained in the institutional structure, but in practice the two were deeply intertwined. Military officers serving as Civiel Gezaghebbers brought their military training, military subordinates, and military mindset to what was nominally a civil administrative role. This arrangement had both advantages and disadvantages. On the one hand, it ensured that the civil administration in potentially unstable areas had immediate access to military resources and could respond rapidly to security threats. On the other hand, it tended to militarize the civil administration, applying approaches and attitudes suited to military operations to the more nuanced demands of civilian governance, including revenue collection, dispute resolution, and community development.

## Military Officers in Civil Positions

In Atjeh, Civiel Gezaghebbers administered onderafdeelingen (sub-districts) that were considered to require both civil governance and security oversight. Notable Civiel Gezaghebbers present at the meetings included Majoor [[Th. J. Veltman]] (Pidië), Kapitein [[G. F. B. Watrin]] (XXII Moekims), and [[W. Wessel]] (Tamiang). The presence of these military officers in civil administrative positions was not merely a temporary expedient but a structural feature of Atjeh's governance, reflecting the colonial administration's assessment that certain areas of the Gouvernement remained too insecure to be entrusted to purely civilian administration. The rank of these officers—ranging from Kapitein to Majoor—indicated the seriousness with which the colonial government viewed the security challenges in their respective districts, with higher-ranking officers assigned to areas deemed more volatile.

The use of military officers in these positions reflected the continued militarization of Atjeh's governance, even decades after the initial Dutch conquest, and the perception that certain areas still required military oversight alongside civil administration. This perception was not without justification: resistance to Dutch rule continued in various forms throughout the Gouvernement, from armed insurrection to passive non-compliance, and the colonial administration's ability to maintain control depended in significant part on the deterrent effect of military force. However, the militarization of civil governance also had costs, including the alienation of populations that might otherwise have been more receptive to civilian administration and the distortion of administrative priorities toward security concerns at the expense of development and service delivery.

## Proposed Reforms and Title Changes

Gouverneur [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. N. A. Swart]] asked whether the rank of Civiel Gezaghebber would also be open to military Officers who showed ambition for the position, reflecting concern about career paths for military personnel serving in Atjeh's unique governance structure. This question highlighted the practical challenges of administering a territory where military and civil functions were so deeply intertwined. Military officers who had acquired valuable local knowledge and administrative experience through their service as Civiel Gezaghebbers needed assurance that their career prospects would not be limited by the structural separation of military and civil services. Swart's inquiry suggested that the existing arrangements, while practically necessary, created uncertainties about professional advancement that could discourage talented officers from pursuing these positions.

The proposed reform envisioned renaming the position, with the titles Controleur and Aspirant-Controleur being transferred to Civiel Gezaghebbers to avoid the impression of a "korps van minderen rang" (corps of lower rank). This proposed change was significant because it revealed the colonial administration's awareness of the status anxieties that could undermine the effectiveness of the civil service. The title of Civiel Gezaghebber, while accurately describing the position's function, apparently carried connotations of inferiority relative to the Controleur corps of Java and Madoera, whose members enjoyed higher status and better career prospects within the unified bestuurskorps. By proposing to extend the Controleur title to Civiel Gezaghebbers, the reform aimed to eliminate this perceived disparity and create a more unified and motivated administrative corps, even if the underlying structural differences between Atjeh's governance and that of other regions remained.
