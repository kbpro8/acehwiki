**Arabic loanwords in Acehnese Malay** constitute a corpus of roughly one hundred‑plus Arabic‑derived lexical items that entered the Malay language as used in the Sultanate of [[Aceh]] during the [[early modern period]], most systematically recorded in the 16th‑century *Malay translation of the Burda*. The surviving manuscript (see *[[Malay translation of the Burda]]*) lists about 130 Arabic items, ranging from specialised theological terminology to everyday vocabulary, and they appear embedded in distinctive Acehnese Malay morphological patterns such as the suffix **‑nja**, the suffix **‑kan**, and the prefix **ber‑**. The corpus therefore illustrates the depth of Islamic scholarly influence on Acehnese linguistic and cultural life.

## Corpus and Documentation

The manuscript, dated to the [[16th century]], provides Arabic entries together with Malay glosses (source pages [[92]]–[[97]], especially [[96]]–[[97]]). Examples include:

- [[A. 'ahd|A. 'ahd]] “setia, afspraak, avereenkomst”  
- [[A. al‑hakk|A. al‑hakk]] “nubureatnja, de waarheid van zijn profeetschap”  
- [[A. al‑sirat|A. al‑sirat]] “de smalle brug over de hel, die ten hemel leidt”  

The list also contains core religious terms such as [[Allah]], [[Shaytan]] and [[Shahada]], as well as vernacular items like [[tangkas]] “kwiek paard” and [[tunjuk]] “to show”. Earlier scholarship on the same manuscript identified roughly 130 Arabic items, grouping them into three functional categories (see below).

## Functional Categories

1. **Direct borrowings** – Arabic forms retained because no suitable Malay equivalent existed, e.g. [[ash‑hur al‑hurrum]], [[cibbän]], [[rakham]].
2. **Specialised scholarly terms** – understood mainly by learned readers of Arabic, e.g. [[munsif]], [[muhdath]], [[tahayyur]], [[takallub]].
3. **Generalised loanwords** – Arabic items that became part of everyday Malay with sometimes localized meanings, e.g. [[äkhira]], [[ādjil]], [[darratuhā]], [[cadl]], [[cálam al‑shahāda]], [[casl]], [[badan]], [[djamā‘a]], [[hakika]], [[islām]], [[kalām]], [[lafz]], [[la'ik]], [[latāja]], [[lawh]], [[makhiik]], [[martaba]], [[disa]], [[mushkil]], [[nimma]], [[nisba]], [[sabr]], [[şädik]], [[sakratul‑mutt]], [[sidk]], [[takwä]], [[tazufik]], [[sudhr]], [[şähir]], [[lahir]].

These categories demonstrate the spectrum from highly technical religious language to terms that have been fully naturalised in the Acehnese Malay lexicon.

## Morphological Integration

Acehnese Malay adapts Arabic roots to its own morphological system. The suffix **‑nja** often marks possession, while **‑kan** creates causative forms; the prefix **ber‑** produces verbal nouns. For instance, the Arabic root *ḥaqq* appears as [[hakika]] “truth”, which can take the suffix **‑nya** (rendered as **‑nja**) to become “its truth”. Such adaptations show how Arabic lexical material was not merely borrowed but actively reshaped to fit local grammatical patterns.

## Significance

The integration of Arabic terms into Acehnese Malay facilitated the transmission of Islamic doctrine, legal concepts, and Sufi mysticism, thereby shaping the religious identity of [[Aceh]] and its literary production. The corpus also provides concrete evidence of cultural and scholarly exchange between the Sultanate of Aceh and the wider Islamic world, underscoring the role of translation projects such as the *Malay translation of the Burda* in mediating that exchange.
