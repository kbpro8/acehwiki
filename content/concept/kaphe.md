**Kaphé** (Atjèhnese, from the Arabic *kāfir*, "infidel" or "unbeliever") is the Atjèhnese term for a non-Muslim, historically applied in Atjèh — above all in the period of the [[Atjèh War|Atjèh War]] and the long colonial twilight that followed it — to the Dutch colonial rulers, and, more broadly, to any European or Christian against whom the duty of *[[prang sabi|prang sabi]]* (holy war) might be discharged. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* glosses the term as "infidel; historically applied to non-Muslims, esp. the Dutch," and treats the violent sentiment attaching to it — *kaphé-hatred* — as the central religious-political fact of Atjèhnese society in the years before and during the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion]].

## The kaphé and the Atjèh War

The hatred of the *kaphé* was, in Piekaar's reading, the negative pole of Atjèhnese religious consciousness throughout the colonial period. The great resistance leader [[Teukoe Panglima Pólém|Teukoe Panglima Pólém Sri Moeda Perkasa Moehamad Daoed]] "in his youth the acceptance of the authority of the infidel (Atj. *kaphé*) had been an abomination and the conduct of the holy war (Atj. *prang sabi*) a religious duty." The same mentality survived in isolated regions into the 1930s. In the *sagi* of the [[XXV Moekims|XXV Moekims]] — the country of [[Lhóng|Lhóng]] and [[Tjalang|Tjalang]] — "the distance to the time in which the [[Atjèh War|Aceh War]] called the devout Muslims to the fight against the 'kaphé' (unbeliever), seemed smaller … The standstill of time was reflected in the religious instruction." The [[Lhóng action (1933)|Lhóng action of 1933]] was the last flicker of this militant tradition.

## The F-organisatie and the second eruption

The Japanese invasion reactivated the *kaphé*-hatred. "Only a small number of leaders and militant youth threw themselves into the battle for this reason. Religious motives, *kaphé* (Atj. infidel)-hatred and *[[prang sabi|prang sabi]]* (Atj. holy war) preaching the masses followed the call of the leaders, whatever the further-reaching motive of these leaders may have been." Yet Piekaar insists the hatred was not purely negative: "in this negative fanaticism of Islamic *kaphé*-hatred the positive element of the yearning for freedom, stemming from local Acehnese patriotism or group-consciousness … was as it were latent." It was "so closely … interwoven in the Acehnese national character" that "this positive factor seemed to have receded entirely into the background" during the [[Atjèh War|Atjèh War]].

## The [[Atjèh murder|Atjèh murder]] connection

The *kaphé*-hatred also underwrote the phenomenon of the *[[Atjèh murder|Atjèh murder]]*. The perpetrator, "in this way sought an escape from his difficulties in death, of which he assured himself in advance by perpetrating an attack on an unbeliever and which, in his conviction, would open the gates of paradise for him." A saying in circulation in [[1942]] captured the same logic in compressed form: "*kaphé ploeëng, daja taniOng*" — "the unbelievers have fled, the anti-Christ has arrived" — simultaneously hailing the Japanese as the deliverer from Dutch rule and casting them as a still darker threat.

## The Japanese and the kaphé

The Japanese occupation reversed the polarity of *kaphé*-hatred without extinguishing it. Piekaar records that "the Japanese military, completely unfamiliar with the country and people … would come into conflict with the same Atjèhnese religious fanaticism that had been their ally in driving out the Dutch infidels." From [[1942]] onward the *[[hikajat prang sabi|hikajat prang sabi]]* was forbidden by the new regime, and by [[1943]] the official religious authorities of [[Maibkatra|Maibkatra]] were beginning to place "the positive idea of 'fatherland-love' … alongside the negative idea of '*kaphé*-hatred,' which opened the possibility for a gradual ripening of minds in the direction of a modern nationalism."
