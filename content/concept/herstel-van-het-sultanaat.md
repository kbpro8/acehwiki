**Herstel van het sultanaat** ("restoration of the sultanate") was the Dutch colonial policy proposal — debated from [[1879]] to [[1892]] and intermittently pursued as official strategy — to restore the indigenous sultanate in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] under [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed|Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] as sultan, within the framework of Dutch sovereignty, as the means of obtaining the submission of the [[Atjehers|Atjehers]] and ending the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. The proposal was the subject of a celebrated article by [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]], "Het herstel van het sultanaat in Atjeh," published in the *Indische Gids* of [[December]] [[1886]]; was supported by successive ministers of colonies including [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]], [[Minister van Bloemen Waanders|Minister van Bloemen Waanders]], and [[Minister Sprenger van Eijk|Minister Sprenger van Eijk]]; and was adopted as official policy during the governorship of [[General van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] ([[1886]]–[[1891]]). It was rejected by [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in his [[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|1893 brochure]] on grounds that the present sultan's lineage had never possessed real authority, had been placed on the throne by bribery, and was opposed by the chiefs who would be compelled to support it against their own interests.

## The Proposal

The proposal for the restoration of the sultanate arose from the perceived impossibility of obtaining submission by military force after the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration]] of [[1884]]. Three options were identified by [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]] when he became governor in [[March]] [[1883]]:
1. Concentration in a strongly fortified position with a waiting attitude — which he rejected as the greatest possible error.
2. Renewed aggression to recreate the position of [[1880]], followed by a new conciliatory approach and contact with the sultan — which he regarded as the best option but could not recommend because the government had indicated that neither the army nor the finances permitted further expansion of the military force.
3. Immediate attempt to bring the sultan, with or without his guardian, over to the Dutch side, thereby depriving the irreconcilable chiefs of their rallying point and the means by which they brought the common man into the field against the Dutch — which remained as the only practicable option.

Although Tobias did not mention the restoration in his initial letter of June [[1883]] to [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed|Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]], the idea of restoring the sultanate was already his intention. In [[August]] [[1883]], during a visit by [[Governor-General 's Jacob|Gouv. Gen. 's Jacob]], the Minister of Colonies, [[Minister van Bloemen Waanders|van Bloemen Waanders]], raised the same idea. In the first half of [[October]] [[1883]], the Governor of Atjeh wrote to [[Tekoe Nanta|Tekoe Nanta]] of the [[VI Moekims|VI Moekims]] (also called [[Tekoe Lampadang|Tekoe Lampadang]]) hinting that if the chiefs of the war party submitted, a restoration of the sultanate under Mohamad Daoed might be possible — the first indication the Atjehers received from the Dutch side about the possibility of restoring their old form of government under Dutch sovereignty.

The idea was subsequently rejected by the Indian government after the departure of Minister van Bloemen Waanders in [[December]] [[1883]], but was revived by [[Minister Sprenger van Eijk|Minister Sprenger van Eijk]] in [[1886]], supported by [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] (who had been advocating a central indigenous administration in Groot-Atjeh since [[1879]]), [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] (whose letter, published by [[L. W. A. Kessler|L. W. A. Kessler]] in the *Nieuwe Rott. Cour.* of [[29 September]] [[1886]], indicated he was not opposed but wished to remove the obstacle that many considered to stand in the way of re-introduction), and [[Governor-General van Rees|Gouv. Gen. van Rees]] (who was also not against the principle). [[General van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]], who assumed the governorship at the end of [[1886]], was instructed to proceed in the direction of restoration of the sultanate, with the principle as his basis, making contact with [[Kemala|Kemala]] gradually while avoiding the appearance that the Dutch sought it.

## Van Heutsz's Rejection

[[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], who had served as Chief of Staff under Van Teijn and had discussed the question extensively with him (both in Atjeh and in correspondence after Van Teijn's departure for the Netherlands), rejected the restoration on the following grounds:

1. **The present lineage was of Bugis origin and had never possessed power.** It descended from two Bugis brothers (*Daengs*) from [[Wadjo|Wadjo]] on [[Celebes|Celebes]]; one of them, [[Daeng Mancoer Boegis|Daeng Mancoer Boegis]], had been wrecked on [[Poeloe Bras|Poeloe Bras]] and brought to [[Loong|Loong]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]], where he prospered. His son [[Tekoe Loöng|Tekoe Loöng]] had married a granddaughter of [[Soetan Mogal|Soetan Mogal]]; their offspring [[Maharadja Lela Malajoe|Maharadja Lela Malajoe]] had secured the throne for his son [[Sultan Alaïdin Achmad Sjah|Potjoet Din]] by bribery — the [[Djenamoe Atjeh|Djenamoe Atjeh]] of thirty-two *katies* of gold. The lineage "never possessed power in the realm."

2. **The lineage had been placed on the throne by bribery, not by hereditary succession or force of arms.** The first sultan of the present line, Alaïdin Achmad Sjah, had been elected because his father bribed the principal chiefs. This meant that the sultanate was a purchased position, not a natural one.

3. **The sultan was destitute when the Dutch arrived.** At the beginning of the Aceh War, [[Sultan Machmoed Sjah|Sultan Machmoed Sjah]] — a "nothing-signifying, of nearly all authority and influence deprived, besides nearly reduced to poverty" sultan — was found on the throne. The last legitimate male descendant of Sultan Alaïdin Djauhar Alam (Toeankoe Woed) had died with [[Sultan Ibrahim Mancoer Sjah|Sultan Ibrahim]] in [[1870]]; the last sultan, Mohamad Daoed, and [[Toeankoe Abdul Madjid|Toeankoe Abdul Madjid]] and [[Toeankoe Hoesin|Toeankoe Hoesin]] were all descended from *[[goendiq|goendiqs]]* (secondary wives of lower rank).

4. **The chiefs did not want a sultan.** In [[1874]], after the death of Sultan Machmoed, the chiefs did not choose any sultan at all — not even [[Toeankoe Haschim|Toeankoe Haschim]], the strongest candidate. In [[1878]], when Haschim finally secured Mohamad Daoed's election, the chiefs chose a child who could not govern, "clearly indicating that they themselves, each in his own territory, wished to hold the reins."

5. **The coastal chiefs would oppose a sultan under Dutch protection.** The chiefs of the principal coastal states — particularly [[Pedir|Pedir]] — had explicitly stated that they did not want a sultan under Dutch protection. The head of Pedir reasoned that a sultan under Dutch protection, even without direct authority over the dependencies, would exercise indirect influence and draw income from them, and those who refused to pay would be kept in disfavour with the government through false reports from the sultan. The chiefs would thus have to "pay double."

6. **The restoration would merely play into [[Toeankoe Haschim|Haschim]]'s hand.** By seeking the sultan's submission, the Dutch had already confirmed what Haschim wished to prove: that the sultan was the centre around which everything turned. Restoring him would strengthen the Kemala party and give the resistance a legitimate rallying point.
