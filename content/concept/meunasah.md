**Meunasah** (from Arabic *madrasah*, via Malay *surau*) was the village chapel of [[Great Acheh|Acheh]], the communal building at the heart of every [[Gampong|gampong]]. It served multiple functions simultaneously — a sleeping-place for men, a rest-house for strangers, a house of prayer, and a place of assembly for village affairs.

## Functions

**Religious:** The meunasah was where the village held daily prayers (the Friday midday service requiring a larger [[Meusigit|meusigit]] — a proper mosque — if sufficient worshippers could be assembled). It housed the furniture needed for religious litanies (*ratébs*), including kettledrums (*rapa'i* or *rapana*) and wooden rings known as *pulèt*. A large lamp (*kandé*) was lit on the nights of the fasting month.

**Social and political:** The affairs of the [[Gampong|gampong]] were debated in the meunasah. Village festivals were held in its courtyard, [[Kanduri|kanduris]] (religious feasts) organized, and marriage contracts concluded. A person of rank arriving unexpectedly would go first to the meunasah and announce his arrival from there.

**Accommodation:** It served as the regular sleeping place for the men of the gampong — since in Acehnese domestic custom the husband typically slept away from his wife's house during most of his marriage. Strangers passing through were housed there.

## Physical Description

The meunasah was generally a wooden building on posts. Some were built not on posts but on a raised stone foundation finished on top with cement, with stone stairs giving access. Such more imposing stone structures were called *déah* (from Arabic *zāwiyah*). The building contained a masonry niche (*mehrab* or *merab*) to indicate the direction of [[Mecca]]. Its courtyard was sometimes surrounded by a low stone wall.

## Where the Headless Village

Where the heads of the village were not both pious and watchful, the meunasah was apt to become the scene of all manner of Acehnese iniquities. In its courtyard fights of cocks and other fighting birds were held; within the building gambling went on and other vice was shamefully practised at night.

## Additional account from *De Islaam in Indonesië* ([[1947]]) — the *rangkang*

The meunasah is also known in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] by the alternative name **[[Rangkang|rangkang]]** (in [[R. L. Mellema|Mellema]]'s spelling also *rangkng*), recorded by [[R. L. Mellema|R. L. Mellema]] in his comparative ethnological survey *[[De Islaam in Indonesië (Mellema, 1947)|De Islaam in Indonesië (in het bijzonder op Java)]]* (Amsterdam, [[1947]]). Mellema introduces the term in his chapter on *Godsdienstonderwijs in Indonesië* (religious education in Indonesia), where he describes the trajectory of the *haddji* — the pilgrim returned from [[Mecca]] — who, having spent some years in the holy city in the study of the law and the faith, might settle as *goeroe* (religious teacher) and give elementary instruction in the village prayer-house. He then enumerates the regional names of that building across the archipelago:

> "in midden-Java langgar geheten, in Soenda 'tajjeg' in midden-Sumatra 'soerau' en in Atjeh bekend als 'meunasah' of 'rangkng'."

That is: in middle-[[Java|Java]] called *langgar*, in [[Sunda|Soenda]] *tajjeg*, in middle-[[Sumatra|Sumatra]] *soerau*, and in Atjeh known as *meunasah* or *rangkang*. The double naming in Atjeh — *meunasah* and *rangkang* — suggests either a regional variation within Atjeh or a functional distinction (the building as chapel versus the building as school) that Mellema does not further elaborate.

Mellema notes that all buildings of this class — *langgar*, *tajjeg*, *soerau*, *meunasah*, *rangkang* — are recognisable by "het spitte, gestapelde dak" (the steep, tiered roof), the characteristic stacked-roof form that Mellema, earlier in the survey, traces back to the pre-Islamic Indonesian community-house (the *balai* or *bale*). The *rangkang* thus shares with the *meunasah* the architectural lineage that connects the village prayer-house to the older Indonesian tradition of the piled, tiered-roof communal building. The comparative enumeration is the Mellema-source counterpart to [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s monographic account of the meunasah in *[[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|De Atjehers]]* ([[1893]]–[[1894]]), and it furnishes the alternative name *rangkang* that Snouck's monograph does not foreground.
