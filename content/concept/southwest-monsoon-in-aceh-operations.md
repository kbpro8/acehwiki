**Southwest Monsoon in Aceh Operations** was the annual meteorological phenomenon—historically referred to in Dutch colonial literature as the southwest monsoon (bad monsoon) (bad monsoon) or west monsoon—which brought violent storms, rough surf, and torrential rainfall to the northern coast of the [[Aceh Sultanate]] between April and October. The monsoon exerted a defining influence on the timing and outcome of European military invasions.

## Impact on the First Expedition

In his [[1875]] critique of the [[Dutch East Indies Government]], General [[G. G. Booms]] identified the approaching [[Southwest Monsoon in Aceh Operations]] as a primary cause of the defeat of the [[First Aceh Expedition]] in April [[1873]]. 

Because the expedition did not debark at [[Meuraxa]] until [[8 April]] [[1873]], commanders General [[J. H. R. Köhler]] and General [[E. C. van Daalen]] faced a strict three-week window before the May monsoon rendered coastal landings and communication with the naval squadron impossible. Following Köhler's death on [[14 April]] [[1873]], van Daalen ordered a withdrawal after [[16 April]] [[1873]] precisely to avoid having his army cut off and inundated by monsoon floods.

## Planning for Subsequent Campaigns

To evade the hazards of the southwest monsoon (bad monsoon), the [[Dutch East Indies Government]] scheduled the landing of the [[Second Aceh Expedition]] under General [[J. van Swieten]] for December [[1873]], during the favorable northeast monsoon. 

Later, when General [[J. L. J. H. Pel]] organized [[General Pel's Campaign in Aceh]] in [[1875]] and [[1876]], operations against the [[IV Moekims]], [[VI Moekims]], and [[XXII Moekims]] were timed for the dry winter months to ensure stable supply lines across the Acehnese coastal plain.
