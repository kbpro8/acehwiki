**Beuming's account of the origin of the Atjeh-oorlog** was the introductory chapter of *[[Schetsen uit den strijd op Groot-Atjeh (1911)|Schetsen uit den strijd op Groot-Atjeh]]* ([[1911]]), in which [[P. H. R. Beuming]] set out for the Dutch reading public what he held to be *de ware oorzaak* — the true cause — of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. It is a soldier's apologia rather than a critical history, and stands at the opposite pole from the socialist indictment of the war delivered in the [[Tweede Kamer]] in [[1897]]; see [[Van Kol's refutation of the causes of the Atjeh-oorlog (1897)]].

## Occasion

Beuming explains that when the non-commissioned officers sat together in *de groote pijp* at [[Koeta Radja]] — the sergeants' club-room — it had sometimes struck him that some among them *zich feitelijk geen goede voorstelling konden maken omtrent het ontstaan van den eigenlijken Atjeh-oorlog*. Returned to the mother country, he found this to be the case in a very high degree among his countrymen. Many, he wrote, still believed *dat de expeditie naar Atjeh louter ten doel had ons koloniaal gebied uit te breiden* — that the expedition to Atjeh had merely aimed at extending Dutch colonial territory. This conception he declared wholly incorrect, adding that had the government of the day not been compelled by circumstances, *zij zich wel tweemaal zou bedacht hebben, alvorens te besluiten tot dezen onzaligen veldtocht* — it would have thought twice before resolving on this ill-starred campaign.

## The argument from piracy

Beuming's case rests on Acehnese piracy. As the Norsemen were once the terror of [[Europe]], he wrote, so the Acehnese had for many centuries been the terror of all [[Sumatra]] and the surrounding islands. Already in the days when the [[Portuguese]] traded with the Indies they were notorious and feared for their bold piracies, and many a richly laden merchantman fell as booty into their hands. With their slender fast craft they even made landings on the coast of neighbouring [[Malacca|Malakka]], plundered the coastal places and carried off the inhabitants as slaves. Atjeh, he concluded, had thus already in earlier centuries marked itself as *een echte roofstaat* — a true robber state — and possessed great influence in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

He records that at the end of the seventeenth century the [[Sultan of Atjeh]] even attempted to drive the Dutch from the [[Westkust van Sumatra|west coast of Sumatra]] and concluded an alliance with [[Siam]] to that end; after the failure of these attempts there arose, as it were by tradition, a hostile disposition against the [[Netherlands]] that constantly sought expression.

## The treaty sequence

The narrative then follows the familiar treaty sequence. In consequence of the numerous piracies the Netherlands bound itself in [[1824]] towards [[England]] to make an arrangement with the Sultan of Atjeh for the security of trade and navigation, while preserving Atjeh's independence — see [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]]. Such an arrangement was first made in [[1857]], but brought the Dutch no step further, since the relation between the Dutch and the Sultan remained strained and hostile. The Dutch government could apply no effective means to curb the piracy, because it had to respect Atjeh's independence.

This obligation was lifted by the [[Sumatra Tractaat (1871)|Sumatra treaty]] concluded with England in [[1871]], by which England renounced its claims on Atjeh and received in exchange the coast of [[Guinea]]. When all attempts to bring the Sultan to better views by diplomatic means failed, and when it was learned that envoys of the Sultan had been at [[Singapore]] with the consuls of [[United States of America|America]] and [[Italy]] to seek help against the Dutch, the forbearance of the Dutch government was at an end and war was declared on the Sultan on [[26 March]] [[1873]].

## The war narrative

Beuming passes rapidly over the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first expedition]] under General [[J. H. R. Köhler|Köhler]], whose sad course he holds sufficiently known — the failed attack on the *[[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]]*, the storming and abandonment of the great *[[Meusigit Raja|missigit]]*, and Köhler's death, *één groot échec*, after which on [[29 April]] [[1873]] the troops were forced to leave the Acehnese coast.

The [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second expedition]] under General [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] had complete success: after stubborn resistance the great *missigit* fell again into Dutch hands on [[6 January]] [[1874]] and on [[23 April]] the formidably strong *Kraton* was conquered; after a series of bloody engagements Dutch territory extended to the [[Kroeng Raba Bay|Kroeng Raba bay]].

After changes of military commanders General [[Karel van der Heijden|Karel van der Heijden]] came to the government, and from his time date the celebrated marches to the [[XXII Moekims]] and [[XXVI Moekims]] and to [[Samalanga|Samalangan]]. Under his administration, Beuming holds, Atjeh was in fact already subjected and the war party crippled.

## The civil interlude and the drama

Beuming's sharpest passage concerns the civil governors. When in [[1881]] Van der Heijden was replaced by the civil governor [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]], *brak het tijdstip aan van het treurige Atjeh-drama* — the period of the sad Atjeh drama began. The war party acted everywhere with boldness again, so that it was thought advisable to abolish the garrisons of [[Masdjid Indrapoeri]], [[Pakan Badak]], [[Kroeng Raba]] and others. Thus the territory won with so much effort and at the cost of so many lives grew gradually smaller, and when in [[1882]] Pruijs van der Hoeven was succeeded by the civil governor [[P. F. Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]], the success achieved by Van der Heijden was wholly lost.

Being of opinion that they had to do not with a fighting population but with bands of malcontents, the authorities forbade the troops to act, and this notwithstanding that Dutch posts and patrols were continually fired on from the so-called friendly *kampongs*.

Beuming is careful to distribute the blame: when at last the proof was furnished that *wij door de zegeningen van het civiel bestuur leelijk in 't moeras geraakt waren* — the blessings of civil government had landed the Dutch badly in the swamp — this was *niet moet worden geweten aan een te kort in bekwaamheid der civiele gouverneurs, maar aan het dualisme in het bestuurstelsel*, not to be ascribed to any deficiency in the ability of the civil governors but to the dualism in the system of administration.

In [[1883]] the administration was again entrusted to a military commander, Colonel [[H. D. Demmeni]], who immediately followed a policy of combating the war party. The struggle on [[Groot-Atjeh]], Beuming observes, gradually acquired a more religious character, since the priestly party had taken over the leadership of the *[[Prang Sabil|Prang Sabil]]*, which he glosses simply as *Heilige oorlog*, holy war.

Through many unfavourable influences under successive governors, the Dutch were by [[1892]] masters only of a small strip of terrain outside Koeta Radja, secured by a chain of permanent *[[Benting|bentings]]* connected by a steam tram — the *[[Geconcentreerde linie|geconcentreerde linie]]*.

## The closing illustration

Beuming closes the introduction with the narrative of the [[Raijah piracy (20 July 1893)|Raijah piracy]] of [[20 July]] [[1893]] as *een enkel staaltje... van de driestheid der Atjehsche zeeroovers*, holding that with it the audacity of the Acehnese was sufficiently demonstrated.

## Assessment

The chapter is valuable less as history than as evidence of what a Dutch non-commissioned officer of the period had been taught and believed about the war he fought. Its central claims — that the war was forced on the Netherlands by piracy and by the [[1824]] obligation, and that the civil interlude of [[1881]]–[[1883]] squandered a victory already won — were the standard apologia of the military party, and may be set against the contrary case argued in the [[Atjeh debate in the Tweede Kamer (16-17 November 1897)|Atjeh debate]] of [[1897]].
