**Kanduri** (also *kenduri*, *kauduris*; from Persian *qandūrī*, cognate with the Malay *kenduri* and the Arabic *qandūriyya*) is the general [[Achehnese|Acehnese]] term for a religious feast — the communal meal held on the occasion of religious holidays, life-cycle events (birth, [[peutron|first contact with the earth]], [[peusunat|circumcision]], marriage, death), the completion of a house, the safe return from a journey, seasonal feasts, and in fulfilment of vows (*nazar*). It was the most characteristic social institution of Acehnese Islam and the form in which the great majority of collective religious observances found their expression. Small *kanduris* are of very common occurrence in every [[gampong|gampong]], and some require the presence of the [[teungku|teungku]]; the word covers both the periodic feasts of the ecclesiastical year and occasional feasts given at the transitions of life.

## Definition and Religious Character

As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, the *kanduri* is distinguished from a purely secular meal by a formal religious element: it is marked by the recitation of selections from the [[Quran|Quran]] or of litanies, followed by a final prayer, after which the assembled company partake of the food. A feast offered to specially honoured guests which lacks this religious consecration is called a *peujamè* (penjamee), or "guest-meal," and is "not in any sense of religious character." The distinction between *kanduri* and *peujamè* runs through the whole etiquette of Acehnese hospitality: even where the proximate occasion of a feast is a secular one — a wedding, a safe return, the completion of a house — the presence of the *teungku* and the recitation of the Quran transform it into a *kanduri*, while the absence of these would leave it a mere *peujamè*.

## Food at a Kanduri

At *kauduris* (religious feasts) and suchlike occasions, **glutinous rice** (*bu kunyèt*) coloured yellow with turmeric was the favourite dish and the principal offering. To this were always added either *tumpoë* (a sort of pancakes, six or seven of which were laid on top of the rice) and *cheuncuruët* (a gelatinous network formed of the same kind of rice), or else grated coconut mixed with red sugar (*u mirah*), or long strips of stockfish boiled in coconut milk, called *keumamaih teunaguën*. At smaller feasts the yellow rice forms the *pièce de résistance*, though a goat is sometimes slaughtered for the guests; otherwise buffaloes, oxen, goats and sheep are seldom killed except at the great annual festivals. Tea and coffee are also served on such occasions, though the use of these beverages is generally restricted to invalids.

At weddings, funeral feasts, receptions of distinguished guests, and other ceremonious occasions, it was customary to serve up the rice and its accessories in a definite traditional manner on *dalöngs* (trays). This manner of service was called *meu'idang*: after the rice, fish, and *gulè* came the tray of sweetmeats, containing a dish of glutinous rice (*bu leukat*) without turmeric, and a dish of *pisang peungat* (ripe plantains sliced thin and boiled with cloves, cinnamon, sugar, and pandan-leaves), often with *srèykaya* (eggs with coconut milk and herbs, well cooked by steaming). The food is prepared by the women of the household or, at larger community feasts, from general contributions under the supervision of the *teungku*, and is brought in *idangs* (trays) to the guests assembled in the *seuramoe Mòë* (the front verandah of an Acehnese house, to which the uninitiated are admitted), or in the [[meunasah|meunasah]] when the feast is given by the *gampong*.

## The Calendar of Monthly Kanduris

Each of the lunar months has its characteristic *kanduri*, which gives the month its popular Acehnese name. The succession includes the [[Kanduri Mo'lot|Kanduri Mo'lot]] (Rabi' al-awwal), the [[Kanduri Apam|Kanduri Apam]] (Rajab), the [[Kanduri Bu|Kanduri Bu]] (Sha'ban — "Rice-kanduri," the popular name of the eighth month), the [[Puasa (Ramadhan fasting month)|Puasa]] (Ramadhan) and the *Leubè raya* that closes it, the Kanduri Apam Seulawah or Kanduri Boh Kayèë in Jumada al-akhir, and the [[Kurubeu'en|Kurubeu'en]] (Dul-hidjah). These monthly *kanduris* are given in the *meunasah*, where the *teungku* recites the appropriate formulas — *meuliku* from the *dikr* at the Mo'lot, the *Barzanji* in honour of the Prophet's birth, and the like — before the assembled householders distribute the food. A specially great *kanduri* is held at the tomb of [[Teungku Anjong|Teungku Anjong]] in [[Gampong Jawa|Gampong Jawa]] on the 12th of Maulid.

Other annual *kanduris* are tied to the agricultural calendar — the *kanduri blang* or "field-feast" that opens the [[Musem ludih blang (Season of the freedom of the land)|musem ludih blang]], and the *kanduri laot* or "sea-kanduri" of the seine-fishers. Each *gampong* also holds its own *kanduri* for the dead, occasional as well as calendrical, and the village *teungku* is the indispensable consecrator.

## Occasional Kanduris and Vows

Beyond the calendar, *kanduris* are given whenever a religious motive invites one: at the conclusion of a Quran recitation (*peutamat daroh*), on the appointment of a *panglima kawom* or *uleebalang*, at the [[Peusijuek (cooling ceremony)|peusijuek]] or "cooling" of a new house or a returned traveller, and as vows (*kaoy* or *nazar*) for the recovery of the sick. The *kanduri* held in honour of the dead — called *kanduri bu* — gives to the eighth month of the Acehnese year ([[Sha'ban|Sha'ban]]) its popular name "Rice-kanduri."

The official invitation (*meuròh*) to a *kanduri* is always given in the form of an offering of *ranub batèë* (betel in a copper or silver bowl lined with ornamental cloth), and, where the invitation is addressed to persons of high consideration such as *tuankus*, the symbolical gift is presented in a more costly *sirih*-bowl (*keureudong*) in place of the *batèë*. Snouck Hurgronje notes that [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] was rebuked by his fellow *ulamas* for lavishing on *kanduris* and on the repair of chapels money that, in their view, should have been used to erect forts along the *linie* and to fit out a fleet to harass the Dutch by sea — an indication of how central the institution had become to Acehnese religious and political life alike.

## Religious and Social Significance

The *kanduri* was embedded in the religious calendar. On the *ureë raya* (Eid feast day, first of Shawwal after Ramadan), many went to visit their family burial places (*bhöm*), decorating the head of the tomb with flowers (*jeumpa*, *seumanga*) and the *bungöng peukan* ("market-flowers"), burning incense and offering prayers. The *kanduri* at the *meunasah* was also a setting for the *ratéb saman* — the terribly noisy religious litany derived from [[Rateb Saman|the saint Samman]] — and other communal religious activities.

As a social institution, the *kanduri* expresses at once the commensality of the Muslim community and the Acehnese idea that no important transition — birth, [[peutron|first contact with the earth]], [[peusunat|circumcision]], marriage, voyage, sickness, death — is complete without a religious feast. The *kanduri* thus occupies in Acehnese life a place midway between the prescribed liturgy of Islam and the purely customary usages of the country.
