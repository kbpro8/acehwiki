**Mukim** was the intermediate administrative and religious unit of [[Great Acheh|Acehnese]] society, sitting between the individual [[Gampong|gampong]] (village) and the [[Uleebalang|uleebalangship]] (territory). It was essentially a Friday-mosque association — a grouping of [[Gampong|gampongs]] lying sufficiently close to one another to form a single congregation for the Friday midday prayer.

## Origin

When a number of gampongs lay sufficiently close to one another to admit of their being united into a single Friday association in accordance with the behests of Islamic law, they constructed a [[Meusigit|mosque]] (*meusigit*), choosing the most central possible site. This might fall within one of the gampongs so united, or in a place lying without all the gampong enclosures. For some of these associations the term "district" would be the most applicable name; for others, whose gampongs rather resembled "wards," the term "parish" corresponded most exactly — this being the comparison drawn by [[R.J. Wilkinson]] and adopted also in [[Penang]] and Province Wellesley under British administration.

## The Imeum Mukim

The head of the mukim was the **[[Imeum Mukim]]** (*imeum*, from Arabic *imam*), who administered the secular as well as religious affairs of the mukim. He was the link between the [[Keuchi'|keuchi']] (village headman) below and the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] above, and was responsible for the maintenance of order across all the gampongs in his mukim.

## The Three Sagis

[[Great Acheh]] was divided into three major groupings of mukims — the [[Sagi System|sagis]]:
- The **XXII Mukims** — the highland sagi
- The **XXV Mukims** — on the left bank of the [[Acheh River]]
- The **XXVI Mukims** — on the right bank of the [[Acheh River]]

## Use of the Term Elsewhere

[[R.J. Wilkinson]] noted that in [[Penang]] and Province Wellesley the English Government had adopted the word *mukim* to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes — corresponding almost exactly to "parish." In the Federated Malay States the word had the same modified meaning as in Acheh.

The word *mukim* in Kedah bore the same modified meaning as in Acheh. Newbold (*British Settlements in the Straits of Malacca* II:20) observed that in the former place each mukim consisted originally of at least 44 families, a clear indication of the original intent of this territorial subdivision.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Moekim

**The moekim** was the intermediate territorial and religious unit of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and the surrounding Atjehsche lands, defined as the *Vrijdagskring* — the Friday-service circle — formed by a group of [[Gampong|gampongs]] around a common *meuseugit* (mosque) where the obligatory Friday congregational prayer was held. *De Buitenbezittingen* follows Prof. Dr. [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s treatment in *De Atjehers* (Vol. I, p. 84 e.v.) in describing the moekim as the link between the [[Gampong|gampong]] and the *oelèëbalangschap*, a constitutionally significant unit whose head, the [[Imeum|imeum]], often rivalled the territorial chief himself.

## Religious origin

The institution arose, the source explains, once Islam had taken root in Atjeh. The Shafiʿite school — "ook in Atjeh gehuldigd" — required for the Friday service the presence of at least forty (modified in Atjehsche practice to forty-four) "mannelijke, vrije, meerderjarige moekims" — male, free, adult congregants. To meet this requirement, heads who sought the support of the clergy — "hoofden die den steun van de geestelijkheid zochten en soms vrome rijke lieden" — saw to it that a *meuseugit* was built "op een centraalpunt" for several neighbouring gampongs (originally as a rule four), so that the prescribed number of worshippers could assemble within the lawful walking distance. The word *moekim* itself means only "ingezetene" — inhabitant — but it came to denote the entire congregational district.

## The Imeum and the drift to adat

At the head of the moekim stood the [[Imeum|imeum]], whose original task was "eene geheel of grootendeels godsdienstige taak" — wholly or mostly religious: ensuring that Allah's law was upheld and the ritual prescriptions of Islam observed, doing for the *meuseugit* what the [[Teungkoe|Teungkoe meunasah]] did for the village prayer-house. Yet the institution soon degenerated — "het ambt der Imeums spoedig geheel ontaard" — as imeums strove "om over de gampongs, die tot hun Vrijdagskring behoorden, overal de macht van een oelèëbalang toe te eigenen." Many became "adathoofden zonder eenig godsdienstig karakter" — adat chiefs without any religious character — and some "hebben het zelfs geheel tot de zelfstandigheid gebracht" — achieved full independence from the [[Oelèëbalang|oelèëbalang]].

## Constitutional position

Under a strong oelèëbalang, the imeum was "niet veel meer dan tusschenpersoon" — little more than an intermediary — but under a weak one an energetic imeum could place himself, "zooveel zijn eigen gebied betreft, bijna geheel in diens plaats." The moekim, like the [[Gampong|gampong]], was retained in the Dutch administrative reorganisation as a cadastral and ecclesiastical subdivision: the three [[Sagi|sagi]]'s of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] were named for their nominal moekim counts — the XXII, XXVI, and XXV Moekims — designations "behouden gebleven ook nadat door toename van de bevolking, het aantal moekims is toegenomen."
