**Islam in Aceh** denotes the historical development, practice, and cultural expression of the Islamic faith within the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Sultanate of [[Acheh]]]] from the early second millennium CE through the colonial period. [[Acheh]] was one of the earliest territories in the Indonesian Archipelago to adopt Islam, a fact attested by both archaeological evidence — Mohammedan tombstones with inscriptions from the fifteenth century — and the literary tradition as noted in the [[Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas (1926)|*Nederlandsch Indië Platen Atlas*]] ([[1926]]). From this early Islamisation Aceh grew into a major centre of Islamic learning, Sufi orders, and devotional literature, exemplified by the adoption of Arabic works such as the [[Burda (poem)|Burda of al-Busiri]] in Malay translation (see [[Malay translation of the Burda (16th century)]]), and by the seventeenth-century orthodox Sufi revival of [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]].

## Early Islamisation

Islam entered [[Aceh]] via trade routes from the Arabian Peninsula and [[Gujarat]]. The atlas, in its chapter on religion, addresses the early spread of Islam in the Archipelago:

> *"Uit gegevens uit reisverhalen en Mohammedaansche grafsteenen met opschriften uit de 15e eeuw valt af te leiden dat de Islam destijds reeds vasten voet had gevat, zoowel op de N.O. kust van Sumatra (de Moslimsche rijkjes Perlak en Pasé in omstreeks 1500) als kort daarna op de N.O. kust van Java. Dat de streken, die reeds vroegtijdig werden bekeerd, behooren o.a. de Molukken, Atjeh en Palembang, terwijl ook de Menangkabausche Maleiers reeds in oude tijden tot den Islam moeten zijn overgegaan."*

(From data in travel accounts and Mohammedan tombstones with inscriptions from the fifteenth century, it can be deduced that Islam had by then already gained a firm footing, both on the N.E. coast of Sumatra — the Muslim principalities of [[Perlak]] and [[Pasé]] around [[1500]] — and shortly thereafter on the N.E. coast of Java. Among the regions that were converted at an early date are, inter alia, the [[Moluccas]], Atjeh, and [[Palembang]], while the [[Menangkabau|Minangkabau]] Malays must also have converted to Islam in ancient times.)

By the early fourteenth century, Achehnese rulers claimed the title *Sultan* and patronised *madrasahs* (religious schools) that taught Arabic, Persian, and Malay. The specific mention of [[Perlak]] and [[Pasé]] as Muslim principalities (*Moslimsche rijkjes*) on the north-east coast of Sumatra around [[1500]] provides a terminus ante quem for the Islamisation of the broader region. The presence of Muslims in Perlak and Pasé by that date suggests that Acheh itself, lying adjacent to these sultanates, was already in the process of conversion or had recently converted.

The atlas also notes that the Islam that reached the Archipelago came via Persia and Hindustan and was already modified by the customs and thought-patterns of the peoples of India, with which the Indonesians were already partly familiar through the earlier influence of Hinduism. This "adaptability" (*aanpassingsvermogen*) of Islam is cited as a reason for its rapid spread.

## Sufi Influence and Devotional Poetry

The sixteenth-century Malay translation of the [[Burda (poem)|Burda]] illustrates the integration of Arabic devotional poetry into Achehnese religious life. The poem's themes — such as the Prophet's supremacy over *jinn* and humans (page 54) and the call to resist *shaitan* (page 53) — were incorporated into local *tazkirah* (devotional instruction) and *qasidah* (devotional ode) recitals.

In the seventeenth century, the Sultanate of Acheh became the principal stage of the orthodox Sufi revival led by [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī]], who arrived in Acheh in [[1637]] and there claimed the authority of the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]] *silsila* (spiritual genealogy) — transmitted to him by his *shaikh* [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]] at [[Surat]] — against the [[Wujudiyyah]] mystics of the Achehnese court, [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]. The Rifāʿiyya, founded by [[Ahmad ar-Rifa'i|Aḥmad ar-Rifāʿī]] in the marshlands of southern Iraq in the twelfth century, gave Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī the sanction of a *ṭarīqa* (mystic order) that had its origin in the heartlands of Sunni Islam.

## Institutional Structures

- **Madrasahs** — centres of Qur'anic and Arabic studies where texts like the *Burda* were taught.
- **Zawiyahs** — Sufi lodges that used the *Burda* for *dhikr* (remembrance) sessions.
- **Royal Patronage** — Achehnese sultans commissioned translations of key Arabic works to legitimise their rule and promote piety.

## Significance

The atlas's classification of Atjeh among the earliest Islamic regions of the Archipelago is consistent with the scholarly consensus that North Sumatra was the gateway through which Islam entered the Indonesian world. From this early foothold, Acheh grew into one of the principal Muslim sultanates of the archipelago — the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Sultanate]] that under [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] became, in the seventeenth century, the foremost Muslim power of the Indonesian archipelago — and into a major centre of Islamic learning, Sufi devotional literature, and orthodox *tariqah*-based scholasticism, of which the Malay translation of the Burda and the Rifāʿiyya-based polemics of Nūruddīn ar-Rānīrī are among the principal surviving documents.
