**Administrative Decentralization** (*bestuursdecentralisatie*) in the Dutch East Indies referred to the process of transferring administrative authority and responsibility from the central colonial government to lower-level regional and local governmental organs. The concept gained particular prominence during the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] held across Sumatra, where it became a central principle in discussions about restructuring the colonial governance system.

## Historical Context

The concept of administrative decentralization emerged gradually during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in response to several converging pressures:

- The increasing complexity of administering vast and diverse territories
- The rising costs of maintaining a large centralized bureaucratic apparatus
- Growing demands for greater local participation in governance
- Lessons learned from costly military campaigns, particularly the extended [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1903]]), which had strained colonial resources

The [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] represented the first systematic attempt to conceptualize and implement administrative decentralization across the Dutch East Indies on a comprehensive basis.

## Two Forms of Decentralization

As articulated by **S. de Graaf**, the Government Commissioner for Reorganization, administrative decentralization could take two distinct forms:

### 1. **Autonomy** (*autonomie*)

Autonomy referred to the delegation of management of local household or domestic affairs (*huishoudelijke belangen*) to autonomous local bodies established at the municipal, village, or regency level. The prototypes were:

- **Municipal councils** (*gemeenteraden*) on Java and Madura, established to manage local public works, sanitation, education, and similar matters affecting daily community life
- **Regency councils** (*regentschapsraden*) on Java and Madura, composed of the indigenous regent (*regent*) as presiding officer, European and indigenous officials, and a substantial proportion of non-official indigenous representatives, with responsibility for supervising the use of regency funds
- Proposed autonomous bodies for the [[Outer Possessions]] (*Buitenbezittingen*), including [[Aceh]], though these proved more difficult to implement due to questions about the historical roots and institutional capacity of potential local units

The ultimate purpose of autonomy was to stimulate local popular interest and participation in governance by creating venues where communities could collectively manage affairs directly affecting their own localities.

### 2. **Self-Governance** (*zelfbestuur*)

Self-governance referred to the transfer to lower-level governmental organs of responsibility for executing specific state functions and services. Unlike autonomy, which was conceived as a local self-management of household affairs, self-governance involved the delegation of substantial state authority. Proposed functions to be transferred to provincial self-governing organs included:

- Indigenous education (*Inlandsch onderwijs*)
- Police administration (*politiewezen*)
- Public works (*waterstaat*)
- Public credit systems (*volkscredietwezen*)
- Other technical and administrative services

The distinction between autonomy and self-governance was critical: autonomy was essentially participatory and educative in purpose, while self-governance was functional and responsible—it involved the exercise of delegated state power, not merely community self-management.

## Provincial Reorganization

A central element of administrative decentralization in the reform proposals was the consolidation of the existing multitude of small provincial units (*gewesten*) into a smaller number of substantially larger provinces. The rationale for this consolidation included:

1. **Technical capacity**: Larger provinces could support specialized provincial bureaus and advisory staffs capable of drafting regulations and providing technical guidance in such domains as engineering, finance, and education
2. **Personnel efficiency**: Qualified European administrative personnel were scarce; concentrating them in fewer, larger provincial centers would enable more intensive oversight and higher quality administration
3. **Financial sustainability**: Larger provinces with greater territorial and population bases could sustain more complex administrative structures
4. **Continuity and institutional development**: Larger, more stable provincial units would provide better basis for long-term administrative development

On Java and Madura, the reform envisioned reducing the number of provinces to approximately three large units. In the [[Outer Possessions]], the proposals included the consolidation of multiple smaller residencies into larger provincial units—a change that would, for instance, involve merging [[Aceh]] with the [[Sumatra's Oostkust|East Coast of Sumatra]].

## Aceh and the East Coast Merger Proposal

The application of administrative decentralization principles to [[Aceh]] figured prominently in the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]], particularly during the sessions at [[Medan]] and [[Koeota-Radja]]. The proposal to merge [[Aceh]] with the [[Sumatra's Oostkust|East Coast]] (*Sumatra's Oostkust*) into a single enlarged provincial unit generated substantial discussion, with Controller [[J. Tideman]] voicing particular concerns about combining two regions of markedly different character:

- **Aceh**: A territory with deep Islamic administrative traditions, indigenous sultanate heritage, a population with strong historical consciousness, and an agrarian economic base centered on pepper cultivation
- **East Coast**: A commercially oriented region dominated by plantation agriculture, with significant Chinese merchant involvement, and a more ethnically mixed population

Tideman argued that merging these disparate regions would create administrative complications and could provoke resistance from populations with different orientations and interests. However, **S. de Graaf** and **J. de Groot** countered that mergers of this type need not create difficulties if provincial regulations were applied with flexibility to account for regional variations. Not every regulation formulated at the provincial level, De Graaf argued, needed to apply uniformly across all districts and subdivisions within a province.

## Implementation Challenges

The [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] revealed significant challenges in implementing comprehensive administrative decentralization:

### Personnel Supply
A consistent concern was whether sufficient qualified European personnel could be recruited and deployed to staff the expanded provincial centers, advisory bureaus, and oversight bodies. The colonial service faced competition for educated personnel, and many Dutch young people were choosing employment in Europe rather than in the tropics.

### Indigenous Participation
While the reform proposals included provisions for indigenous representation in autonomous and self-governing bodies, questions persisted about whether such representation would prove genuine or merely ceremonial, and whether indigenous populations would develop authentic interest in participating in governance structures that might still remain substantially under European control.

### Institutional Capacity
A particular challenge arose in the [[Outer Possessions]], where colonial administration had to determine whether existing indigenous administrative units (*marga* communities in Palembang, districts in Korintji, regencies on South Celebes) possessed sufficient historical cohesion and institutional development to serve as vehicles for autonomous or self-governing status.

### Financial Sustainability
The reform presumed that provincial self-governing bodies would have access to adequate financial resources. Questions arose about the appropriate mix of fixed provincial revenue assignments versus participation in shared colonial revenues, and about the extent to which the central government would retain control over provincial budgetary decisions.

## Significance

Administrative decentralization as formulated during the [[1913–1914 Administrative Reform Meetings]] represented a significant departure from the highly centralized administrative model that had characterized Dutch colonial governance through the nineteenth century. The principles articulated during these meetings—particularly the distinction between autonomy (local household management) and self-governance (delegated state function), the logic of provincial consolidation, and the framework for mixed European-indigenous governance bodies—would shape colonial administration in the Dutch East Indies through the remainder of the colonial period.

The specific application of these principles to [[Aceh]]—through the proposed merger with the [[Sumatra's Oostkust|East Coast]], the creation of provincial councils with indigenous representation, and the delegation of certain state functions to the provincial level—marked a transition from the military governance that had dominated Aceh during and immediately after the [[Aceh War]] toward incorporation into a rationalized, bureaucratic colonial administrative system.
