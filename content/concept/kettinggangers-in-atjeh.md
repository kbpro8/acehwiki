The **[[Kettinggangers in Atjeh|kettinggangers in Atjeh]]** (chain-gang convict labourers) were the *dwangarbeiders* (forced labourers) — convicts from the Dutch colonial prison system — who performed the heaviest work of the [[Bezettingskorps in Atjeh|bezettingskorps]]: the construction of roads, redoutes, magazijnen, kazernen and the field railway between [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] and [[Oeleh Leh]]. Borel mentions the kettinggangers repeatedly as the labour force on which the Dutch vestiging depended, and as the victims of the worst mortality of the occupation: "onder de kettinggangers catastrofaal" — among the kettinggangers catastrophic — was his description of the cholera mortality of [[1874]] and [[1875]].

## The Convict Labour System

The kettinggangers were, in Borel's account, the convict labourers of the Dutch colonial prison system — *dwangarbeiders* (forced labourers) sent from the prisons of Java and Sumatra to Atjeh to perform the heaviest work of the occupation. The kettinggangers constructed the roads, redoutes, magazijnen, kazernen and the field railway between K. Radja and Oeleh Leh; they handled the debarkation of stores at the [[Zeehoofd|zeehoofd]] at Oeleh Leh; they drew the wheeled stretchers that carried the dead through the interior of the Kraton to the cemetery at [[Kota Podjoet|K. Podjoet]]. The kettinggangers were typically chained — hence the name — and were under the guard of armed schildwachten. They were, in effect, the slave labour force of the Dutch vestiging: the men on whose backs the physical infrastructure of the occupation was built. The use of convict labour was, in the Dutch colonial system, a standard expedient: the prisons of Java and Sumatra supplied a steady stream of convicts for public works in the colonies, and the Atjeh-oorlog was no exception.

## The Mortality

The mortality of the kettinggangers was, in Borel's account, the worst of the occupation. The kettinggangers performed the heaviest work in the most exposed conditions — handling the debarkation at Oeleh Leh under enemy fire, constructing the redoutes and the field railway under the kwade moesson, drawing the wheeled stretchers through the interior of the Kraton. They were housed in the same wretched kazerneloodsen as the troops — barracks with no zijwanden, through which the rain poured — and they were fed the same reduced rations of rijst (boeboer). The cholera that broke out among the troops and koelies in [[1874]] and [[1875]] was, in Borel's account, "onder de kettinggangers catastrofaal" — catastrophic among the kettinggangers. The mortality of the kettinggangers was, in Borel's implicit judgement, the hidden cost of the Dutch vestiging: the cost paid by the convict labourers whose deaths did not appear in the official casualty lists of the war but whose bodies filled the graves at K. Podjoet alongside those of the soldiers.

## The Cattle Theft and the Watch

The kettinggangers also feature in Borel's account of the *cattle theft from the veekraal* in May [[1874]]. At night the Atjehneezen would "bekruipen" the veekraal, chase off the wachthebbende kettinggangers, and with the blanke [[Klewang|klewang]] attack, driving off some of the "verschrikte dieren." After losing "een honderd stuks vee," the Dutch were forced to bring the kraal inside the Kraton. The episode illustrates both the vulnerability of the kettinggangers — who could be chased off by the Atjehneezen — and the *verregaande brutaliteit* of the enemy, who could penetrate the Dutch lines to steal cattle from under the noses of the convict guards. The kettinggangers also feature in Borel's account of the movement of the dead: the wheeled stretchers that carried the dead through the interior of the Kraton to the cemetery at K. Podjoet were "drawn by two chain-gang convicts" — a practice Borel strongly criticises as injurious to morale, since the "ominous little wagons" had to go inside the parapet, past the barracks, in full view of the troops. The kettinggangers were thus, in Borel's account, both the labour force of the vestiging and the witnesses of its mortality — the men whose work built the Dutch posts and whose hands drew the dead to the cemetery.
