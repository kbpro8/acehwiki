A **missigit** (modern spelling *mesigit*, from Arabic *masjid*) is a mosque or prayer-house of the [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] — a building or enclosure used for the daily prayers and the Friday service. The missigit recurs in the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] as a strongpoint of particular symbolic as well as strategic importance: the conversion of a captured missigit into a fortified Dutch redoubt — enclosing the building within a sandbag parapet and using it for officers' quarters — was a characteristic operation of the middle phase of the war, and the Atjehese counter-attacks on such works were correspondingly fierce. The [[Missigit Longbatta|Missigit Longbatta]] taken on [[31 December]] [[31 December]] [[1874]] and fortified by [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Tweede-Luitenant C. J. Snijders]] in [[January 1875|January]] [[1875]] is the best-documented instance; the [[Mandarsah Poetih|Mandarsah Poetih]] (the "white bedehuis") of [[28 January]] [[28 January]] [[1875]] is another.

## The Missigit as Strongpoint

The [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] used their missigits as natural strongpoints: the solid construction of the larger mosques (stone or brick, in a kampong of timber and bamboo) made them resistant to artillery fire, and the surrounding low earthwork — the typical Atjehese mosque enclosure — provided a ready-made rampart. The [[Missigit Longbatta|Missigit Longbatta]] at the time of its capture had "een lage, aarden omwalling, die nagenoeg de gedaante had van een regthoek, waarvan de langste zijde ongeveer 30, de kortste 25 strekkende meter vuurlijn telde"; the [[Mandarsah Poetih|Mandarsah Poetih]] was "een door artillerievuur reeds ten deele vernield steenen gebouwtje." Both were thus suitable for incorporation into a Dutch redoubt with relatively little additional earthwork.

## The Dutch Conversion Practice

The Dutch practice, as recorded in the [[1944]] biography of [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Snijders]], was to trace a langwerpige redoute around the captured missigit, enclosing the building within the parapet. The missigit itself was then fitted up as officers' quarters — "de Missigit zelve voor officierslogies ingericht" — while the rest of the garrison and the coolies bivouacked in the open until the bamboo barracks could be built. The arrangement gave the officers the most solid building on the site for their quarters, and gave the garrison a built-in last-ditch defence in the form of the mosque walls. The parapet was typically of sandbags, with a wet ditch where water was available (the [[Missigit Longbatta|Missigit Longbatta]] had a wet ditch on the east front fed by an existing water conduit), and the auxiliary obstacles of [[bamboe-doerie|bamboe-doerie]] and [[ranjoe|ranjoe]] planting.

## The Religious-Cum-Military Character of the Resistance

The Atjehese resistance to the loss of a missigit was particularly fierce. The biographer [[D. van den Berg|Van den Berg]] records of [[Missigit Longbatta|Missigit Longbatta]] that "de Atjehers wilden niet lijdelijk in het verlies van hun heiligdom berusten en deden herhaaldelijk pogingen om dit te heroveren" — the Atjehers refused to accept the loss of their sanctuary passively and made repeated attempts to retake it. The defence of a missigit thus had a religious as well as a military character, and the conversion of a missigit into a Dutch redoubt was a religious as well as a military provocation.

## The village *missigit* and its architecture

Away from the theatre of war, the village *missigit* of the [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] was a modest prayer-house, characterised by a broken (pitched) roof, an absence of permanent walls, and elaborate wood-carving (Dutch: *snijwerk*). These simple structures — open to the air on three sides and roofed in thatch or shingle — represented the indigenous religious architecture of the Acehnese countryside before the more substantial colonial-built mosques of the early twentieth century. They are to be distinguished from the larger, government-funded mosques such as the [[Meusigit Raja]] at [[Koeta-Radja]], which was constructed by the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Dutch East Indies colonial government]] in a more robust architectural style and on a far grander scale than the village *missigit*.
