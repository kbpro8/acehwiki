**Lam**, **Tjot**, **Glé**, **Boekit**, **Kroeng**, **Aloer**, **Pagar**, *market*, *mosque*, **Balei**, **Binasa** and *ravine* were the principal [[Achehnese]], Malay and Dutch topographic prefixes and elements used in the place-names of the [[Groot-Atjeh]] theatre of operations described in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*. The prefix *Lam-* denoted a village near water (e.g., [[Lamsoet|Lam Soet]], [[Lambarih|Lam Barih]], [[Lamkoenjit|Lam Koenjit]]); *Tjot-* / Acehnese denoted a hill (e.g., [[Tjot Goeë]], [[Tjot Rang]], [[Tjot Tjako]]); *Glé-* / Acehnese denoted a hill or mountain (e.g., [[Glé Taroempas]], [[Glé Broeë]]); *Bt.* / Malay/Acehnese denoted a hill; *Kroeng-* / *Kroečng* / Acehnese denoted a river (e.g., [[Kroeng Raba]], [[Kroeng Gloempang]], [[Kroeng-Kali]]); *Aloer-* denoted a stream or creek; *Pagar* denoted a fence, often a living fence of thorny plants (*levende pagars*); *Pasar* / *Passer* denoted a market; *Masdjid* / *Missigit* denoted a mosque (and a *versterkte masdjid* a fortified mosque); *Balei* denoted a hall or building; literally "destruction", "ruined" was used as a prefix for fortifications; Dutch denoted a valley or gorge.

## Etymology and Meaning

The Achehnese/Malay/Dutch topographic vocabulary is largely descriptive. *Lam-* is Acehnese for "village near water" or "place at the mouth of a river" — the prefix is attached to a water-feature name (as in [[Lamsoet|Lam Soet]], where *Soet* denotes a stream). *Tjot-* / *Cot-* is Acehnese for "hill" — cognate with Malay *choot* / *chot*. *Glé-* / *Geulé-* is Acehnese for "hill" or "mountain", often applied to larger eminences than *tjot*. *Bt.* is the Dutch abbreviation for Malay/Acehnese for "hill", from Sanskrit *bhūmi* "earth" or *parvata* "mountain", via phonological adaptation; the full form is *Boekit*. *Kroeng-* / *Kroečng* / *Krueng* is Acehnese for "river" — the OCR variants reflect the difficulty of rendering the Acehnese velar nasal in Dutch orthography. *Aloer-* is Malay for "stream", "creek" (from *alir*, "to flow"). *Pagar* is Malay for "fence" — often a living fence of thorny plants (*levende pagars*). *Pasar* / *Passer* is Malay/Indonesian for "market" (from Persian *bāzār*). *Masdjid* / *Missigit* are Acehnese/Dutch renderings of Arabic mosque; *Missigit* is the older Dutch-Indies spelling. *Balei* is Malay for "hall", "building" (from Portuguese *baleare*? or Sanskrit). *Binasa* is Malay for "destruction", "ruined" — used as a prefix for e.g., *Koeta Binasa Baroe* = "New Ruined Fort". *Kloof* is Dutch for "cleft", "gorge", "valley" — a non-Acehnese term used by the Dutch to describe the deep ravines that characterised the inland topography.

## Historical Context

In the first semester of [[1896]] the topographic prefixes were the indispensable key to the map of [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The *Lam-* villages — [[Lamsoet|Lam Soet]], [[Lambarih|Lam Barih]], [[Lamkoenjit|Lam Koenjit]], [[Lam-Goet|Lam Goet]], [[Lam-Pisang]] — were the principal sites of the buitenlinie and the principal targets of Achehnese attack. The *Tjot-* hills — [[Tjot Goeë]], [[Tjot Rang]], [[Tjot Tjigari]], [[Tjot Tjako]], [[Tjot Pliet]] — were the high-ground features that dominated the *sawah* and the raised paths. The *Glé-* mountains — [[Glé Taroempas]], [[Glé Broeë]] — were the larger eminences of the inland ranges. The *Kroeng-* rivers — [[Kroeng Raba]], [[Kroeng Gloempang]], [[Kroeng-Kali]], [[Kroeng Daroe]] — were the water features that defined the line of march and the position of defensive works. The *Aloer-* streams — *Aloer Oe* — were the smaller watercourses. The *levende pagars* — living fences of thorny plants — were Achehnese defensive features around kampongs. The *pasar* of [[Lamkoenjit]] was the great market of the [[IX Moekims]]. The *masdjid* — including the fortified mosque — was the religious and often defensive centre of each moekim.

## Significance

The Achehnese topographic prefixes are the indispensable key to the map of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and to the tactical problem of the [[Aceh War]]. A Dutch column marching on a *Lam-* village knew it would encounter water — a stream, a river, a flooded *sawah* — and that the village would be defined by that water feature. A column approaching a *Tjot-* or *Glé-* knew it would encounter high ground held by Achehnese defenders. A column crossing a *Kroeng-* knew it would encounter a river crossing, often a defended bridge or ford. The *levende pagars* — living fences of thorny plants — were Achehnese defensive features that impeded Dutch infantry and channelled them into killing grounds. The *masdjid* — particularly the *versterkte masdjid* — was the religious and defensive centre of each moekim, often the last strongpoint of Achehnese resistance. The survival of these prefixes in modern Acehnese and Indonesian place-names testifies to their enduring descriptive power.

## Account from *Het recht op den grond in Atjeh* ([[D. W. N. de Boer]], [[1931]]) — glé and pageue in the law of land

Two of these topographic terms carried a technical legal sense in the customary law of the soil described by [[D. W. N. de Boer]].

### Glé and toenong — the uplands

Besides its topographic meaning of hill or mountain, *glé* denoted the upland districts of a *[[Landschap (Atjehnese)|landschap]]*, synonymously with *toenong*: "de *toenong* of *glé*, d.z. de bovenstreken (het hoogland) van zijn gebied" — the upper districts, the highland, of his territory. Economically calculating as he was, an *[[Oeleebalang|oeléëbalang]]* would often exempt colonists in particular districts from the *[[Thiplah tano|haq thiplah-tano]]* obligation for their first three years, precisely in order to attract immigration into his *glé*; the *[[Haq radja taléë|radja taléë]]*, however, was still collected at the first harvest.

The same word named one of the sovereign levies of the *oeléëbalangs*: the *[[Adat glé|adat glé]]*, the composite tax on cultivation carried on outside the [[Gampong|gampong]] boundaries — that is, in the forest and in the mountains. It comprised a levy on wet rice fields identical with the *[[Boeët oemòng|boeët oemòng]]* charged within the village; the *[[Padi lintjah|padi lintjah]]* (also *padi tadjoë*, "dibbled padi") on dry fields, at two *naleh* of *padi* per *hippaang padi*; and the *[[Thiploeëh-tha|thiploeëh-tha]]* — literally "one of the ten" — the tithe of the crop on gardens, a levy found elsewhere in the Archipelago as well, for instance in [[Bali]]. All were abolished by article 15 sub I of [[Staatsblad 1919 No. 822]], the chiefs receiving a fixed *[[Civiele lijst|civiele lijst]]* in compensation.

### Pageue — the fence as act of appropriation and as evidence

The *pagar* of the general vocabulary is the Atjehnese *[[Pageue|pageue]]*, which in the law of land marked the completion of an occupation. Occupation of waste ground was announced first by planting ordinary stakes at the corners of the terrain and joining them by a *[[Rintisan|rintisan]]* (boundary path), and in a further stage by complete enclosure, the *pageue*. Its remains afterwards served as proof: a claimant asserting earlier use of ground fit only for a garden or *[[Ladang|ladang]]* had to point, with his witnesses, either to trees or shrubs surviving from an earlier crop or to the remnants of a former *pageue*. Where no such remains could be discerned and only a single isolated tree stood — by preference a fruit tree such as a *doerian* or *manggistan* — the claimant could formerly invoke the *[[Haq thilangkob|haq thilangkob]]*, the right of the felling-length, extending his claim to a circle about the tree with the tree's own height as radius. That right, De Boer recorded, no longer occurred in his own day; he noted it only because the recording of "antiquities of customary law" could serve the comparative study of the *adat* law of other regions. The *pageue* was also a cost item in garden *[[Mawaih|mawaih]]* contracts, the party supplying the land furnishing the material and labour for its erection.
