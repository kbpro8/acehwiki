**Nutmeg (Aceh)** (Dutch: *nootmuskaat*, Latin: *Myristica fragrans*) was a high-value spice exported through the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]], consisting of the dried seed kernel of the nutmeg tree either with the surrounding aril (Dutch: *dop*) intact or with the aril removed, and sometimes "chalked" (Dutch: *geklakt*) — coated with a thin layer of slaked lime — to prevent spoilage and insect attack during the long sea voyage to Europe. The mace (Dutch: *foelie*) attached to the outer shell was itself a significant export, and both products were sourced primarily from the [[Molukken|Moluccas]] and, after the late nineteenth century, from Grenada in the [[Kleine Antillen|Lesser Antilles]] (Dutch: *Kleine Antillen*). Nutmeg and mace together reinforced the role of [[Aceh]] as a redistribution hub in the broader spice network of the Indonesian archipelago.

## Export forms

The nutmeg trade of Atjeh distinguished several export forms, each commanding a different market price:

- **With aril** (Dutch: *met dop*): Fresh whole nuts with the aril intact, principally used for culinary purposes and the highest-priced grade on the European market. The aril was removed by the buyer after purchase, dried, and sold separately as [[Mace (Foelie)|foelie]].
- **Without aril** (Dutch: *zonder dop*): The seed kernel alone, with the aril already removed and processed for the mace trade. This was the standard bulk export form.
- **Chalked** (Dutch: *geklakt*): The kernel coated with a thin layer of slaked lime (Dutch: *gebluste kalk*), which sealed the surface against moisture, mould and the larvae of the nutmeg weevil (*Araecerus fasciculatus*). Chalked nuts were principally exported to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and to the German sausage and bakery trades.

The chalked variety was easily recognised by its dull white coating and slightly dusty feel; buyers tested for genuine chalk by rubbing the surface and tasting for the characteristic alkaline tang of lime.

## Trade dynamics

Nutmeg's high value made it a focal point of colonial competition in the early modern period. The [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] had enforced a strict monopoly over Moluccan nutmeg from the [[17th century|seventeenth century]], including the celebrated destruction of nutmeg trees outside the Banda Islands (Dutch: *Banda-eilanden*) to suppress contraband. That monopoly broke down in the late [[18th century|eighteenth]] and early [[19th century|nineteenth centuries]] when the [[British Empire|British]] transplanted nutmeg trees to Penang, [[Malacca|Malakka]], Ceylon and — most successfully — to Grenada in the [[Kleine Antillen|Lesser Antilles]].

By the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]] the [[Molukken|Moluccas]] remained the principal supplier of nutmeg to the Atjeh transit trade, but Grenada had become a serious competitor on the European market, where its uniformly large, pale-kernelled nuts were preferred by grinders and bakers. The Dutch colonial government responded with quality-control regulations and inspection marks (Dutch: *keurmerken*) on Atjeh-re-exported nutmeg to defend the reputation of Moluccan produce.

## Atjeh as redistribution hub

Although the [[Aceh|Atjehnese]] interior did not itself produce nutmeg in commercial quantities, the residency served as a redistribution hub for the spice trade between the Moluccas, Zanzibar, Penang and Europe. The spice was landed at [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]] and the [[Sabang|free port of Sabang]], sorted by Chinese and Acehnese middlemen, repacked in mats or tins, and re-exported to [[Java|Batavia]], Penang, and the European markets. The colonial customs regime treated nutmeg as a transit commodity (Dutch: *doorvoer-artikel*), and the residency's import and export statistics therefore recorded roughly equal quantities entering and leaving Atjeh.

This redistributive role had medieval roots: the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] in the [[16th century|sixteenth]] and [[17th century|seventeenth centuries]] had served as the great entrepôt for the spice trade between the Maluku archipelago and the Ottoman, Mughal and European worlds, and the colonial transit trade of the early twentieth century was a direct descendant of that role. By the [[1930s]] the consolidation of the spice trade through direct steamship lines between the Moluccas and Europe had reduced Atjeh's redistributive function, though the [[Sabang|free port of Sabang]] continued to channel nutmeg through to Penang and Singapore until the Japanese occupation of [[1942]].
