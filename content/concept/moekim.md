**Moekim** (plural: [[moekims]]) refers to the district-level territorial unit in [[Atjeh]] (Aceh), typically centred around a mosque (*meuseugit*) and forming the basic building block of the [[sagi]] (alliance) system. The three [[sagi]] of [[Groot-Atjeh]]—the [[XXV Moekims]], [[XXII Moekims]], and [[XXVI Moekims]]—derived their names from the number of [[moekims]] they originally contained.

## Structure and significance

The [[moekim]] was the smallest territorial unit that formed part of the larger administrative structure of [[Atjeh]]. It developed around the mosque (*meuseugit*) and was associated with the [[Vrijdagskring]] (Friday circle)—the community of worshippers who gathered for Friday prayers. The rise of the [[moekim]] as a territorial and religious unit was one of the fundamental institutional features of [[Atjeh]] society.

The territorial authority over the [[moekims]] was exercised by the [[oelèëbalangs]]—hereditary chiefs who governed as local rulers (*radja's*) of their districts. These chiefs formed alliances ([[sagi]]) that encompassed the territory where the sultan exercised direct authority. The number of [[moekims]] in each [[sagi]]—[[XXV]], [[XXII]], and [[XXVI]]—indicated the original size of the alliance.

## Role in the Aceh War

The [[moekims]] served as the basic units of both resistance and administration during the [[Aceh War]]. Dutch military strategy focused on controlling or subduing specific groups of [[moekims]]—particularly those in the [[XXII Moekims]] (the core of resistance under [[Panglima Polim]]) and the [[VII Moekims]] (the mountain district connecting [[Pedir]] to the [[XXII Moekims]]). The Dutch established forts, stage lines, and administrative posts at the level of individual [[moekims]] or groups of [[moekims]], attempting to extend direct authority throughout [[Groot-Atjeh]].

The persistence of resistance at the [[moekim]] level—particularly in the [[XXII Moekims]]—demonstrated that the conflict was not merely a struggle between the Dutch and a central sultanate, but a distributed resistance involving independent territorial communities coordinated through religious and family networks.
