**Moekim** (from Arabic *moeqim* "inhabitant") was the Atjèhnese ecclesiastical-administrative unit, originally a Friday-prayer circle of (typically four) villages with a mosque. Each moekim had an *imeum* as head. By Kreemer's time, moekim's had become administrative *bestuursressorten* (administrative districts). In Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*, often mistranscribed in the OCR as *Mockim*, the moekim was the smallest [[Achehnese]] administrative unit, a parish-like cluster of several [[kampong]]s organized around a single [[masdjid]] (mosque) — the basic religious-administrative building block of the Sultanate, the unit of which a single [[imam]] or [[oelama]] had charge and the unit to which an [[hoeloebalang]] was attached.

## Origin

The *moekim* originated as an ecclesiastical unit — a Friday-prayer circle of typically four villages (*gampöng*) with a mosque (*meunasah* or *meuseugit*). The head of each moekim was the *imeum* (from Arabic *imam*), originally the leader of the Friday service.

## The three sagi's of Groot Atjèh

The three *sagi*'s of [[Groot Atjèh|Groot Atjèh]] were named for their number of moekim's:
1. **[[XXII Moekim's|XXII Moekim's]]** ("Twenty-two Moekim's")
2. **[[XXV Moekim's|XXV Moekim's]]** ("Twenty-five Moekim's")
3. **[[XXVI Moekim's|XXVI Moekim's]]** ("Twenty-six Moekim's")

## Evolution into administrative units

Originally religious-ecclesiastical units, the *moekim*'s evolved into administrative *bestuursressorten* (administrative districts). The *imeum*'s also evolved: originally the religious leader (Arab. *imam*) of the Friday service, the *imeum* later became a hereditary secular head ("Oelèëbalang tjout") without religious character. By Kreemer's time, *imeum*'s were *adathoofden* (customary heads) subordinate to Oelèëbalang's or independent; there were approximately 58 imeum's in Groot Atjèh plus 22 independent.

## The VII Moekim's Pidië

A notable moekim-federation was the **VII Moekim's Pidië** (comprising Nitjah, Tandjong, Beurabö, Gögö, Peudaja, Koenjèt, Palöh, and Geuleudiëng), which was transferred from the Gouvernementsgebied of Groot Atjèh to the new afdeeling Pidië as part of the new onderafdeeling Padang Tidji under the [[bestuursindeeling van 1922|bestuursindeeling van 1922]].

## Wakeuëh-districts

Some moekim's were *wakeuëh*-districts (free-lordly domains) whose chiefs were withdrawn from the authority of the sagi-heads. Examples: in the XXV Moekim's — Loeëng Bata, Pagar Ajé, Lam Sajoen; in the XXII Moekim's — the III Moekim's Keureukön; in the XXVI Moekim's — Lho' Goelöng, Tjadé' Mön Batèë, Koeala Gigiëng.

## Account from Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* — Moekims of 1896

The [[Groot-Atjeh]] theatre of operations was historically divided into numbered groups of moekims — the [[VI Moekims]], [[IX Moekims]], [[XXII Moekims]], [[XXV Moekims]], [[XXVI Moekims]], the [[V Moekims Montassik]], the [[III Moekims Kerkoen]], the [[III Moekims Daroe]], the [[VII Moekims Baid]], the [[XIII Moekims Toengkoeb]] and the recalcitrant [[Lamkrak Moekims|four Lamkraksche Moekims]] — each cluster of moekims being itself part of a larger [[sagi]] federation.

### Etymology and Meaning

The word *moekim* is the Acehnese spelling of Arabic مقيم, literally "resident" or "one who stays", and in the Acehnese institutional sense came to denote the body of resident worshippers attached to a particular mosque. In Malay the term is *mukim*; in Kruisheer's Dutch orthography it is consistently plural *moekims*, while the OCR occasionally produces *Mockim* or *Mockims*. The moekim should not be confused with the village (*kampong*): a moekim was an ecclesiastical-administrative unit comprising several kampongs, all of whose inhabitants were notionally parishioners of the same mosque. Multiple moekims in turn aggregated into a [[sagi]].

### Historical Context of 1896

In [[1896]] the moekim was the operative unit at which the [[Dutch]] colonial government and the Achehnese resistance alike measured allegiance. The first semester of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] described in *ATJEH '96* is in effect a rolling catalogue of which moekims had "defected" to [[Teuku Umar]] and which had been "pacified" or "tuchtiged" (punished) by Dutch columns. Thus [[Toekoe Hoesin Lhongbatta]]'s defection carried with him the "3 Moekims Lamrebo, 4 Moekims Ateuh of the sagi of [[XXVI Moekims]], and the Moekims Hoho and Lam Djampoe of the [[V Moekims Montassik]]". The III Moekims Kerkoen were distinguished as having "remained free of moslemin" — i.e., had not (yet) been infiltrated by hostile resistance fighters.

### Examples in the Source

Kruisheer refers to the moekim as the unit of settlement geography throughout the work. He notes the [[IV Moekims|IV en VI Moekims]] of [[Lepong]] as Umar's heartland, the [[IX Moekims]] as containing the strategically placed [[Lamkoenjit]] pasar (market), the [[VII Moekims Baid]] as the source of incursions against [[Biloel]], and the [[XIII Moekims Toengkoeb]] as the territory of [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]]. The [[Lamkrak Moekims|four Lamkraksche Moekims]] — Lamkrak, Kroeng Mas, Lamlheue, and Ateuh — were the planned target of the cancelled operation of [[30 March]] [[1896]] which Umar pre-empted by his defection. The Moekim Lam Tengah, led by [[T. Tjoet Lamtengah]], defected to Umar in the night of [[30 March]]–[[31 March]] [[1896]]. The Moekim Lam Djampoe of the V Moekims Montassik "lay entirely within the Dutch post line" and was therefore strategically vulnerable.

### Significance of the Moekims in 1896

The moekim is the indispensable institutional unit for understanding the [[Aceh War]]: it was the level at which loyalty was bargained, the level at which a religious appeal (*sabil Allah*) was effectively preached, and the level at which the Dutch attempted to install friendly [[hoeloebalang]]s. The repeated Dutch catalogue of "dubious" or "doubtful-minded" moekims in the spring of [[1896]] reflects the fact that Umar's defection was contagious precisely at this ecclesiastical-administrative scale: a moekim could change sides as a body when its mosque head, its imam, and its hoeloebalang agreed.
