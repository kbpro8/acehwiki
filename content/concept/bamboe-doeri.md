**Bamboe-doeri** (Dutch for "thorny bamboo"; modern Indonesian *bambu duri*) was the thorny bamboo palisade used both by the Acehnese as the principal outer defence of their fortified kampongs and *bentings*, and by the Dutch as an auxiliary obstacle planted on the outside of sandbag breastworks, throughout the [[Aceh War|Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1914]]). The *bamboe-doeri beplanting* — sometimes of five metres' width — was the most formidable obstacle to Dutch storming parties throughout the war, and its adoption by Dutch engineers around provisional works illustrates the practical accommodation of Dutch fortification practice to local Acehnese materials and conventions.

## As an Acehnese Fortification

The bamboe-doeri was a thick hedge or palisade of thorny bamboo planted around the enemy's fortification, sometimes of considerable width. It is mentioned in the source work in connection with the stormings of the [[Battle of the Missigit (10 April 1873)|missigit]] of [[April 1873|April 1873]], the fight near kampong [[Lemboe|Lemboe]] of [[December 1873|December 1873]], the *sawah* (rice-field) of [[Lamkrak|Lamkrak]] in [[1878|1878]], and especially the [[Batoe Iliq Expedition (July 1880)|Batoe Iliq expedition]] of [[July 1880|July 1880]], where three successive Dutch stormings failed against the impregnable bamboe-doeri palisades of [[Batoe Iliq|Batoe Iliq]].

In the fight near kampong [[Lemboe|Lemboe]] of [[25 December|25 December]] [[1873|1873]], the soldiers of the right half of the third bataljon "had to hew a way through the heavy bamboe-doern verhakking (*verhakking* — chopping/entanglement) of five metres' width" with their *kapmes* (machete). In the [[Batoe Iliq Expedition (July 1880)|Batoe Iliq expedition]] of [[July 1880|July 1880]], the three successive stormings of [[15 July|15 July]], [[22 July|22 July]] and [[1 August|1 August]] all failed against the impregnable bamboe-doeri palisades. "All attempts to remove the bamboe-doern or to break through it failed, by the murderous fire from the benting and by the masses of stones thrown from it upon the troops... Even an attempt was made to sprinkle the bamboe-doern under the enemy's fire with petroleum and to set it alight, but in vain."

## Use in Dutch Fortification Practice

The biographer [[D. van den Berg|Van den Berg]] records that the provisional sandbag breastwork of the [[Benting Kota Alam operation (13 November 1874)|Benting Kota Alam]] constructed by [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Second Lieutenant C. J. Snijders]] on [[13 November|13 November]] [[1874|1874]] was given, on the west, south, and east fronts, "een voorloopige zandzakborstwering [...] welker afwerend vermogen over een gedeelte van de lengte door eene aan de buitenzijde aanwezige bamboedoerie (doornbamboe) haag werd verhoogd" — a provisional sandbag breastwork [...] whose defensive value over part of the length was raised by a bamboe-doerie (*doornbamboe*, thorny bamboo) hedge present on the outside. The landward (north) side was closed with a breastwork of stacked tree-trunks, of which there was a plentiful supply on the site.

The bamboe-doeri was thus an auxiliary obstacle to the sandbag parapet, raising its "afwerend vermogen" (defensive value) over part of the length. The use of an existing Acehnese vegetable obstacle — bamboe-doeri hedges are a common feature of Acehnese kampong defences — illustrates the practical accommodation of Dutch engineer practice to local materials and local fortification conventions, and is an integral part of the Dutch sandbag-*benting* system of the early Aceh War.

## Significance

The bamboe-doeri was the characteristic form of Acehnese fortification in the [[Aceh War|Aceh War]], and its effectiveness against Dutch storming parties is one of the principal reasons for the high cost of Dutch operations in Aceh. The combination of bamboe-doeri palisade, earthen parapet, and the hail of stones hurled from the parapet, made the typical Acehnese *benting* a formidable obstacle to infantry assault, and frequently required the use of artillery to open a breach. The same obstacle was, in turn, adopted by Dutch engineers as an auxiliary to their own provisional works — a small but telling instance of the technological reciprocity between Acehnese and Dutch fortification practice in the early years of the war.
