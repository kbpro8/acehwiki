**"Red or Green Merdeka"** is the comparative historiographical framework developed by the Dutch historian Henri J. H. Alers in his 1956 study *Om een rode of groene Merdeka: Tien jaren binnenlandse politiek Indonesië, 1943–1953*, in which he treats the Indonesian revolution as unfolding along two parallel and structurally analogous tracks — a "red," urban, and ultimately communist-inflected track centred on [[Java|Java]] and [[Soekarno|Sukarno]], and a "green," rural, and Islamic track whose clearest and most fully realised instance, in Alers's account, is [[Atjèh|Atjeh]] under [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]].

## The core comparison

Alers argues that both the "red" nationalist-communist movement on Java and the "green" Islamic movement in Atjeh drew their revolutionary energy from the same underlying social antagonism — the feudal or traditional-authority class against its self-styled representatives of the people — but that Atjeh's Islamic character gave its revolution both a narrower geographical scope and, paradoxically, a sharper, less-diluted expression than its Javanese counterpart. He writes that Sukarno and Daoed Beureuh, "each over the heads of the Japanese administrators," called forth a fanatical psychosis directed against the established feudal order; Sukarno's was necessarily "red" because it embraced the whole of Java and could draw on no other available idiom, while Daoed Beureuh's was necessarily "green," both because it was confined to Atjeh and because the Japanese occupiers openly courted Islam as a political instrument, unlike anything they risked tolerating from Sukarno's broader synthesis.

## Alers's key claim about Atjeh: no "city," no "red"

Central to Alers's argument is his observation that the antagonism between [[Ulama|Ulama]] and *[[Uleebalang|Uleëbalang]]* in Atjeh — unlike the equivalent tension elsewhere in Indonesia — never expresses itself in the vocabulary of "freedom" (*vrijheid*), because Atjeh, in his reading, still barely knows the city as a "liberating instrument." Where the factor of urbanisation enters a society's revolutionary politics, Alers argues, the "red"–"green" distinction as such first becomes meaningful; without it, the contest remains one purely between Petrus and Nero, Marx and Nietzsche — that is, a contest of authority rather than of ideology in the modern sense.

## The comparative chronology

Alers sets out an explicit side-by-side chronology of Java and Atjeh to argue that, phase for phase, Atjeh's political development ran on a faster clock than Java's:

| Java (urban-red) | Atjeh (rural-green) |
|---|---|
| End of the [[Java War|Java War]], [[1830]] | End of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh War]], [[1903]] |
| Political penetration of the moderate [[Moehammadijah|Muhammadiyah]] movement, [[1908]] | Beginning of moderate, scarcely anti-authority political awareness, [[1925]] |
| Beginning of the organised anti-authority "red" political current, marked by the P.K.I.–S.I. schism, [[1921]] | Beginning of the organised anti-authority "green" religious-political current, marked by the founding of the [[Poesa|Poesa]], [[1939]] |
| Beginning of the social revolution under "red" directives, [[17 August]] [[1945]] | Beginning of the social revolution under "green" directives, [[December]] [[1945]] |

## Application to the events of 1953

Alers extends the same framework to the [[Atjèh's accession to the Negara Islam Indonesia (1953)|1953 rebellion]], describing the Republic's simultaneous confrontation with insurgencies in [[West Java|West Java]], South Celebes, and Atjeh as a "threefold Islamic uprising," and noting the irony that Colonel [[Kolonel Simbolon|Simbolon]] — tasked with reconquering "green" Atjeh for the Republic — had himself declared for "green" during the [[17 oktober-affaire|17 October 1952]] controversy on Java. He also frames the political struggle between [[Poesa|Poesa]], "red" central-government unitarism, and the surviving *Uleëbalang* class's [[Badan Keinsjafan Rakjat|Badan Keinsjafan Rakjat]] as replaying, within Atjeh alone, the same three-cornered contest of republican, feudal-federalist, and central-unifying forces that Alers traces across Indonesia as a whole.
