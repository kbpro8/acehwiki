**Opiumregie** was the government opium monopoly introduced in the gewest [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]] on [[1 April]] [[1913]] (Stbl. [[1913]] N°. 219, modified by Stbl. [[1920]] N°. 235), replacing the earlier *pacht* (farm) system. The introduction in [[1913]] was the last such transition in the Dutch East Indies, marking the final consolidation of Dutch administrative control over [[Aceh|Atjèh]] after decades of war.

## Background: The pacht system

For centuries, the Dutch colonial government had farmed out the right to sell opium at retail to private entrepreneurs — predominantly Chinese — in a system known as the *pacht*. This system incentivised the farmers to maximise consumption through aggressive and often corrupt practices, leading to widespread addiction and smuggling.

Before the *Opiumregie*, opium in Atjèh was managed through the *pacht* system:
- **Stbl. [[1876]] N°. 151** — introduced *opiumpacht* and *speelpacht* in Groot-Atjèh, applying the Soematra's Oostkust pachtreglement (Stbl. [[1875]] N°. 236 Bijl. C)
- **Stbl. [[1884]] N°. 168** — reorganised the *opiumpacht* for Groot-Atjèh

In the Onderhoorigheden, opium was managed through agreements with the hoofden. The *verklaringen in achttien artikelen* (after [[1877]]) ceded opium rights to the Government with *schadeloosstelling* (compensation). The [[Hasil-reglement|Hasil-reglement]] of [[1902]] governed the collection for and on behalf of the hoofden.

**The Westkust licenses**: On the Westkust, before the *Opiumregie*, governors issued opium-import licenses to hoofden: Generaal Van Teijn (as Westkust governor, September [[1887]] – March [[1891]]): 619 kists at ƒ300/kist; Kolonel Pompe van Meerdervoort (Van Teijn's successor, April [[1891]] – January [[1892]]): 67 kists.

## Introduction of the regie

In [[1893]], a government-run system, the *regie*, was introduced on [[Madoera|Madoera]] as a trial. The *regie* was then gradually rolled out across the outer territories:

|------|-----------|
| [[1897]] | Lombok |
| [[1902]] | Lampongsche Districten |
| [[1905]] | Sumatra's Westkust, Tapanoeli, Benkoelen, Ternate |
| [[1906]] | Palembang, Djambi, Banka |
| [[1907]] | Celebes en Onderhoorigheden, Menado |
| [[1908]] | Zuider- en Oosterafdeeling van Borneo, Bali |
| [[1909]] | Billiton, Westerafdeeling van Borneo, Timor |
| [[1910]] | Amboina en Onderhoorigheden |
| [[1911]] | Riouw en Onderhoorigheden |
| [[1912]] | Oostkust van Sumatra |
| **[[1913]]** | **Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden** |

## The verboden kringen

The *Opiumregie* had *verboden kringen* (forbidden circles) where opium sale was entirely prohibited:
- [[Sërbödjadi|Sërbödjadi]] — from [[1911]], Stbl. N°. 280
- [[Gajö Loeüs|Gajö Loeüs]] — from [[1913]]
- [[Alaslanden|Alaslanden]] — from [[1913]]
- [[Takéngön|Takéngön]] — added [[1920]]

In the *verboden kringen*, a license system applied, but few opium users were found — the Gayo and Alas populations did not use opium.

## The regie system

The opium *regie* in Atjèh was characterised by a geographically nuanced system of control:
1. **Free Consumption** — in some areas, the use of opium was officially "free" for certain classes of consumers
2. **Licensing System (*Licentie-stelsel*)** — restricted or unrestricted; in some areas use was prohibited for the indigenous population
3. **Decreasing Licenses (*Afloopend licentie-stelsel*)** — in some areas, the number of licenses was deliberately reduced over time; specifically in the temporary sub-department of [[Gajö Loeüs|Gajö Loeüs]]
4. **Prohibited Zones (*Verboden kringen*)** — where opium use was entirely forbidden

**Sales points and price**: By [[1920]] there were 49 sales points (down from 57 in [[1913]]). The price was raised from ƒ12.50/thail to ƒ20 in May [[1921]] (Bt. N°. 71).

## Revenue

For [[1913]], the total revenue from the Opium *regie* across the Indies reached **10.729 million guilders**. A notable economic effect of the switch was an immediate, sharp increase in reported consumption figures — not because of a real increase in use, but because the government was now capturing revenue from opium that had previously been smuggled and sold illegally.
