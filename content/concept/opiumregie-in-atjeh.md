**Opiumregie** was the government opium monopoly introduced in the gewest [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]] on [[1 April]] [[1913]] (Stbl. [[1913]] N°. 219, modified by Stbl. [[1920]] N°. 235), replacing the earlier *pacht* (farm) system. The introduction in [[1913]] was the last such transition in the Dutch East Indies, marking the final consolidation of Dutch administrative control over [[Aceh|Atjèh]] after decades of war.

## Background: The pacht system

For centuries, the Dutch colonial government had farmed out the right to sell opium at retail to private entrepreneurs — predominantly Chinese — in a system known as the *pacht*. This system incentivised the farmers to maximise consumption through aggressive and often corrupt practices, leading to widespread addiction and smuggling.

Before the *Opiumregie*, opium in Atjèh was managed through the *pacht* system:
- **Stbl. [[1876]] N°. 151** — introduced *opiumpacht* and *speelpacht* in Groot-Atjèh, applying the Soematra's Oostkust pachtreglement (Stbl. [[1875]] N°. 236 Bijl. C)
- **Stbl. [[1884]] N°. 168** — reorganised the *opiumpacht* for Groot-Atjèh

In the Onderhoorigheden, opium was managed through agreements with the hoofden. The *verklaringen in achttien artikelen* (after [[1877]]) ceded opium rights to the Government with *schadeloosstelling* (compensation). The [[Hasil-reglement|Hasil-reglement]] of [[1902]] governed the collection for and on behalf of the hoofden.

**The Westkust licenses**: On the Westkust, before the *Opiumregie*, governors issued opium-import licenses to hoofden: Generaal Van Teijn (as Westkust governor, September [[1887]] – March [[1891]]): 619 kists at ƒ300/kist; Kolonel Pompe van Meerdervoort (Van Teijn's successor, April [[1891]] – January [[1892]]): 67 kists.

## Introduction of the regie

In [[1893]], a government-run system, the *regie*, was introduced on [[Madoera|Madoera]] as a trial. The *regie* was then gradually rolled out across the outer territories:

| Year | Territory |
|------|-----------|
| [[1897]] | Lombok |
| [[1902]] | Lampongsche Districten |
| [[1905]] | Sumatra's Westkust, Tapanoeli, Benkoelen, Ternate |
| [[1906]] | Palembang, Djambi, Banka |
| [[1907]] | Celebes en Onderhoorigheden, Menado |
| [[1908]] | Zuider- en Oosterafdeeling van Borneo, Bali |
| [[1909]] | Billiton, Westerafdeeling van Borneo, Timor |
| [[1910]] | Amboina en Onderhoorigheden |
| [[1911]] | Riouw en Onderhoorigheden |
| [[1912]] | Oostkust van Sumatra |
| **[[1913]]** | **Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden** |

## The verboden kringen

The *Opiumregie* had *verboden kringen* (forbidden circles) where opium sale was entirely prohibited:
- [[Sërbödjadi|Sërbödjadi]] — from [[1911]], Stbl. N°. 280
- [[Gajö Loeüs|Gajö Loeüs]] — from [[1913]]
- [[Alaslanden|Alaslanden]] — from [[1913]]
- [[Takéngön|Takéngön]] — added [[1920]]

In the *verboden kringen*, a license system applied, but few opium users were found — the Gayo and Alas populations did not use opium.

## The regie system

The opium *regie* in Atjèh was characterised by a geographically nuanced system of control:
1. **Free Consumption** — in some areas, the use of opium was officially "free" for certain classes of consumers
2. **Licensing System (*Licentie-stelsel*)** — restricted or unrestricted; in some areas use was prohibited for the indigenous population
3. **Decreasing Licenses (*Afloopend licentie-stelsel*)** — in some areas, the number of licenses was deliberately reduced over time; specifically in the temporary sub-department of [[Gajö Loeüs|Gajö Loeüs]]
4. **Prohibited Zones (*Verboden kringen*)** — where opium use was entirely forbidden

**Sales points and price**: By [[1920]] there were 49 sales points (down from 57 in [[1913]]). The price was raised from ƒ12.50/thail to ƒ20 in May [[1921]] (Bt. N°. 71).

## Revenue

For [[1913]], the total revenue from the Opium *regie* across the Indies reached **10.729 million guilders**. A notable economic effect of the switch was an immediate, sharp increase in reported consumption figures — not because of a real increase in use, but because the government was now capturing revenue from opium that had previously been smuggled and sold illegally.

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893) — the 1890 opium-pacht proposal

[[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s [[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|1893 tract]] proposed the *opium pacht* — *opium farm*, also *opiumpacht*, and in Van Heutsz's tract also the *opiumregie* — as the principal "*vaste inkomsten*" of the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]]: the *verkoop van opium in het groot* (wholesale opium sale) that would, together with the assumption of the [[Hatsil|hatsil]] and the *invoerrechten* on luxury goods, place the Dutch "*in den volsten zin des woords op de geldkist van Atjeh*". The empirical anchor of the proposal is the offer a European firm had made in [[1890]] — to farm the opium-pacht, on the introduction of the *scheepvaartregeling*, at ƒ 250,000 per month for the first year, with a projected rise to ƒ 400,000 per month thereafter. Van Heutsz's prescription was to substitute this *pacht* (a farmed-out concession to a private firm) for the existing *licentie*-system under which the chiefs of [[Groot-Atjeh]] sold, for ƒ 200 or ƒ 300 per chest, the right to import opium that then retailed "*in het klein duizenden guldens*" — and to consider, alternatively, an *opiumregie* (a government monopoly) as "*misschien nog beter*".

### Opium as war-necessity for the resistance

The fiscal significance of the opium-pacht rested, in Van Heutsz's diagnosis, on the structural fact that opium was one of the principal commodities the Atjehnese resistance consumed — a war-necessity on a par with arms and ammunition:

> "ook in een Inlandschen staat het oorlogvoeren veel geld kost, zoowel tot onderhoud van hen, die aan den strijd deelnemen en dus niet in eigen levensonderhoud kunnen voorzien, als ter verkrijging van het noodige oorlogsmateriaal en van opium".

Opium, in other words, was among the *noodige* — the necessities — of an inland state at war: it was consumed by the combatants and financed by the export trade the coastal statelets conducted with the Straits Settlements. The corollary, for Van Heutsz, was that the Dutch could not effectively cut the resistance from its supplies without controlling the opium trade — and that the control of the opium trade would, by the same token, supply the Dutch with a major income source. The 1893 tract records, in the chapter on the [[Tamiang]] smuggling-route, that "*in 1890, tijdens de sluiting, het grootste gedeelte van de oorlogscontrabande en opium, die te [[Kemala]] binnenkwamen, ingevoerd waren via Tamiang*" — in 1890, during the closure, the greater part of the contraband of war and the opium that entered Kemala had been imported via Tamiang. The Tamiang route was, in this sense, the smuggling-channel by which the coastal opium trade reached the interior's resistance; and the opium-pacht was the fiscal instrument by which the Dutch would convert that channel from the resistance's supply-line into the colonial state's revenue.

### The 1890 European firm's offer

The empirical anchor of Van Heutsz's prescription is the offer a European firm had made in [[1890]] — to farm the opium-pacht, on the introduction of the *scheepvaartregeling*, at ƒ 250,000 per month for the first year, with a projected rise to ƒ 400,000 per month thereafter:

> "In 1890 bood een Europeesche firma aan, om bij invoering van de scheepvaartregeling, voor het eerste jaar den verkoop van opium te pachten tegen f 250.000 's maands en volgens hare berekening zou zij na dat eerste jaar wel durven gaan tot f 400.000 's maands".

The offer established a concrete fiscal baseline for the *scheepvaartregeling*: ƒ 250,000 per month in the first year, rising to ƒ 400,000 per month thereafter — between ƒ 3,000,000 and ƒ 4,800,000 per year, a sum Van Heutsz considered sufficient to finance the war's continuation from its own proceeds. The offer was conditional on the introduction of the *scheepvaartregeling* — that is, on the closure of the open coast and the routing of all trade through Dutch-administered ports — because the opium-pacht could not be made profitable so long as the chiefs of Groot-Atjeh continued to license opium imports at the nominal rate of ƒ 200 or ƒ 300 per chest.

### The existing *licentie*-system and the retail margin

Van Heutsz's principal fiscal argument against the existing arrangement is that the *licentie*-system — under which the chiefs of Groot-Atjeh sold, for ƒ 200 or ƒ 300 per chest, the right to import opium that then retailed for thousands of guilders — left the bulk of the opium revenue in the hands of the chiefs and the retail traders, not the colonial state:

> "Men moet dus niet, (zooals vóór de sluiting geschiedde …) tegen betaling van f 200 of f 300 eene licentie verstrekken voor den invoer van een kist opium (40 bollen), die dan bij verkoop in het klein duizenden guldens opbrengt".

A *kist opium* — a chest of opium — contained 40 *bollen* (balls); the licence to import one chest was sold by the chiefs for ƒ 200 or ƒ 300; and the same chest, when retailed "*in het klein*" — in small quantities — to the Atjehnese consumers, "*duizenden guldens*" — thousands of guilders — yielded. The retail margin — the difference between the ƒ 200/300 licence fee and the thousands of guilders of retail revenue — was the *winst* the chiefs and retail traders captured, and the *winst* the opium-pacht would transfer to the colonial state. The transfer was, in Van Heutsz's diagnosis, the single most important fiscal operation the *scheepvaartregeling* would perform.

### *Pacht* vs. *regie*

The 1893 tract presents the choice between *pacht* and *regie* as a choice between two instruments of the same fiscal operation. The *pacht* — the farm — would assign the wholesale opium monopoly to a private firm (such as the European firm that had made the [[1890]] offer), which would pay the colonial state a fixed monthly sum (ƒ 250,000 rising to ƒ 400,000) in exchange for the exclusive right to import and sell opium at wholesale. The *regie* — the government monopoly — would have the colonial state itself conduct the wholesale opium trade, retaining the full retail margin. Van Heutsz sets out the alternatives:

> "3e. invoering van de opiumpacht en c. q. die van andere middelen. Opiumregie is misschien nog beter".

The phrase *Opiumregie is misschien nog beter* — the *regie* is perhaps still better — is Van Heutsz's only explicit endorsement of one instrument over the other: the *regie*, in his diagnosis, would yield the colonial state a higher revenue than the *pacht*, because it would retain the full retail margin rather than sharing it with the farming firm. The *pacht*, however, had the advantage of immediate implementation: the European firm's offer of ƒ 250,000 per month was a concrete bid that could be accepted at once, whereas the *regie* required the colonial state to construct its own opium-distribution apparatus from scratch. Van Heutsz's prescription is therefore a practical compromise: introduce the *pacht* immediately, on the European firm's [[1890]] offer, and consider the *regie* as a possible later substitution "*indien de omstandigheden dit toelaten*". This 1893 prescription anticipates the actual [[1913]] transition from *pacht* to *regie* documented above — a transition Van Heutsz had already, in [[1893]], identified as the ultimately preferable form.

### The Oleh-leh exception

The one exception Van Heutsz made to the opium-monopoly was [[Olehleh]] — the Dutch-held outlet on the coast of Groot-Atjeh — which he proposed to declare a *vrijhaven* (free port) for all trade except the opium trade. The proposal is recorded in the 1893 tract as the position [[Van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] had himself adopted:

> "Zooals de stand van zaken nu is, achtte de Generaal van Teijn het noodig dat Oleh-leh, behalve voor den invoer van opium, eene vrijhaven wordt".

Van Heutsz endorses the position: "*Naar mijn oordeel heeft de Generaal gelijk en moet Oleh-leh een vrijhaven blijven*". The Oleh-leh exception is, in Van Heutsz's diagnosis, the operational counterpart of the opium-pacht: the free-port status of Oleh-leh would attract the trade of Groot-Atjeh to the Dutch-held outlet, while the *vrijhaven*-except-opium clause would ensure that the opium trade — the principal *vaste inkomsten* — would be conducted exclusively under the colonial state's fiscal control. The combination of the *vrijhaven* and the *opium-pacht* was, in this sense, the fiscal architecture of the *scheepvaartregeling* at Groot-Atjeh: free trade in everything except opium, and a colonial monopoly on the one commodity whose revenue could finance the war's continuation.

### Significance of the Van Heutsz proposal

The opium-pacht's significance in the [[Aceh Wiki History]] record, in Van Heutsz's 1893 diagnosis, rests on four things: it was the principal "*vaste inkomsten*" of the *scheepvaartregeling* — "*De vaste inkomsten door ons te Groot-Atjeh te trekken, bestaan in de opiumpacht of regie en de pacht op de z.g. kleine middelen, pandhuizen en dergelijke*"; it was the fiscal instrument by which the Dutch would convert the opium trade from a chiefly revenue into a colonial revenue — replacing the ƒ 200/300 *licentie* per chest with a wholesale monopoly that captured the thousands of guilders of retail margin; it was anchored in a concrete empirical bid — the [[1890]] offer of a European firm to farm the opium-pacht at ƒ 250,000 per month for the first year, rising to ƒ 400,000 — that established the order of magnitude of the revenue the *scheepvaartregeling* would yield; and it was the fiscal counterpart to the *vrijhaven*-except-opium clause Van Heutsz prescribed for Olehleh — the architecture by which free trade in everything except opium would coexist with a colonial monopoly on the one commodity whose revenue could finance the war's continuation. The 1893 prescription anticipates the [[1913]] *regie* transition that would, two decades later, implement Van Heutsz's *regie*-preference as the actual colonial policy.
