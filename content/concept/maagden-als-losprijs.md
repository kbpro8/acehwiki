**Maagden als losprijs** — "maidens as ransom" — was the request put to *Commissaris* [[Pieter Soury|Pieter Soury]]'s [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|embassy to Atjeh]] in [[1641]] by the Atjeh court, asking whether the Dutch had brought "Portugeesche jonge dochterkens" — Portuguese maidens — to be delivered as *losprijs* (ransom). The request, made at an audience attended by the *[[Panglima|Panglima's]]* of [[Pedir]] and [[Deli]] and many other Atjeh nobles, is preserved in the chapter-title of chapter XI of [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie*: "Atjehsche ambassade. Inlandsche feesten. Maagden als losprijs."

## The request of 1641

At an audience at the court of the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]], attended by the *[[Panglima|Panglima's]]* of [[Pedir]] and [[Deli]] and many other Atjeh nobles, the chiefs asked whether the Dutch had brought "Portugeesche jonge dochterkens" — Portuguese maidens — to be delivered as *losprijs* (ransom), as they had previously requested.

The context of the request was the Atjeh court's long-running conflict with Portuguese [[Malakka]] — a conflict that had only just been resolved in [[1641]] by the joint VOC–Atjeh–[[Johor|Djohor]] capture of the city. The Atjeh court had Portuguese prisoners of rank in its custody, most notably the nobleman [[Francisco de Souza de Castro|Francisco de Souza de Castro]]. The request for Portuguese maidens — to be exchanged, presumably, for Atjeh prisoners or for some other ransom arrangement — must be understood in that context.

## Soury's response

[[Pieter Soury|Soury]] answered gravely that there were no Portuguese maidens to be had at [[Batavia]], and that the ships had lain seven days at [[Malakka]] but could obtain none there either. His instructions had recommended him to add that the sending of maidens was *tegen onze wet en religie* — against our law and religion — but when the two Atjeh ambassadors who had come back with him from Batavia confirmed what he had said, he omitted that declaration. The omission is itself a small act of diplomatic discretion: Soury chose not to moralise once the practical answer (no maidens available) had been confirmed by the Atjeh ambassadors themselves.

## The connection with the Portuguese prisoner

The "maidens as ransom" request is closely connected with the captivity of the Portuguese nobleman [[Francisco de Souza de Castro|Francisco de Souza de Castro]] at Atjeh — a captivity that intersected directly with the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy]] of [[1641]]. The Atjeh court clearly associated the Dutch visit with the prospect of a Portuguese exchange, and De Castro's person was part of that calculation. The embassy's diplomatic clarification — that Portugal in [[1640]] had been freed from Spain, was no longer the Republic's mortal enemy, and that the Republic had concluded a ten-year truce with Portugal the following year — also bears on the *losprijs* question, in that the Dutch-Portuguese truce complicated the Atjeh court's calculation of what to do with its Portuguese captive.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the "maidens as ransom" request is a striking instance of the sultanate's expectations of its Dutch diplomatic partners — expectations that the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] was neither willing nor able to meet. The request reveals a side of Atjeh court diplomacy that the formal ceremonial of the [[Atjehsche ambassade ceremony (1 July 1641)|1 July 1641 audience]] does not show: the sultanate's interest in human exchange as a means of resolving its long-running conflict with Portuguese Malakka. The Dutch refusal — practical, not moral — is a small but significant marker of the limits of the Dutch-Atjeh alliance.
