***Maloe*** — glossed by [[Maurice Henri du Croo|du Croo]] variously as "embarrassed, at a loss, ashamed" (embarrassed, at a loss, ashamed) — was the Acehnese and Malay concept of shame or loss of face which, in du Croo's analysis, supplied the motive for the great majority of the *[[Atjeh-moord|Atjeh murders]]* and of the local outbreaks of resistance in the pacified years [[1913]]–[[1918]].

## The mechanism

During the years of the general struggle the emotional Acehnese who felt himself disappointed in his domestic or married life, in his family, his life-wishes, his social existence or his relation to his chief or fellow villagers — who had thus become *maloe* — found the solution close at hand: he joined a band. "Through and with that band he could avenge himself; by becoming a band member he made good again what he had done wrong, and he could moreover die as a [[sjahid|martyr]] in the struggle against the [[kaphé|unbeliever]]."

When the *bendewezen* was rooted out, that ready solution disappeared, precisely at a time when the years of struggle in the *rimba* had unsettled countless Acehnese. Those unbalanced men then sought a new solution when they felt themselves *maloe*: "in the killing of an entirely arbitrary unbeliever and in becoming a martyr in that way after all."

## Cases

The concept appears explicitly in several of the cases du Croo records:

- In the [[Murder of Lieutenant-Colonel Scheepens (10 October 1913)|Scheepens case]], the *[[oelèëbalang]]* [[T. Béntara Titeuë]] told the presiding Civil Authority that he understood Acehnese *[[adat]]* could no longer be applied to the man who had wounded his son, "but that he, T. Béntara, if the perpetrator were not punished with at least banishment to [[Java]], would be *shamed* in the eyes of his people." The three months' sentence produced the stabbing.
- In September [[1913]] a young Acehnese thwarted by his parents in his marriage plans "felt himself thereby *shamed* before his village, thought he could live no longer, but decided to stab an unbeliever first" — and murdered a train conductor.
- In May [[1914]] an Acehnese attacked the sentry at [[Lam Meulò]] from shame that his mother had squandered his paternal inheritance and taken up with her own son-in-law: "So: shame, and therefore a murder."

## In attempted resistance

The same mechanism explains the local outbreaks: "an intellectual author who felt himself *shamed* over some trifle, and a number of actual executants who were in the same case, or who did not dare or could not refuse (thus likewise a case of fear of being shamed)."

In the [[Resistance in Daja and Lhong (1914)|Daja and Lhong affair]], [[Nja' Asém]] had risen greatly in esteem — "not only in the eyes of the population but also in his own" — through marriage with the daughter of the principal woman of the *[[moekim]]*, and was thrown entirely off balance by her sudden death, "by which he fell back to the status of an ordinary village man. This threw him entirely off balance and he began to brood."

## Significance

The concept of *maloe* is the interpretative key to du Croo's whole treatment of the residual violence of the pacification years, and the reason he could argue that the *Atjeh-moord* had, as a rule, no political significance whatever — while nevertheless requiring, for its investigation, an administrator "who needed no interpreter and who knew too much of Atjeh and the Acehnese to go astray."
