**Kompeni** — also spelled *compagnie*, the [[Achehnese]] and Dutch-Indies rendering of Dutch the Dutch East India Company, VOC — was the term the Achehnese used to refer to the [[Dutch]] colonial government in the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], long after the formal dissolution of the VOC in [[1799]]. Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* records the term in two distinct registers: as a label heard in enemy shouts during the [[4 April]] [[1896]] night attack on [[Lamkoenjit]], where the attackers identified their target as the *kompeni*; and in [[Teuku Umar]]'s own letters to the Governor of Atjeh, where Umar uses the term *Kompeni* to denote the Dutch colonial state whose enemies he offers to fight. The term's longevity — surviving a full century after the VOC's abolition — testifies to the depth of the Achehnese memory of European colonial presence as a commercial-military phenomenon originating in the *Compagnie*.

## Etymology and Meaning

The word *kompeni* is the Achehnese and Malay rendering of Dutch "company", itself from Old French *compaignie*. In the Indies context the term specifically denoted the VOC, Dutch United East India Company, 1602–1799, the chartered company that had been the original European colonial presence in the archipelago. Although the VOC was formally dissolved in [[1799]] and replaced by direct Dutch state rule, the term *kompeni* survived in indigenous usage as a generic label for the Dutch colonial government. The Achehnese phrase "Company men" specifically denoted Dutch personnel. The term was widely used across the archipelago — in Java, Sumatra, the Moluccas — wherever the VOC or its successors had established a presence.

## Historical Context

In the first semester of [[1896]] the term *kompeni* was the standard Achehnese label for the Dutch colonial government. The night attack on [[Lamkoenjit]] of [[4 April]]–[[5 April]] [[1896]] — attributed to *moslemin* — produced enemy shouts that identified the target as the *kompeni*. [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar|second letter]] to the Governor of Atjeh (Lampisang, [[29 Sawal]] 1313 / [[12 April]] [[1896]]) and his [[Third Letter of Teuku Umar|third letter]] (Lampisang, [[1 Dsoelkaidah]] 1313 / [[23 April]] [[1896]]) use the term *Kompeni* throughout: Umar promises to fight the enemies of the Kompeni but to exclude *oelebalangs* from military affairs, demands ƒ150,000 monthly to pay his warriors, and promises to hand the country over to the Kompeni when the war ends. The term thus functioned both as an Achehnese designation of the enemy and as a self-conscious ironic appropriation by Umar in his correspondence.

## Examples in the Source

Kruisheer records the term in two principal contexts. First, the [[4 April]]–[[5 April]] [[1896]] night attack on [[Lamkoenjit]]: the enemy shouted "kompeni" — using the term to identify the Dutch defenders. Second, [[Teuku Umar]]'s letters: his [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar|second letter]] of [[12 April]] [[1896]] (Bijlage IV) promises "to fight the enemies of the Kompeni"; his [[Third Letter of Teuku Umar|third letter]] of [[23 April]] [[1896]] (Bijlage V) promises "to hand the country over to the Kompeni when the war ends". Umar's use of the term in letters to the Governor of Atjeh — written in Malay with a Dutch intermediary — shows the term circulating in formal diplomatic correspondence, not merely in battlefield shouts. The Dutch text preserves the Achehnese spelling rather than the standard Dutch *Compagnie* to mark the term as an Achehnese usage.

## Significance

The *kompeni* label is a small but revealing indicator of the longue durée of Achehnese engagement with European colonial power. The survival of a VOC-era term into the [[1890s]] testifies to the depth of Achehnese historical memory and to the perceived continuity between the commercial-military *Compagnie* of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and the colonial state of the nineteenth. The Achehnese framing of the Dutch as *kompeni* — a commercial entity — sat alongside the religious framing as unbeliever and the political framing as a colonial state; the three framings were not exclusive but layered. Umar's strategic use of the term in his letters — promising to fight the enemies of the Kompeni while demanding payment — shows the term's malleability in diplomatic discourse.
