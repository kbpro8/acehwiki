**Bidan** was the Atjèhnese traditional **midwife** (*vroedvrouw*) who assisted in childbirth, performed magical and manual procedures, conducted hair-shaving of the newborn, performed circumcision on girls, and provided postpartum care. She received payment (*peunajah*, *seunalén*) after the 44-day period.

## Functions

The *bidan*'s functions included:
1. **Childbirth assistance** — managing the delivery, using techniques such as *ië seuloesoh* ("opening water" with magical formula), *Roempöt Patimah* (Rose of Jericho), and various physical manipulations
2. **Newborn care** — performing the hair-shaving of the newborn
3. **Female circumcision** (*peusoenat* for girls) — performed around age 2, kept very secret, using pincet (*seupét*), sonde (*tjeunoelö*), and a knife
4. **Postpartum care** — supervising the *madeuëng* / *meusalè* / *peudoeë'* (postpartum roasting) for 44 days
5. **Magical procedures** — performing *peutrön boeröng* (exorcism of the childbirth ghost), *meulingkeuë* (stepping over the laboring woman), and *mböïh* (blowing through the fist)

## The ië seuloesoh

The *ië seuloesoh* ("opening water") was given to a woman in difficult labor, prepared by the *bidan* who recited a magical formula (*doe'a seuloesoh*) over a bowl of water — meant to open everything closed and remove all obstacles. Sometimes prepared by putting a key in the water. The *Roempöt Patimah* ("Rose of Jericho," *Anastatica hierochuntica*) was soaked briefly in water and given to the birthing woman to drink.

## The husband's role

In difficult childbirth attributed to *toelah* / *teumeurka* — a state of being "struck by misfortune" believed caused by faults the woman committed against her husband — confession to the husband was one remedy. The *ië srah gaki laköë* ("foot-wash water of the husband") was given to the laboring woman to drink as humiliation before him — "the heaven of the wife is under the soles of her husband's feet."

## Payment

The *bidan* received payment (*peunajah*, *seunalén*) after the 44-day postpartum period. She was a central figure in the reproductive life of Atjèhnese women, combining practical midwifery with magical and religious elements.
