**Atjeh as a Republican stronghold during the Indonesian revolution**, as recorded in *[[De staatkundige ontwikkeling van Indonesië in de 20e eeuw|De staatkundige ontwikkeling van Indonesië in de 20e eeuw]]* (Amsterdam: [[Koninklijk Instituut voor de Tropen|Koninklijk Instituut voor de Tropen]]), is the characterization of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] as one of the three territorial holdouts of the Republik Indonesia after the two Dutch *politionele acties* (police actions) of [[1947]] and [[1948]]. After the Dutch military actions, the work records, the Netherlands succeeded in pushing the factual power of the Republik Indonesia back to "de moeilijk toegankelijke gebieden van het zuidelijke gedeelte van west- en midden-Java, Atjeh en onherbergzame streken op Sumatra en in zuid-Borneo" — the difficult-to-access areas of the southern part of west- and middle-Java, Atjeh, and inhospitable regions on Sumatra and in south-Borneo.

## The politionele acties and the Republican holdouts

The *politionele acties* — the two Dutch military operations against the Republik Indonesia during the Indonesian revolution — were the first (July–August [[1947]]) and the second (December [[1948]]) police actions. The first police action, the work records, was followed by strong international pressure, which was also a cause of the fact that, although the second police action (December [[1948]]) was a military success, it remained without the hoped-for political effect. Both parties — the Netherlands and the Republik Indonesia — saw no possibility of fully realising their objectives.

The result of the two police actions was that the factual power of the Republik Indonesia was pushed back to three categories of territory: the "moeilijk toegankelijke gebieden" (difficult-to-access areas) of the southern part of west- and middle-Java; **Atjeh**; and "onherbergzame streken op Sumatra en in zuid-Borneo" (inhospitable regions on Sumatra and in south-Borneo). Atjeh is thus recorded as one of the three Republican holdouts — and notably as the only one of the three identified by name rather than by geographical description, a distinction that reflects its particular importance as a Republican territory.

## Atjeh's exceptional position

The characterization of Atjeh as a named Republican stronghold — alongside the geographically described holdouts of "southern west- and middle-Java" and "inhospitable regions on Sumatra and in south-Borneo" — reflects Atjeh's exceptional position within the Indonesian revolution. Unlike the other Republican holdouts, which are described by their geographical inaccessibility, Atjeh is named outright, suggesting that its Republican allegiance was a matter of common knowledge. This exceptional position is consistent with the wider Atjehnese revolutionary history: Atjeh had been effectively self-governing under the Ulama since the [[Ulama–Uleëbalang social revolution in Atjeh (1945–1946)|1945–1946 social revolution]], and the Dutch had never re-established their authority there after the [[Japanese invasion of Atjeh|Japanese occupation]]. Atjeh's status as a Republican stronghold was thus not the result of the politionele acties pushing the Republic back to Atjeh, but rather the result of Atjeh's having been Republican — and indeed autonomous — throughout the revolution.

## The road to the R.T.C.

The work records that, via the Van Royen–Roem agreement and a ceasefire agreement in August [[1949]], the parties came to the holding of the Round Table Conference (Ronde Tafel Conferentie, R.T.C.), which led to the souvereiniteitsoverdracht — the transfer of sovereignty — to the representatives of the Republik Indonesia and the Bijeenkomst Federaal (B.F.O.) on [[27 December]] [[1949]] in the Koninklijk Paleis in Amsterdam. The Atjehnese Republican stronghold, having survived the politionele acties, thus passed — with the rest of the Republik Indonesia — into the independent Republic of the United States of Indonesia.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, the Van Meurs account of Atjeh as a Republican stronghold is significant as a concise, independent source for the position of Atjeh within the Indonesian revolution — a source that names Atjeh alongside the geographically described Republican holdouts and that thus marks Atjeh's exceptional position within the revolutionary geography. The account complements the corpus's existing entries on the [[Japanese invasion of Atjeh|Japanese occupation of Atjeh]], the [[Ulama–Uleëbalang social revolution in Atjeh (1945–1946)|1945–1946 social revolution]], and the wider Indonesian revolution, and it furnishes the metropolitan Dutch perspective on Atjeh's revolutionary status within the framework of the politionele acties and the souvereiniteitsoverdracht.
