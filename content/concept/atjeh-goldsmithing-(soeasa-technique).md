**Atjeh goldsmithing — the *soeasa* wrapping technique** — was the specifically Atjehnese method, described in the Dutch colonial craft-survey *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid*, of wrapping a copper core with a thin sheet of *soeasa* (a gold-copper alloy) to produce a gilded-or-silvered ornamental object. The technique is recorded as a distinctive practice of the Atjeh goldsmiths, illustrating the sultanate's place in the wider Indonesian precious-metal craft tradition.

## The *soeasa* alloy

*Soeasa* — known in various indigenous languages by that name, and called *spinsbek* in Dutch colonial parlance — was an alloy of equal parts gold and copper. "Het roodachtige metaal dat onder den naam van spinsbek bekend staat (in verschillende inlandsche talen: 'soeasa') en dat bestaat uit een mengsel van gelijke deelen goud en koper." The alloy's reddish tint, derived from the copper, gave the metal its distinctive colour. *Soeasa* was used across the archipelago as a cheaper alternative to high-karat gold for ornaments.

## The Atjeh wrapping technique

The *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid* describes the Atjeh technique in detail: "Bij de Atjehers b.v. wordt een stuk soeasa zoo plat gemaakt dat het geschikt is om een koperen staaf te omwikkelen, waarbij gezorgd wordt dat het dunne metaal goed tegen het koper aansluit. Nadat alles met een touwtje omwonden is, wordt het voorwerp in een mengsel van borax en water gehouden en daarna in een vuurtje van gloeiende kolen geplaatst. Zoodra de werkman bemerkt dat het metaal eenigszins begint te vloeien, koelt hij het af door er een kommetje water over te gooien. Op het aambeeld worden dan met een iizeren hamer de oneffenheden van het soeasa gelijk geslagen."

Translated: At Atjeh, for example, a piece of *soeasa* is flattened so thin that it is suitable for wrapping a copper rod, care being taken that the thin metal fits closely against the copper. After everything is bound with a string, the object is dipped in a mixture of borax and water and then placed in a fire of glowing coals. As soon as the workman notices that the metal has begun to flow slightly, he cools it by pouring a bowl of water over it. On the anvil, the irregularities of the *soeasa* are then hammered flat with an iron hammer.

The technique thus combines mechanical wrapping (the *soeasa* sheet is bound onto the copper core with string) with a brief heat-treatment (the borax-and-water dip followed by the coal-fire heating, then the water-quench) that bonds the *soeasa* to the copper by partial melting. The final hammering on the anvil removes the irregularities produced by the heat-treatment.

## Comparison with European practice

The same source notes that the *soeasa* wrapping technique stands in contrast to European goldsmithing, where the gilding or silvering of copper objects was achieved by plating (vergulden or verzilveren) rather than by wrapping with a thin sheet of precious metal: "Veelvuldig komt ook de versiering van koperen voorwerpen tot stand door deze niet, zooals bij ons, te vergulden of te verzilveren, maar ze met dun bladgoud te omwikkelen." The Atjeh technique is therefore a specifically Southeast Asian practice — distinct from both European plating and the alternative Indonesian practice of wrapping with thin gold-leaf.

## Atjeh goldsmithing in context

The Atjeh *soeasa* wrapping technique was applied in particular to arm- and foot-rings: "Vooral bij arm- en voetringen wordt deze werkwijze veel toegepast" — This method is especially applied to arm- and foot-rings. Arm- and foot-rings were standard items of personal adornment across the archipelago; the Atjeh use of *soeasa* wrapping for these objects placed the sultanate's goldsmiths within a wider regional craft tradition, while the specific *soeasa* technique gave the Atjeh products a distinctive material quality.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the *soeasa* wrapping technique is a small but concrete instance of the sultanate's craft tradition — a specifically Atjeh method of producing gilded ornaments, distinct from European plating and from other Indonesian practices. The technique also testifies to the place of Atjeh goldsmiths in the wider Sumatran and archipelago craft-world: the same source notes that the Padang Bovenlanden were known for their filigree (*filigraanwerk*) and that the *granuleeren* (granulation, "the ball-technique") of Bataks, Dyaks and Timorese was a different decorative technique again. Atjeh's distinctive contribution to that craft-geography was the *soeasa* wrapping of arm- and foot-rings.
