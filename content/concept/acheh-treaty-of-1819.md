The **Acheh treaty of 1819** was a nine-article commercial and political agreement concluded at [[Pedir]] on [[1819|22 April 1819]] between [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]], acting for the East India Company, and [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] of [[Acheh]]. The compact — long known to Dutch historiography by the title of P. H. van der Kemp's 1900 study, *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — would prove one of the most consequential "scraps of paper" in nineteenth-century Anglo-Dutch relations in the East Indian archipelago. Although described from the very day of signature as a [[Dead letter]], it was nonetheless retained by British diplomacy as a useful instrument with which to obstruct the consolidation of Dutch authority over [[Sumatra]] for more than half a century.

## Background and negotiation

By the close of the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] the throne of Acheh was contested between [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]], supported by the Sultan's party in the capital, and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Saif ul-Alum]] (Syf), who held [[Telok Semawé]]. [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|Captain Coombs' earlier mission]] of [[1817]]–[[1818]] had failed to produce a settlement, and the Bengal Government at [[Calcutta]] therefore equipped a joint commission under Raffles and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]], which arrived on the roadstead of [[Banda Acheh|Acheh]] in early [[1819]]. After a fruitless seven-week sojourn marked by an extraordinary [[Paper war]] between the two British commissioners, Raffles carried his preferred candidate and the commission sailed to Pedir, where the treaty was signed with Jauhar Alum.

## Substantive provisions

The treaty opened with an article of eternal friendship (Art. I) and a British promise to assist the Sultan in removing the rival pretender Syf (Art. II). Trade privileges, including fixed duties and a Sultan's undertaking not to grant monopolies (Art. III), were ceded to the British, together with the right to station an accredited agent at the Sultan's court (Art. IV). Article V preserved the commercial intercourse of British shipping with the ports of Acheh and Telok Semawé, subject only to such temporary blockades as the British Government itself might authorise. Most consequential were Article VI — by which the Sultan undertook to exclude the subjects of every other European power and all Americans from "fixed habitation or residence" in his dominions and to enter into no treaty without British knowledge and consent — and Article VII, by which he further agreed to refuse residence to any British subject objected to by the resident agent. Articles VIII and IX promised the Sultan immediate delivery of arms and a temporary loan, and provided for ratification by the Governor-General of Bengal within six months.

## A "dead letter" from the day of signing

Van der Kemp insists that the agreement "never was anything but a scrap of paper in the eyes of three English administrators themselves". Jauhar Alum never recovered even a fraction of his lost authority; the remaining years of his life were spent in a stockade at the mouth of the [[Acheh River]] without commanding influence or respect, while effective power in the capital lay with [[Panglima Polim]] and the coastal chiefs pursued their own independent courses. As [[Robert Fullerton|Governor Fullerton]] would later write in the [[Pinang Nota (1825)|Pinang Nota of 1825]], "no effect, political or commercial, resulted from the conclusion of that treaty which may truly be said to have been a dead letter from the day it was signed".

## Diplomatic afterlife

Notwithstanding its impotence on the ground, the treaty acquired a potent diplomatic afterlife. When the Anglo-Dutch negotiations that produced the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Treaty of London of 17 March 1824]] reached their final stage, the British plenipotentiaries — without, as Van der Kemp pointedly observes, any of the Dutch negotiators having actually read the Acheh instrument — secured an annexed declaration treating the 1819 treaty as incompatible with Article III of the new convention and undertaking its replacement by a mere arrangement for the hospitable reception of British ships. This Acheh reservation, although presented as inconsiderable, became the cornerstone of subsequent British remonstrances against the extension of Dutch supremacy in northern Sumatra. Its weight was still felt at the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra Treaty of 1871]], where the Netherlands finally obtained an English renunciation of "all remonstrances against the extension of Dutch authority in any part of the island of Sumatra".
