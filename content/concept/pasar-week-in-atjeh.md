**Pasar week in Atjeh** — the five-day market week (*pasar* week) that, alongside the Islamic seven-day week, structures the Atjehnese reckoning of time — is the dual-calendar system recorded in Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]]'s chapter *De invloed van den Islam* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]). The five-day *pasar* week is the indigenous Atjeh (and wider Malay-Indonesian) cycle of market days, in which each day is named for one of five market-places; the Islamic seven-day week, by contrast, is the religious cycle inherited from the Arabic Muslim calendar. The two cycles run in parallel, and their interaction generates the characteristic "selapan" cycle of 35 days.

## Kernkamp's account

Kernkamp introduces the *pasar* week in the course of his discussion of the Islamic calendar's interaction with indigenous Indonesian time-reckoning. His wording: "Sometimes one also connects the ecclesiastical and the civil calendar. Thus on Java the 'geboortedag' falls after 35 days (*selapan*), because that day bears the same name in both the *pasar* week of 5 days and the Muslim week of 7 days." The same *selapan* cycle — the 35-day period after which the *pasar* week and the Muslim week return to the same combination of names — governs, in Kernkamp's account, the timing of the Javanese *geboortedag* (the return of the birth-day after 35 days) and the associated *slametan* (the religious meal given to mark the occasion).

The *pasar* week is, in Atjeh as in Java, the older indigenous cycle alongside which the Islamic seven-day week has been adopted. The five-day cycle governs the market-days of the Atjeh village; the seven-day cycle governs the religious observances of the Atjeh Muslim. The two cycles coexist in Atjehnese time-reckoning, each governing a different domain of life — the *pasar* week the commercial and agricultural, the Muslim week the religious.

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the *pasar* week is significant as one of the surviving pre-Islamic Atjehnese time-reckoning systems. The same survival is documented in the [[Soerat te'h]] (Atjeh divination book), in the [[Atjeh calendar constellations|use of Scorpio and the Pleiades]] for the agricultural year, and in the [[Pen (Atjeh)|*pen* concept]] in ear-piercing and gravestone-placing. The *pasar* week thus belongs to the wider complex of pre-Islamic Atjehnese conceptions of time that, in Kernkamp's chapter, are shown to coexist with — and to interact with — the Islamic lunar calendar.
