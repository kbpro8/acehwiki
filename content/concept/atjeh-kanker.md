**Atjeh-kanker** (Aceh cancer) is a metaphorical concept used by socialist parliamentarian [[H. van Kol]] to describe the [[Aceh War]] and its detrimental effects on Dutch colonial and national interests. First articulated in the [[1897]] parliamentary debates, the phrase captured the idea that the war was a destructive, spreading, and ultimately fatal disease that must be "uitgesneden" (cut out) for the health of the colony and the nation.

## Origin in parliamentary debate

Van Kol introduced the concept during his speech in the [[Tweede Kamer]] on [[1 December]] [[1897]], in the context of the budget debates. After cataloguing the war's atrocities and consequences, he declared: "Wij zijn dus verplicht, in naam van menschelijkheid en recht, zoo snel mogelijk een einde te maken aan den Atjeh-krijg; de Atjeh-kanker moet worden uitgesneden." (We are therefore obliged, in the name of humanity and justice, to make an end to the Aceh war as soon as possible; the Aceh cancer must be cut out.)

The metaphor deliberately associated the war with a pathological process that was consuming the colony and would continue to spread if not surgically removed. It implied that the war was not a healing or civilizing process, but a destructive disease that had infected the body politic of the Dutch East Indies and the Netherlands itself.

## Later usage

Van Kol continued to use the concept in subsequent years. In the [[1901]] debates, he again referred to the war as a "kanker" that was being kept alive by capitalist interests — see [[Economic interests in the Aceh War]]. The metaphor was part of a broader critique of Dutch imperialism, which he argued was being driven by "kapitalistische hebzucht" (capitalist greed) and was causing "koloniale gruwelen" (colonial atrocities).

## Significance

The Atjeh-kanker metaphor is significant for several reasons. First, it represents a deliberate inversion of the official colonial narrative, which often framed the war as a necessary sanitary measure or a civilizing mission. By calling the war itself a cancer, Van Kol challenged the legitimacy of the entire colonial project. Second, the metaphor was later used by other anti-colonial critics and historians to describe the destructive and self-perpetuating nature of colonial warfare. Third, the concept of "cutting out" the cancer implied a radical solution: immediate withdrawal, not reform. This distinguished Van Kol's analysis from liberal critics who advocated for more humane methods of warfare. The term remains a powerful rhetorical artifact of the anti-war movement.
