**The Volksraad** — the People's Council — was the advisory representative body for the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Nederlandsch-Indische]] archipelago whose installation was proclaimed in the *Staatsblad* of [[1916]] and which was actually installed in [[May 1918|May]] [[1918]]. The Volksraad is, in the [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] retrospective, the type-specimen of the *ethische politiek* state's chronic lateness — its proclamation in [[1916]] and its installation in [[1918]] came too late to anchor moderate opinion in the [[Sarekat Islam]] and to contain the radical wing of Indonesian nationalism.

## The 1916 proclamation

By the end of [[1916]] the institution of the Volksraad was proclaimed in the *Staatsblad* of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Nederlandsch-Indische]] government. The instrument was, however, not actually implemented until [[May 1918|May]] [[1918]] — an eighteen-month delay that, in [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]]'s verdict, made it harder for the moderate leadership of the [[Sarekat Islam]] to keep its radical wing in check.

## The 1918 installation

The Volksraad was finally installed in [[May 1918|May]] [[1918]]. The delay was a recurrent pattern of Dutch colonial governance in the [[Ethische Politiek|ethical-policy]] era: political concessions came too late to capture the "psychologisch ogenblik" — psychological moment — at which they could have anchored moderate opinion. The colonial state "bijna steeds iets te laat [kwam], zodat het psychologisch ogenblik gemist werd" — almost always came a little too late, so that the psychological moment was missed.

## The Pleyte defence

[[Th. B. Pleyte|Pleyte]] (lib.), minister of Colonies [[1913]]-[[1918]], defended the Volksraad as a step in the political education of the Indies population. The political awakening of the Indies — even after the outbreak of the First World War — was, in his judgement, "een veel aangenamer en veel grootser taak" — a much more pleasant and much greater task — for a ruler "dan een slapend volk er onder te houden" — than to keep a sleeping people under. He ventured, despite the political difficulties of the past year, to make possible a greater freedom of association and assembly (*vereniging en vergadering*) — a measure that his predecessor [[D. Fock|Fock]] had already discussed with governor-general [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]].

## The Sarekat Islam context

The slowness of Volksraad implementation made it difficult for the moderate leadership of the [[Sarekat Islam]] to keep its radical wing in check. The [[Sarekat Islam|Sarekat Islam]] — the major indigenous political association of the period — had pressed for the Volksraad; the colonial state's slow tempo in implementing it did half the work of the radical wing. The post-[[1918]] radicalisation of the [[Sarekat Islam|Sarekat Islam]] is, in part, the consequence of the Volksraad delay.

## The Bergsma counter-current

The Volksraad had its critics. [[Mr J. H. Bergsma|Bergsma]] (lib.) — minister of Colonies [[1897]]-[[1901]] — had posed the thought-experiment: if one were to ask 20 million *inlanders* whether they wished for municipal councils and regional councils, they would reply "och, geef ons geen nieuwe bevelen ... laat ons dan verder ons zelf kalm ontwikkelen en tot rust komen" — oh, give us no new orders ... let us then further calmly develop ourselves and come to rest. The Bergsma counter-argument is, in the [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] retrospective, the type-specimen of the *ethische politiek* counter-current that doubted whether the indigenous population actually desired the representative institutions that the colonial state was constructing for it.

## Significance

The Volksraad is, in the [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] retrospective, the type-specimen of the *ethische politiek* state's chronic lateness. Its proclamation in [[1916]] and installation in [[1918]] came too late to anchor the moderate wing of the [[Sarekat Islam|Sarekat Islam]]; the post-[[1918]] radicalisation of Indonesian nationalism is, in part, the consequence.

## Account in du Croo’s Generaal Swart (1943)

[[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] "is not afraid of the representative system; he wants a People's Council," said [[J. W. Meijer Ranneft]], chairman of the Volksraad, at the unveiling of the [[Van Heutsz-Monument te Batavia|Van Heutsz Monument]]. *[[De Locomotief]]* had objected in [[1918]] that "now, however, that the People's Council has ushered in a new era for colonial development," the daily leadership of the Council of the Indies would better have been entrusted to a man more familiar with the political currents in the colony. Three years later the *[[Java-Bode]]* recorded: "For many, the interest he always showed in the People's Council has been surprising; he was often an attentive visitor at its sittings. A man who, after a career through the autocratic schools of the Outer Possessions, followed the rise of parliamentarism with so much interest proved that he understood the voice of the times."
