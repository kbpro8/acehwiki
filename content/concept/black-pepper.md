**Black Pepper (Aceh)** (Dutch: *zwarte peper*) was a major export spice of the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] and later of the colonial residency of [[Atjeh]], produced from unripe pepper berries harvested as part of a pepper bunch (Dutch: *pepertros*) and dried on the floor until the fruit flesh shrivelled and turned black. Together with [[White Pepper (Aceh)|white pepper]], it formed the backbone of Aceh's pre-colonial trade with Europe and the Islamic world, and was one of the principal commodities through which the sultanate projected economic power across the [[Strait of Malacca]] in the [[16th century|sixteenth]] and [[17th century|seventeenth centuries]]. After the [[Aceh War|Aceh War of 1873]] and the subsequent [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]], black pepper remained a mainstay of the smallholder economy of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and the [[Westkust van Atjeh]] until its gradual decline in the early twentieth century.

## Cultivation and processing

The pepper vine (*Piper nigrum*) was trained up living support trees — typically the *gliricidia*, *dadap* or *Erythrina* — in the pepper gardens (Dutch: *pepertuinen*) that surrounded every Acehnese village in the pepper districts. The berries were harvested in bunches (Dutch: *pepertros*) while still unripe, then spread on a flat mat or earth floor and dried in the sun. As the fruit flesh dried, it shrivelled and turned black, producing the familiar wrinkled black peppercorn of commerce. The alternative product, [[White Pepper (Aceh)|white pepper]], was made by soaking ripe berries to remove the outer husk before drying, a more labour-intensive process that yielded a smoother, lighter-coloured kernel.

Acehnese pepper was renowned for its pungency and high piperine content, and the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] factors at [[Banda Acheh|Kota Radja]] in the seventeenth century regularly noted that "Atchinse peper" commanded a premium over the produce of [[Java]] and [[Malacca|Malakka]]. The pepper gardens of the Pidië coast and the [[Westkust van Atjeh|West Coast]] were the demographic and economic heart of the pre-colonial sultanate, supporting dense populations of smallholder cultivators.

## Decline of the Acehnese pepper trade

The quality and volume of Acehnese black pepper declined markedly in the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]] owing to insufficient agronomic practices, disease in the older plantings, and competition from European-managed pepper gardens on [[Java]] and [[Bangka]]. The protracted [[Aceh War|Aceh War]] from [[1873]] disrupted cultivation, destroyed pepper gardens, and drove smallholders from the land; many of the older pepper terraces were abandoned during the guerrilla phase of the war and never replanted. After the [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]] of the early twentieth century, the [[Dutch East Indies|colonial administration]] attempted to revive pepper cultivation through extension work, but the price advantage had shifted to the more systematically managed estates of [[Bangka]] and to the new plantation crops of [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]], [[Copra|copra]] and [[Palm Oil (Aceh)|palm oil]].

The opening of the [[Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen|Atjeh railway]] and the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajo Road]] in the early twentieth century accelerated the shift away from pepper toward rubber, coconut, and coffee cultivation. The [[Agrarische Wet 1870]] (Agrarian Law of 1870), by allowing European leasehold estates on indigenous land, also contributed to the displacement of the smallholder pepper economy in favour of plantation crops grown for the European market.

## Trade and historical significance

Historically, black pepper was a cornerstone of Aceh's trade with European markets and with the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] in the seventeenth century, when the sultanate was one of the principal pepper suppliers of the Indian Ocean network. The pepper revenues of Atjeh remained significant enough that the Encyclopaedisch Bureau, in its [[1913]] survey of the [[Buitengewesten|Buitengewesten]] landschapskassen, recorded a *peperhasil* of f 125,000 for the [[Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]] landschapskas — the largest miscellaneous pepper revenue of any Buitenbezittingen gewest. The decline of pepper signalled a broader shift in the colonial economy of [[Atjeh]] toward plantation rubber, copra, and palm oil in the early twentieth century. By the [[1930s]] pepper had ceased to be a major Acehnese export, though it remained a smallholder crop in parts of the interior.
