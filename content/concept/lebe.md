**Lébé** — Dutch/Atjehnese spelling of standard Indonesian *lebe*, from Malay *lebai* (itself from Arabic *laʾib* or related forms), in Atjehnese *leubé* — is the title of the village-level Islamic religious official in the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Dutch East Indies]], the lowest grade of the religious-official hierarchy that ascends through the *modin* and the *panghoeloe* to the *kadi*. The term is recorded in Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]]'s chapter *De invloed van den Islam* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]).

## Kernkamp's account

Kernkamp introduces the *lebé* in the course of his discussion of the *djakat* (zakat) collection. His wording: "In West Java one knows a hierarchical collection by the religious officials, beginning in the *desa* and with the top at the Regentship-head-place. There the *lebé*'s go round in the villages in harvest month and get from each *tani* some *padi*. (In the [[Gajoland|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas]] lands, the *imam* does this, for example.) Elsewhere, for example in Atjeh, the candidates wait calmly for what one wishes to bring them." The West-Javanese *lebé* is thus the village-level collector of the agricultural tithe; in [[Gajoland|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas]] the same function is performed by the *imam*, and in Atjeh proper by no hierarchical collector at all.

The same chapter elsewhere distinguishes the *lebé* from the village Quran-teacher (*goeroe ngadji*). In West Java, Kernkamp notes, "one will also never forget the Quran-teacher (*Goeroe ngadji*), who has given the children some religious instruction. If there the local *adat* brings it that one brings the *pitrah* to the *lebé* or *modin* of one's own *kampong*, and the *goeroe-ngadji* lives far away (which happens there), then one even pays this religious tax twice".

## Atjeh form: *leubé*

The Atjehnese form of the term is *leubé* (so spelled in the corpus, e.g. in the description of the Atjeh *Teungku* in Kernkamp's chapter: "the Teungkoe, the 'mother of the *gampong*' ... who ... is expected to be *leubé*, that is, 'pious', and to faithfully perform his religious duties"). In Atjeh, the *leubé* virtue (piety and the faithful performance of religious duties) is an expectation of the [[Teungku|Teungkoe]] office rather than a separate office itself; the institutional separation between the *lebé* (as village religious official) and the *teungku* (as mosque/meunasah director and religious teacher) that obtains in West Java does not obtain in Atjeh, where the *teungku* combines the two functions. The Atjeh *leubé* is thus less an office than a quality — the quality of piety expected of the village religious teacher.

## Significance

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the *lebé* / *leubé* is significant as one of the principal village-level Islamic offices of the Indies, and as a marker of the institutional difference between the hierarchical West-Javanese system (in which the *lebé* collects the *djakat* village by village) and the looser Atjeh system (in which the *teungku* combines religious and educational functions, and the *djakat* is collected on a "wait and see" basis at the [[Meunasah|meunasah]]). The same difference, in Kernkamp's account, marks the Atjeh case as institutionally less developed than the West-Javanese — a difference that fits the wider pattern of Atjeh Islam being presented as more vigorous in practice but looser in institutional structure than the Javanese variant.
