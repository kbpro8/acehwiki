**Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur** (Regional Self-Governance) was the form of governance that Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] concluded was the only viable option for the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] during the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]]. Unlike the local zelfbestuur (local self-governance through autonome lichamen / autonomous bodies) proposed for other Buitenbezittingen, Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur involved the transfer of certain branches of algemeen burgerlijk bestuur (general civil administration) to lower organs—specifically the gewest (region)—rather than creating autonomous bodies at the local level. This distinction was fundamental to the reform proposals: where other territories would receive local autonomy through self-governing municipalities or districts, Atjeh's unique political landscape demanded a different approach that concentrated decentralized authority at the regional tier rather than distributing it downward.

The concept of Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur thus represented a compromise between the centralizing tendencies of the colonial state and the recognition that Atjeh's governance structures could not simply be subsumed under the standard decentralisatie framework being applied elsewhere in the Buitenbezittingen. It acknowledged that while some form of administrative decentralization was both desirable and necessary, the particular conditions prevailing in Atjeh—especially the coexistence of directly ruled and self-governing territories within a single governmental unit—precluded the more ambitious forms of local autonomy envisioned for other regions.

## Rationale and Context

The reason Atjeh could not receive local zelfbestuur was explained by Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meeting ([[19 January]] [[1914]]): only [[Groot-Atjeh]] and part of [[Singkel]] were rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied (directly ruled territory), while the remainder of the Gouvernement consisted of Inlandsche Zelfbesturen (Native Self-Governments/landschappen). The coexistence of directly ruled territory and self-governing landschappen within a single governmental unit meant that creating additional autonomous local bodies was impractical. This dual structure was unique among the Buitenbezittingen, where other governmental units tended to be either entirely under direct rule or wholly composed of self-governing territories, but not both simultaneously. The resulting administrative complexity rendered the standard model of local zelfbestuur unworkable in the Atjeh context.

De Graaff therefore concluded: "ook voor genoemd gouvernement bij den tegenwoordigen staat van zaken geene andere oplossing bestaat dan zich te beperken tot Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur" (for the said government, given the present state of affairs, no other solution exists than to limit itself to Regional Self-Governance). This conclusion was not merely an administrative convenience but reflected a fundamental assessment of Atjeh's political realities. The Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden had been gradually assembled through decades of military expansion and political consolidation, resulting in a patchwork of governance arrangements that defied the neat categorizations required by the broader [[Bestuursdecentralisatie]] program. The Inlandsche Zelfbesturen that occupied much of the Gouvernement's territory already possessed their own indigenous rulers and governance traditions, making the superimposition of yet another layer of autonomous local bodies both redundant and potentially disruptive.

## Relationship to Bestuursdecentralisatie

De Graaff also noted that the broader reorganisatie was principally about bestuursdecentralisatie (administrative decentralization), but that granting greater independence to the Inlandsch bestuur was "voor dit gewest vooralsnog van minder beteekenis" (of less significance for this territory for the time being). This qualification was significant because it signaled that the colonial administration recognized the limitations of applying uniform reform principles across vastly different territories. While the general thrust of the reorganization was toward greater local autonomy and self-governance, Atjeh's particular circumstances—including the still-tenuous nature of Dutch control in some areas, the complex relationship between the colonial government and the indigenous [[Inlandsch Bestuur]], and the legacy of decades of military conflict—meant that a more cautious, regionally-focused approach was warranted. The Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur model thus became the practical expression of this cautious pragmatism, offering a form of decentralization that was real but limited, concentrated at the regional level rather than dispersed throughout the complex local governance landscape.

The adoption of Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur for Atjeh also had implications for the proposed [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]]. By concentrating decentralized authority at the gewest level, the reform effectively subordinated Atjeh's regional governance to the new provincial structure, rather than allowing it to develop its own internal autonomy through local self-governing bodies. This was consistent with the broader objective of integrating Atjeh more fully into the administrative framework of Sumatra, a goal that had been discussed since the earlier phases of the reorganization at Palembang and Padang in 1913, where Controleur [[J. Tideman]] and Hoofdambtenaar [[J. de Groot]] had already debated the merits and risks of merging Atjeh with Sumatra's Oostkust.
