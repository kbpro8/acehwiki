**Uleebalang** (also *uleëbalang*, lit. from Malay *orang balang* — "warrior") were the hereditary territorial chieftains who formed the backbone of [[Great Acheh|Acehnese]] political and military structure. They were simultaneously military leaders, administrators, collectors of revenue, and judges within their territories, which consisted of one or more [[Mukim|mukims]] and their constituent [[Gampong|gampongs]].

## Functions

An uleebalang served three primary functions:

**Military leader** — commanding the fighting men of his territory in war, both in offensive operations and in defence against rivals or against the Dutch during the [[Aceh War]].

**Administrator** — maintaining order and resolving disputes within his territory, arbitrating land sales, collecting tolls and levies, and overseeing the [[Keuchi'|keuchi']] (village headmen) and [[Imeum Mukim|imeum mukims]] below him.

**Judge** — deciding civil and criminal disputes, collecting deposits (*ha' ganchëng*) from parties to disputes as a pledge, and taking a portion of the settled debt as recompense.

## Revenue

The three chief uleebalangs (*panglima sagi*) used to receive from the sultan, on his accession to the throne, a "wedding gift" (*jinamëë*) amounting to $500. Against this it became the established rule for a newly constituted panglima sagi to pay at least an equal sum to the sultan for the letters patent confirming his appointment. Revenue also came from: tolls on trade passing through their territory; land-sale fees (1% of every rice-field sold); blood-money (*[[Bila|bila]]*) levied from guilty parties; and their share of harbour dues and port revenues.

## The Three Sagi Chiefs

The most powerful uleebalangs were the three *panglima sagi* — the chiefs of the three [[Sagi System|sagis]] of [[Great Acheh]]:
- The chief of the XXII Mukims (highland sagi)
- The chief of the XXV Mukims
- The chief of the XXVI Mukims

## Relationship to the Sultan

The uleebalangs were theoretically subordinate to the [[Aceh Sultanate|Sultan]] but in practice were largely autonomous, especially as the sultanate weakened. During the [[Aceh War]], the uleebalangs of the coastal districts were generally more inclined toward accommodation with the Dutch than the highland uleebalangs, who were more easily influenced by the [[Teungku|teungkus]] (religious leaders).

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Oelèëbalang

**The oelèëbalang** was the territorial chief of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and the surrounding Atjehsche lands — "de heeren des lands, de territoriale hoofden bij uitnemendheid" — whom books and speech alike called the *radja*'s of their districts. *De Buitenbezittingen*, following Prof. Dr. [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] (*De Atjehers*, Vol. I, p. 91 e.v.), describes the oelèëbalangs as lords whose authority was "ouder dan dat der Sultans" — older than that of the Sultans themselves — and therefore not in fact derived from any royal appointment. After [[1874|1874]] they were the chiefs with whom the Dutch concluded the [[Korte Verklaring|Korte Verklaring]], recognising each as *zelfbestuurder* of his own *landschap*.

## Title and territory

The Atjehsche language had no single word for an oelèëbalang's territory comparable to *gampong* or *moekim*. It was described variously as *nanggrèë* (negari, land), *seuneumat* (from *mat* = to encompass), or *makan* (district), or by Malay terms such as *djadjahan* (dependency) and *daërah*. The handbook uses *oelèëbalangschap*, later reduced in official documents to *landschap* — landscape or polity — "zoodra de landstreek als zelfbestuur was erkend en ten bewijze daarvan door den oelèëbalang de zoogenaamde 'Korte Verklaring' was geteekend." The compact "regelt zijne verhouding tot het Nederlandsch-Indisch Gouvernement."

## Antiquity and recognition

Although their authority did not derive from royal appointment, the oelèëbalangs of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and of the Onderhoorigheden "stelden veel prijs op een acte van erkenning vanwege het hof te [[Koeta Radja|Koeta-Radja]]" — set great store by a recognition act from the Sultan's court — "hoewel zij hunne rechten geenszins daarvan afhankelijk beschouwden." Such acts were the [[Sarakata|sarakata]]'s issued by the [[Sultans of Atjeh|Sultan]]. A full description of the oelèëbalang's combined functions as "legeraanvoerder, bestuurder en rechter" — army commander, administrator, and judge — lies outside the handbook's scope, but the institution's antiquity is stressed throughout.

## Federation in the Sagi

In [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] the oelèëbalangschappen were grouped, again in immemorial fashion, into the three federations called the [[Sagi|sagi]]'s — the XXII, XXVI, and XXV Moekims — each with its *Panglima sagi* chosen from among the constituent chiefs. [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s explanation, quoted in the handbook, is that "evenals nu de gampongs, die op zichzelf staande aan de genade en ongenade van elken geweldenaar overgeleverd zouden zijn, hare kracht vonden in de vereeniging onder eenen oeleëbalang, zoo moesten wel de oelèëbalangs in hun onderlingen strijd het nut van een offensief en defensief verbond gaan inzien." Through this defensive logic the oelèëbalangs of Atjeh maintained, for centuries, a stable balance against one another and against the Sultan.
