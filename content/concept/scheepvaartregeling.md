**The Scheepvaartregeling** (literally "shipping regulation", also "maritime trade regulation") was the Dutch system of controlled maritime trade on the coasts of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] — first proposed by [[Scherer|Scherer]] in [[May 1881|May]] [[1881]], first introduced on a part of the [[Noordkust|North Coast]] in [[May 1882|May]] [[1882]], first applied throughout Atjeh in [[May 1883|May]] [[1883]], withdrawn by the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[October 1884|October]] [[1884]] (except on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]], where it was maintained), and finally and definitively introduced in [[October 1893|October]] [[1893]] for the East Coast and [[April 1894|April]] [[1894]] for the North and West Coasts — that [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]] identifies as the principal strategic instrument neglected by the Dutch throughout the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Its consistent application, Kruijt argues, was the principal means by which the Dutch could have mastered the coasts and so ended the war; its half-hearted and inconsistent application was, in Kruijt's analysis, the principal cause of the protraction of the war from [[1873]] to [[1896]].

## Scherer's Doctrine

The doctrine of the *Scheepvaartregeling* was set out most fully by the *Assistent-Resident* [[Scherer|Scherer]] in his lecture of [[20 January]] [[20 January]] [[1891]] before the [[Indisch Genootschap|Indisch Genootschap]] at 's-Gravenhage on the subject "*Hoe moet Atjeh gepacificeerd worden*" (How Atjeh must be pacified). The resistance came not from [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] but from the *onderhoorigheden* (dependencies) — the [[Oostkust|East]], [[Noordkust|North]], and [[Westkust|West Coasts]]. There the money was earned with which the costs of the war were met; there the young fanatical people were gathered to make incursions into Groot-Atjeh; and there the resistance retired in perfect safety when the Dutch put the fire too close to the shins of the guerrillas in Groot-Atjeh. "*De meester van de kust is meester van het land*" (The master of the coast is the master of the land), Scherer held.

The *Scheepvaartregeling* was the means by which the Dutch could exercise political influence on the coasts at low cost and with decisive effect. A few Dutch-controlled harbours for general trade were opened on the coasts — [[Edi|Edi]] and [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]] on the East Coast, [[Poeloe Rajja|Poeloe Rajja]] on the West Coast, and so on. All trade had to pass through these harbours under Dutch supervision. Import and export clearance, visitation, and the levying of import and export duties took place there by Dutch personnel. The products of surrounding statelets were transported to the commercial centres so created by small coastal vessels, while imports moved in the opposite direction. The revenues came into Dutch hands. The Dutch sat on the money-chest. The Sultan's share — formally the Dutch's by right of succession to the Sultanate — came in regularly; the remainder was periodically paid out to the rightful claimants who had given no cause for complaint. To those who misbehaved, fines were imposed, deducted from their money; or, according to the gravity of their offence, the whole was withheld, or their harbour was closed to all import and export.

## The 1881 Proposal and the 1882 Partial Introduction

Already in [[May 1881|May]] [[1881]] Scherer, stationed at the post established at [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]] in [[1881]], set out to the new Governor [[Pruijs van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]] the necessity of a rational *Scheepvaartregeling*. The Governor was not persuaded: *C'était precher un converti*. By the pressure of circumstances the Governor gave Scherer, in [[May 1882|May]] [[1882]], the freedom to introduce the regulation on a part of the [[Noordkust|North Coast]]. Howsoever only partially and with defective means applied, it worked excellently.

## The 1883 General Introduction and the 1884 Withdrawal

In [[May 1883|May]] [[1883]] the Governor [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]], equally convinced of its great usefulness, ordered, under further approval of the government, the introduction of Scherer's *Scheepvaartregeling* in all Atjeh. Even though unprepared and with inadequate means, it worked excellently under Scherer's able leadership — until, in [[October 1884|October]] [[1884]], by order of the High Government in the Netherlands, it was wholly withdrawn and even the abolition of all posts on the coasts was decided. Only on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]], where it had worked for ten years, was it maintained, in order not to lose all influence there as well.

## The Van Teijn Memorandum of August 1889

The later Governor [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]] continued to urge the *Scheepvaartregeling* on the government. In [[August 1889|August]] [[1889]] he wrote:

> "*Hoe meer ik kennis maak met den aard en het karakter van den Atjeher, hoe meer ik gevestigd raak in mijne overtuiging, dat wij steeds een middel in de hand moeten hebben om zonder veel bezwaar dadelijk te kunnen straffen, indien ons gezag niet wordt geëerbiedigd. Daartoe geeft de scheepvaartregeling gelegenheid. 't Zij door heffen van boeten, in te vorderen door naasting voor korteren of langeren duur van de in- en uitvoerrechten; 't zij door allen in- of uitvoer te verbieden. Zonder deze regeling zullen wij nimmer ons gezag op de kuststaatjes kunnen handhaven en zullen wij nimmer voldoenden invloed in Groot-Atjeh kunnen uitoefenen.*"

[[Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] confirmed all these opinions in full in his excellent and clear brochure "*[[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz)|De onderwerping van Atjeh]]*", published in [[1898]].

## The 1893–1894 Definitive Introduction

How fully [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]] himself was convinced of the good effect of the blockade and considered its strict and permanent maintenance necessary, he had already from the beginning, with the Resident [[Scherer|Scherer]], arranged the preparation of a *Scheepvaartregeling*. Scherer came over to Atjeh for this purpose, visited the various coastal statelets, had discussions with the chiefs — who, in the prospect of being released from the straitening bands of the blockade, were glad to see him come. Only [[Pedir|Pedir]] refused to accept the conditions set. The *Scheepvaartregeling* replaced the blockade as Scherer's regulation was gradually introduced: in [[October 1893|October]] [[1893]] for the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] and in [[April 1894|April]] [[1894]] for the [[Noordkust|North]] and [[Westkust|West Coasts]], with respectively [[Edi|Edi]], [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]], and [[Poeloe Rajja|Poeloe Rajja]] opened as harbours for general trade.

This measure was, alas, also not carried through consistently, not vigorously and forcefully applied. Not only not as intended by Van Teijn (after submission obtained by blockade), but inadequately prepared and therefore prematurely introduced, and again at once weakened by special licences and concessions. The Dutch were not sitting on the money-chest, they were sitting beside it, and it had all the appearance as if it were more instituted to please the Atjehers than to put power in the hands of the Dutch.

## Kruijt's Judgement

Kruijt's judgement of the *Scheepvaartregeling* is the most sustained strategic argument of his brochure. Already under [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] — who had perceived that it was the *onderhoorigheden* that fed the resistance in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], that drew profit from the war, that had an interest in its continuation, and that therefore without supervision of the coasts the end was not foreseeable — the necessity of coastal control had been recognised. Van der Heijden had, against the wish of the government at Batavia, introduced such control on his own responsibility in [[July 1879|July]] [[1879]]; the government had acquiesced, on condition that it should be temporary; and it had been abolished in [[1881]], to be greatly regretted — for, whatever its initial shortcomings, it contained the principle of the *Scheepvaartregeling*, and might already then have become what was only instituted ten years later and ought to have been from the start.

The protraction of the war from [[1873]] to [[1896]] was, in Kruijt's analysis, principally the consequence of the Dutch failure to apply the *Scheepvaartregeling* consistently. Every Dutch governor who had had experience of the coasts — Van der Heijden, Scherer, Laging Tobias, Van Teijn, Deijkerhoff — had urged it; every Dutch government — in the Netherlands and in the Indies — had refused or half-heartedly applied it. The Dutch's "penny-wise and pound-foolish" parsimony had reduced the [[Koninklijke Marine|Marine]] in Atjeh to four ships, so that proper supervision of the coasts was no longer possible. The Dutch's blind trust in the policy of arming [[Teuku Umar|Teuku Umar]] had substituted a far more costly and far more dangerous policy for the cheap and effective instrument that the *Scheepvaartregeling* provided.

## Significance

The *Scheepvaartregeling* is the central strategic concept of Kruijt's brochure and the principal strategic instrument of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Its consistent application was, in Kruijt's analysis, the principal means by which the Dutch could have ended the war earlier; its half-hearted and inconsistent application was the principal cause of the protraction of the war. The doctrine that "*de meester van de kust is meester van het land*" (the master of the coast is the master of the land), first articulated by Scherer and confirmed by Van Teijn and Van Heutsz, would be vindicated by the subsequent Dutch victory under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] after [[1898]], which rested on the systematic application of the *Scheepvaartregeling* and the marechaussée campaigns of the late 1890s and early 1900s. The *Scheepvaartregeling* is, with the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]], one of the two principal Dutch strategic concepts of the war, and the one whose neglect Kruijt most deplores.
