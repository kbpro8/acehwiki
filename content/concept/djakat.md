**Djakat** — Atjehnese/Dutch spelling of Arabic *zakāt* (زكاة), the obligatory alms-tax on wealth, "the third of the five pillars of Islam" — is the contribution that every Muslim must pay on certain categories of property (agricultural produce, livestock, merchandise, gold and silver) at fixed rates, for distribution to specified categories of recipients named in the Quran (the poor, the needy, those in debt, etc.). The Atjeh form of the *djakat*, and its collection, is recorded in Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]]'s chapter *De invloed van den Islam* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]).

## The Atjeh djakat

Kernkamp's account of the *djakat* places it within the broader Indonesian context of Islamic obligatory contributions. He notes that "the Islam knows yet a second religious tax — and then no more: what is further demanded by superiors is of the Devil! — which consists of the tithes of various agricultural products and (under certain conditions) a smaller percentage of livestock, merchandise, and gold and silver. In the Indies, one pays almost exclusively the tithes of the harvest, a few very pious persons not excepted. In a land with a non-Muslim government, no collection or distribution of it naturally takes place on the part of the fiscus. The tax, called *djakat* on Java, which forms the third of the 5 pillars of Islam, is therefore paid entirely voluntarily, as is also the *pitrah*."

In Atjeh, Kernkamp further specifies, the *djakat* is collected by the *imam* (rather than by the *lebé* as in West Java): "in West Java one knows a hierarchical collection by the religious officials, beginning in the *desa* and with the top at the Regentship-head-place. There the *lebé*'s go round in the villages in harvest month and get from each *tani* some *padi*. (In the Gajo and Alas lands, the *imam* does this, for example.) Elsewhere, for example in Atjeh, the candidates wait calmly for what one wishes to bring them." The contrast between the hierarchical West-Javanese collection and the Atjeh "wait and see" approach is characteristic of the looser institutional structure of Atjeh Islam, in which the village *imam* waits at the *meunasah* for the householders to bring their contributions rather than going round to collect them.

## Significance

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the *djakat* is significant as one of the three principal Islamic obligatory contributions (alongside the [[Pitrah|*pitrah*]] and the *zakāt* on mineral wealth) that Kernkamp documents for the Indies. The Atjeh form of collection — by the village *imam* at the *meunasah*, on a "wait and see" basis — places the village prayer-house and its *imam* at the institutional centre of Atjeh Islamic life, alongside the *pitrah* collection at the same venue. The same chapter notes that, in Atjeh, the *kadi* (religious judge, pronounced *kali*) had historically been independent, but that "his jurisdiction has been usurped by the secular heads, who now use them only as unsalaried advisers" — a development that places the Atjeh *djakat* collection in a context of institutional erosion of the religious-legal apparatus.
