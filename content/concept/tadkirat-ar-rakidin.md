**Tadkirat ar-rakidïn** ("Vermaning der stilzittenden" — "Admonition to the Sitter-Still," i.e. those who sit by passively; Arabic *Tadhkirat ar-rākinūt*, "exhortation to the inactive") was a series of Arabic-titled political-religious pamphlets distributed by [[Teungkoe Koetakarang|Teungkoe Koetakarang]] (real name Nja Abbas), containing political-religious teachings calling for better organisation and greater energy against the Dutch, invoking the *tjab limöng* (fivefold seal).

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], who succeeded in obtaining three copies, classes it with the politico-religious literature of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]] and calls it "the principal monument of his pen and one of the most important specimens of Achehnese politico-religious literature."

## Contents and argument

The pamphlets called for better organisation of the Atjèhnese resistance, greater energy in opposing the Dutch, and religious motivation for the struggle — invoking the **tjab limöng** (fivefold seal), part of the esoteric apparatus of resistance linking oelama-authority with political-military action.

In the *Tadkirat-ar-rakinut*, the Dutch are represented as outcasts of humanity whose aim is the destruction of the countries over which they hold sway, while at the same time attention is drawn to their powerlessness in respect of Acheh. The Achehnese, the Teungku proceeds, are well able to expel the Dutch; even the very babes are best appeased by being offered a sword as a toy. The country is rich enough in munitions of war and wants nothing but good generalship. The Sultan he hardly mentions, employing only the similitude of the marriage of the Sultans with the State of Acheh to give point to the remark that a bridegroom who will not lift a finger to save his bride from the stranger is unworthy of her.

## Critique of the chiefs and the rival ulamas

The uleebalangs, the pamphlet continues, hold no consultation with the ulamas as they ought to do; they think only of their own particular interests, and by reason of the "louse questions" (so runs the Teungku's favourite metaphor) which cause disruption between them, they see not the "elephant" which threatens the whole nation. The people of the gampongs are also to blame, for many of them imitate the inactivity of the uleebalangs and withdraw from all share in the jihad on the pretext that this duty rests not on the individual but on the community in general.

Even the ulamas come in for a share of the blame. Some sit on their prayer-carpets or lose their senses over ascetic exercises — alluding to [[Teungkoe Koetakarang|Koetakarang]]'s rival [[Habib Samalanga]], who is not however mentioned by name. Others devote themselves to the repair of mosques far away from the scene of the war, and hold great *kanduris* or religious feasts, squandering the money subscribed for the war — here he refers to [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]]. The collection of the sabil monies should be made by concerted arrangement, and a central treasury established (of course with Teungku Kutakarang as its administrator) in the neighbourhood of the theatre of war.

## Association with Teukoe Oema

Kreemer notes that [[Teungkoe Koetakarang|Teungkoe Koetakarang]] was associated with **Teukoe Oema**, whose sudden defection (*afval*) in [[1896]] caused major upheaval in Dutch political and military policy. The *Tadkirat ar-rakidïn* pamphlets were part of the broader religious-political literature that sustained the Atjèhnese resistance during the Atjèh-oorlog.

## Place in the political literature of the war

The pamphlet thus reveals the dissensions which prevailed even among the ulamas — the party of action. It belongs to the heroic age of the holy war against the Dutch, before the submission of Teuku Uma in [[1893]] revealed the hollowness of the secular resistance. After that submission, Teungku Kutakarang withdrew to a great extent from public life and died in November [[1895]], without ever coming into contact with the Government.

## Relation to the Tadj-oel-moelk

The *Tadkirat ar-rakidïn* should be distinguished from [[Teungkoe Koetakarang|Koetakarang]]'s other major work, the *[[Tadj-oel-moelk|Tadj-oel-moelk]]* — a 141-page compilation of chronology, astrology, medical recipes, and magical formulas compiled at the request of Sultan [[Sultan Mantsoer Sjah|Mantsoer Sjah]] ([[1838]]–[[1870]]). The *Tadkirat ar-rakidïn* was explicitly political-religious; the *Tadj-oel-moelk* was a more general compilation of esoteric knowledge.
