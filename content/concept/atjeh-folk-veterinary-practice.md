**Atjeh folk-veterinary practice** — the body of folk-veterinary knowledge and technique applied by the Atjehnese (Atjeher) to the medical care of buffaloes and cattle — is documented in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s [[Kreemer's De Karbouw (1956)|*De Karbouw* (1956)]] (pp. 165-168) as a substantial corpus of remedies, surgical techniques, and magical-therapeutic practices. The corpus is documented principally through [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] (vol. II, pp. 51, 55-56, 326) and [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer's *Atjeh*]] (vol. II, pp. 596, 619, 624, 645, 647, 548 vg.) — and exemplifies the wider Atjehnese pattern of integration of folk-veterinary technique with magical-therapeutic and Islamic-amulet practice.

## Documented practices

The principal documented Atjeh folk-veterinary practices recorded in *De Karbouw* are:

### Wound treatment: salt on the nasal septum wound

When the Atjeher pierces the nasal septum of a buffalo to draw the nose-rope through, he rubs kitchen salt on the wound so that it heals quickly (*De Karbouw*, p. 166; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 647). Salt is also used as a general wound-remedy.

### Diarrhoea remedy: burnt hide and puffed rice

For diarrhoea, the Atjeher prescribes internally an infusion of burnt buffalo-hide and puffed rice, both ingredients first being finely pounded (*De Karbouw*, p. 166; [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] II, p. 51).

### Gall-bladder remedy: swallowing whole

From Atjeh comes the report that the gall-bladder (*[[phét|phét]]*, Mal. *pahit*) of buffalo, cow, or goat is swallowed whole as a remedy against wasting disease (*uitterende ziekte*) (*De Karbouw*, p. 167; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 645).

### Joint-marrow remedy

A remarkable practice of Atjehse origin: for unpleasant sensations in the joints, one asks a person slaughtering a buffalo or cow for one or more of the legs in unbroken state, takes out the joint-marrow (*[[oetaq toeleuëng|oetaq toeleuëng]]*) and rubs it on the painful parts or drinks an extract of it (*De Karbouw*, p. 167; [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] II, pp. 55-56).

### Surgical practice: cauterising with the glowing iron

The Atjehers cauterise wounds with maggots in buffaloes and cattle with the glowing iron (*gloeijzer*) (*De Karbouw*, p. 168; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 619; [[Kreemer's Volksheelkunde|Kreemer, *Volksheelkunde*]], pp. 103 vg.).

### Holy-grave therapy for sick cattle

The Mohammedaanse Atjeher brings his sick cattle to a holy grave (*heilig graf*, *kramat*) to wash the animals' heads with water there; if the distance is too great, he takes some earth from the grave and mixes it in the drinking water of the sick beasts (*De Karbouw*, p. 168; [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] II, p. 326). This is the principal documented Atjehnese instance of the integration of folk-veterinary practice with Islamic saint-veneration (*kramat*-cult).

### Magic "cooling" of epidemics

A magical "verkoeling" (cooling) is applied to cattle and stables to neutralise "hot" (baleful) influences when an epidemic breaks out (*De Karbouw*, p. 168; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] I, p. 509; II, pp. 548 vg.) — an instance of the wider Atjehnese "verkoeling" concept of magical neutralisation of "warm" disastrous influences.

### Bespreken/bespuwen with sirih-spittle

The Atjeh practice of bespuwen (spitting) with sirih-spittle, possibly mixed with chewed magical leaves/herbs, as a calming/pain-killing measure (*De Karbouw*, p. 165; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 553) — a general Indonesian practice, but with explicit Atjeh attestation.

### Therapeutic power of buffalo-spittle / wallow-water

The water from a pool in which a buffalo has wallowed has therapeutic power, both internally and externally administered (*De Karbouw*, p. 160; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 596).

### The *sakét oelèe neuraja* maturation-crisis concept

The Atjehnese concept of the [[sakét oelèe neuraja|*sakét oelèe neuraja*]] — the critical maturation-crisis through which every buffalo must pass before adulthood, recognised by "the falling-out of the nest-hairs" — is part of the wider folk-veterinary conceptualisation of the buffalo's life-cycle (*De Karbouw*, p. 172; [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] II, p. 52; [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer, *Atjeh*]] II, p. 624).

## Significance

Atjeh folk-veterinary practice is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history as the documented corpus of folk-veterinary knowledge and technique applied by the Atjehnese to the medical care of buffaloes and cattle. The corpus exemplifies the wider Atjehnese pattern of integration of folk-veterinary technique (salt on wounds, burnt-hide diarrhoea remedy, gall-bladder swallowing, joint-marrow rubbing, glowing-iron cauterisation) with magical-therapeutic practice (sirih-spittle bespuwen, spittle/wallow-water therapy, the *sakét oelèe neuraja* concept) and with Islamic-amulet and saint-veneration practice (the *kramat*-grave therapy for sick cattle, the "verkoeling" of epidemics). The corpus is documented principally through [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s [[Snouck Hurgronje's De Atjehers (1893-94)|*De Atjehers*]] and [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|*Atjeh*]], and is re-cited in [[Kreemer's De Karbouw (1956)|*De Karbouw*]] as one of the principal documents of the Atjehnese folk-veterinary complex.
