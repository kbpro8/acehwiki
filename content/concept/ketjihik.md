**Ketjihik** — also spelled *ketjig*, *ketjiq* or *ketjik* in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* — was the [[Achehnese]] religious-administrative office held by a subordinate mosque functionary, probably a deputy [[imam]] or keeper of the mosque's temporal affairs. The ketjihik ranked below the imam and the [[oelama]] in the religious hierarchy, but his proximity to the mosque and to the imam's household gave him intelligence value to the [[Dutch]] colonial government. Kruisheer names two ketjihiks: *Ketjihik Oemar*, who served as guide to General Pel during his tours of the [[IV Moekims|IV]] and [[VI Moekims]] in [[1876]] and who, two decades later, was an informant on [[Teuku Umar]]'s meetings with the heads of the clergy; and also *Ketjik Bintang*, who was at one point designated as Umar's successor as commander of the *legioen* and was reported killed in late May [[1896]].

## Etymology and Meaning

The word *ketjihik* is Acehnese, of uncertain etymology. It appears to derive from Arabic خطيب, "preacher", one who delivers the *khutbah* sermon at Friday prayer via Acehnese phonological adaptation, although the semantic scope in Acehnese usage is broader and may include a deputy-imam or mosque administrator. Kruisheer's OCR variants — *ketjihik*, *ketjig*, *ketjiq*, *ketjik* — reflect the difficulty of rendering the Acehnese palatal consonants in Dutch orthography. The office was subordinate: a ketjihik could serve as guide or intelligence asset to a foreign power, but he did not have the doctrinal authority of an oelama or the parish-wide legitimacy of an imam. The combining form *Ketjik Bintang* shows the title used as a personal name-component.

## Historical Context

The two named ketjihiks reveal the office's dual function in the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]: religious-administrative intermediary and intelligence asset. *Ketjihik Oemar*, having served General Pel in [[1876]] and received the silver medal for his services, was by [[1896]] a senior member of the immediate circle of [[Teuku Umar]] and [[Tjoet Nja Din]]. His report to the Dutch that Umar and his wife were holding "busy meetings with the heads of the clergy" was significant intelligence on the eve of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]]. *Ketjig Bintang van Lam Loempoer* — alternately styled *Ketjik Bintang* — had been designated by Umar as his successor as commander of the *legioen*. According to the Telegram to Regeering of [[28 May]] [[1896]], Ketjik Bintang was among those reported killed in the wake of the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896]] — rumours of "270 dead and 130 severely wounded among Oemar's own people" were noted as uncertain in reliability.

## Examples in the Source

Kruisheer's principal reference to the office is the entry for *Ketjihik Oemar*: "Served in [[1876]] as guide to General Pel during his tours in the IV and VI Moekims; wore the silver medal for merits. Belonged to the immediate circle of Toekoe Oemar and could know of his intentions. Reported that T. Oemar and his wife Tjoet Nja Din were holding busy meetings with the heads of the clergy." The Ketjig Bintang is referenced in the *Telegram to Regeering* of [[28 May]] [[1896]] as "Ketjik Bintang (formerly designated successor as commandant of the legion) had been killed" — a report whose reliability the source flags as uncertain. The steamship *General Pel* is noted as named after the same General whom Ketjihik Oemar had guided in [[1876]], and arrived in Atjeh on [[8 April]] [[1896]].

## Significance

The ketjihik illustrates the layered structure of Achehnese religious administration and the value of subordinate religious functionaries as intelligence assets. The Dutch colonial government's cultivation of the ketjihik Oemar over two decades — from guide in [[1876]] to informant in [[1896]] — shows the patient, multi-generational character of Dutch intelligence work in Aceh. The office's survival as an identifiable rung in the religious hierarchy also confirms the institutional depth of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Acehnese mosque community]]: the moekim had not only an imam but a ketjihik, and each could be separately approached, cultivated or eliminated by the colonial power.
