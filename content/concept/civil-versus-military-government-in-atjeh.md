**The civil-versus-military government question in Atjeh** was the Dutch administrative controversy — running from the [[Establishment of Civil Government in Aceh (6 April 1881)|establishment of the civil government of 1881]] to the eve of the First World War — whether the conquered or half-conquered sultanate of [[Atjeh]] should be administered by a civil *Gouverneur* or *Resident*, as in the ordinary residencies of the Indies, or by a military governor, as befitted a theatre of war.

## The establishment and failure of the civil government, 1881–1884

By decision of [[6 April]] [[1881]] the military government of Atjeh was replaced by a civil administration under a Gouverneur; the experiment, associated with the names of [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruys van der Hoeven]] and [[P. F. Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]] and with the military counter-weight of kolonel [[J. F. Haus|Haus]], collapsed within three years: the Resident found himself in a humiliating position, losing approximately 120 men between September [[1882]] and March [[1883]], and the lesson was read, by its critics, to be that a *civiel gezag* in a country still at war was a contradiction. By the [[Government decisions of 19 August 1884|decisions of 19 August 1884]] the civil government was abolished, and kolonel [[Demmenie|Demmeni]] was named military governor.

## Scherer's adage and Coblijn's rejoinder

The Resident [[G. A. Scherer]], in his Indisch-Genootschap lecture *[[Hoe moet Atjeh gepacificeerd worden? (Scherer)|Hoe moet Atjeh gepacificeerd worden?]]*, pleaded anew for the civil side with the adage that it does not stand to reason that every able officer of the Indies army is also an able administrator. [[W. A. Coblijn|Coblijn]] retorted in *[[Hoe Atjeh te onderwerpen (Coblijn, 1893)|Hoe Atjeh te onderwerpen]]* ([[1893]]) that the the Netherlands had witnessed the contrary in a long series of soldier-administrators: [[Andreas Victor Michiels|Michiels]], [[J. K. J. de Stuers|De Stuers]], [[Colonel C. A. de Brauw|De Brauw]], [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]], Andresen, [[W. E. Kroesen|Kroesen]], [[G. M. Verspyck|Verspyck]], Happé, [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Pel]], [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]], [[Demmenie]], [[Van Teijn]], and how many subaltern officers of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]] who ruled entire districts with the greatest success; while the British in India showed likewise — Clive, Low, Metcalfe, Henry and George Lawrence, Outram, Edwardes, Lumsden, Davidson, Durand and Nicholson. The question, Coblijn wrote, was not man or office but character; and in a country at war, armed authority alone could govern.

## Moens and the practice of 1913

Thirty years later the question *civiel- of militair bestuur op Atjeh* (civil or military government in Atjeh) was again publicly much discussed. [[W. Moens|Moens]], in the *[[Reisindrukken van Atjeh (Moens, 1914)|Reisindrukken van Atjeh]]* ([[1914]]), gave his opinion that the existing arrangement should not be disturbed: Gouverneur [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] combined in his person the civil and the military authority, and had behind him a civil administrator's experience of nine years — as Resident of the Zuid- en Ooster-afdeeling of [[Borneo]], as Gouverneur van Celebes, and then in Atjeh. Moens quoted the saying that was current: *wij weten, wat wij hebben, maar wij weten niet wat wij krijgen* (we know what we have, but we know not what we shall get), and warned that one should not transplant the young and still tender *plantje* (little plant) of the confidence and prosperity that had been gained. The [[Handover of Aceh to a civil Gouverneur (1918)|handover of Aceh to a civil governor]] was finally effected only in [[1918]], in the last year of Swart's government.

## Significance

The controversy is significant in the constitutional history of the Indies as the Atjeh case of the general nineteenth-century debate between *het gezag van het zwaard* and *het gezag van den ambtenaar* — a debate in which the peculiar persistence of the Atjehnese resistance forty years after the landing of 1873 made the province for generations the standing argument of both parties.
