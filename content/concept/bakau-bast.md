**Bakau Bast** (Dutch: *bakau-bast*) was an indigenous export product (Dutch: *inheems exportproduct*) of the colonial residency of [[Atjeh]], consisting of the bark (Dutch: *bast*) of the *Rhizophora* mangrove trees (Dutch: *bakau-bomen*) that fringed the estuaries and tidal swamps of the [[Aceh|Atjehnese]] coast. The bast was harvested, dried, and exported for its high tannic acid (Dutch: *looizuur*) content, used in European tanneries for leather production and in the textile industry for dyeing. Although never as lucrative as [[Pinangnut (Betel Nut)|pinangnut]] or [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]], bakau bast provided a steady supplemental income for coastal Acehnese communities engaged in mangrove exploitation during the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]].

## The mangrove resource

The mangrove forests of Atjeh — particularly along the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]], the Tamiang river delta, and the estuaries of the [[Aceh River|Atjeh River]] — were among the most extensive in the Indies. The dominant species, *Rhizophora mucronata* and *Rhizophora apiculata*, were known to colonial foresters as *bakau* and were valued for the high proportion of tannin in their bark. Mangrove bark typically yielded between 25 and 35 per cent tannic acid (Dutch: *looizuur*) by dry weight, a concentration that rivalled the best European and South American tanning materials.

Colonial forest law classified the mangrove belt as state forest (Dutch: *staatsbosch*), and the [[Buitengewesten|Buitengewesten]] forestry service regulated felling through concession licenses (Dutch: *kapvergunningen*) issued to Acehnese, Chinese and European concessionaires. Cutting was concentrated in the drier months when the swamp could be entered on foot or by small perahu.

## Extraction and processing

The bark was stripped from felled trees in long ribbons using a specialized bark-iron (Dutch: *bastmes*), then bundled and dried in the sun for several weeks. Once dried, the bast was either shipped raw to European tanneries or processed locally into a concentrated tannin extract. In the simplest domestic process, the bark was boiled in large copper vats (Dutch: *ketels*) and the resulting tannin liquor evaporated into a thick dark paste used locally for dyeing cotton and preserving fishing nets. For export, the dried bast was pressed into bales of approximately 80 kilograms and shipped through [[Belawan]] and [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]] to Amsterdam, Hamburg and Liverpool.

A secondary use of the bakau-bast trade was the production of cutch (Dutch: *catechu*), a dark resinous extract used in dyeing sails and fishing gear. The combination of bark, cutch and mangrove timber gave the Acehnese mangrove economy a layered structure that survived the decline of the individual commodity streams.

## Economic significance

Bakau bast never rivalled the great plantation crops of [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]], [[Copra|copra]] or [[Palm Oil (Aceh)|palm oil]], but it filled an important niche in the coastal economy. It provided cash income to fishing villages in the off-season, employed small perahu owners as transporters, and fed the European leather industry that supplied shoes and harness to the [[Indische Leger|Netherlands Indies Army]] and to civilian markets in [[Java]] and Europe. The trade also intersected with the colonial fisheries and salt regimes, since tanned fishing nets lasted longer in salt water than untreated ones.

After [[1920]] the expansion of synthetic tanning agents and the substitution of chrome tanning for vegetable tanning reduced European demand for mangrove bark. By the [[1930s]] bakau bast exports from Atjeh had fallen to a fraction of their pre-war peak, although the mangrove forests themselves remained important for firewood, charcoal and coastal protection. The commodity is a reminder of the diversity of the Atjeh export economy before its consolidation around rubber and oil palm.
