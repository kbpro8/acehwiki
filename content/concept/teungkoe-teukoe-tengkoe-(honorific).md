**Teungkoe** — also written *Teukoe* (pronounced *tekoe*) or *Tengkoe* — is an Atjehnese honorific which is, in Broersma's account, *'eigenlijk wel hetzelfde als toeankoe'* — *really much the same as toeankoe* — and a designation of high official dignity. The fuller form *[[Toeankoe (title)|toeankoe]]* is borne particularly by the sultan and his relatives; the shorter form *teungkoe/teukoe/tengkoe* is borne by chiefs and dignitaries, often in combination with a further title, as in *Teukoe Tjhi Perlak* (where *Tjhi* is the *keutihi* title), *Teungkoe habib Moehamat bin Sajet Oesoeih* (where *habib* indicates Arabic descent), or *Teukoe moeda Oesoch* (where *moeda* means *young*). The title is the principal non-sultanic honorific of Atjehnese chiefs.

In the religious dimension, Tengkoe was also the title given to Islamic scholars (*Schriftgeleerden*) in [[Aceh]] who had completed their religious education — typically at institutions such as the celebrated school at [[Tirou]]. The title in this usage signified expertise in Islamic religious sciences and authorized the holder to serve as Imams, religious teachers, and community religious leaders.

## Education and the Tirou School

The school at [[Tirou]] — a plantation in the binnenlanden (interior) of the Noordkust (north coast) of [[Atjeh]] — was one of the most prominent institutions for training future Tengkoe. Students who completed the curriculum received the title upon graduation and were authorized to lead prayers, deliver sermons, interpret Islamic law, and serve as religious advisors. The curriculum included Quranic recitation and interpretation, Hadith (prophetic traditions), Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*), theology (*aqeedah*), and Arabic language. The school produced graduates who spread across Acehnese society as Imams, *khatib*s (preachers), teachers, and legal advisors.

## Role in the Resistance

During the [[Aceh War]], many Tengkoe — particularly graduates of the Tirou school — played significant political roles. The combination of religious authority and community respect made them influential figures who could mobilize popular support. Religious scholars like [[Sjech Saman van Tirou|Tengkoe Sjech Saman di Tiro]] used their authority to advocate for the *heilige oorlog* (holy war) against the Dutch. The title Tengkoe lent legitimacy to their pronouncements on the lawfulness of armed resistance. Tengkoe throughout [[Groot-Atjeh]] helped organize the collection of *[[Wang Sabil]]* (holy war funds) and networks of support for fighters. Dutch colonial observers recognized the political significance of this religious authority: the challenge of governing [[Groot-Atjeh]] was complicated by the fact that religious leaders bearing the title Tengkoe possessed authority that secular officials could not easily replicate or undermine.
