**Bestuursdecentralisatie** (Administrative Decentralization) was the core principle of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]], involving the transfer of governmental authority from the central colonial administration to lower-level organs. For the Buitenbezittingen generally, this meant granting autonomie (autonomy) and zelfbestuur (self-governance) through the creation of autonome lichamen (autonomous bodies) at the local level. The concept of bestuursdecentralisatie was not new in 1913; it had been the subject of discussion and experimentation within the colonial administration for decades, driven by the recognition that the highly centralized governance structure of the Netherlands East Indies was increasingly inadequate to the task of administering a vast and diverse territory. The decentralisatiebeweging (decentralization movement) had gained momentum in the early twentieth century, informed by both practical administrative considerations and the liberal political philosophy that favored greater local participation in governance.

The specific form that bestuursdecentralisatie took in the Buitenbezittingen was shaped by the distinctive characteristics of these territories compared to Java and Madoera. The Buitenbezittingen were administered through a more centralized and directly controlled apparatus than Java, where the Inlandsch Bestuur provided a well-established framework for indirect rule. The reform program aimed to address this imbalance by creating local autonomous bodies in the Buitenbezittingen that could exercise a range of governmental functions previously reserved for the central colonial administration. These autonome lichamen would have the authority to manage local affairs, raise certain revenues, and make decisions on matters of local concern, thereby reducing the administrative burden on the central government and bringing governance closer to the population it served.

## Atjeh's Exceptional Status

However, for the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]], De Graaff noted that bestuursdecentralisatie took a different form: only [[Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur]] (Regional Self-Governance) was feasible, and granting greater independence to the Inlandsch bestuur was "voor dit gewest vooralsnog van minder beteekenis" (of less significance for this territory for the time being). This qualification effectively placed Atjeh in a separate category from the other Buitenbezittingen, acknowledging that the standard decentralisatie model could not be applied there without modification. The reasons for this exceptional treatment were multiple and interconnected: the coexistence of rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied (directly ruled territory) and Inlandsche Zelfbesturen within the Gouvernement, the incomplete development of the [[Inlandsch Bestuur]] in the directly ruled areas, the continued security concerns in some parts of the territory, and the complex political dynamics that distinguished Atjeh from the more pacified regions of Sumatra.

The limitation of bestuursdecentralisatie to the gewest (regional) level in Atjeh meant that the primary beneficiary of decentralization would be the proposed [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]], the new regional administrative unit that would incorporate Atjeh along with other territories in northern Sumatra. Rather than creating multiple layers of autonomous local governance within Atjeh itself, the reform concentrated decentralized authority at the regional tier, where it could be exercised by a Gewestelijke Raad (Regional Council) and a regional bestuur. This approach had the advantage of simplifying the governance structure in a territory where the existing arrangements were already complex, but it also meant that the benefits of decentralization—greater local participation, more responsive administration, reduced central control—would be realized at the regional level rather than at the local level where they would most directly affect the population.

## Significance for Colonial Governance

The concept of bestuursdecentralisatie as applied to Atjeh revealed the fundamental tension within the colonial administration between the desire for administrative uniformity and the recognition of local particularity. On the one hand, the entire reform program was predicated on the assumption that a more decentralized governance structure would be more efficient, more responsive, and more legitimate than the centralized system it replaced. On the other hand, the colonial administration was sufficiently pragmatic to recognize that uniform principles could not be applied uniformly across territories as diverse as the Buitenbezittingen. The result was a flexible approach to bestuursdecentralisatie that allowed for different forms and degrees of decentralization in different territories, with Atjeh receiving the most limited form—Gewestelijk Zelfbestuur—while other regions were granted more extensive local autonomy.

The bestuursdecentralisatie principle also connected to the broader question of the relationship between the European Bestuur and the Inlandsch Bestuur. Decentralization implied not only the transfer of authority from the central government to lower-level organs but also, potentially, the transfer of authority from European officials to indigenous administrators. This latter dimension was particularly sensitive in Atjeh, where the colonial administration was still in the process of consolidating its control and where the Inlandsch Bestuur in the rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied was acknowledged to be underdeveloped. De Graaff's characterization of greater independence for the Inlandsch bestuur as being of "less significance for the time being" reflected this cautious assessment, effectively deferring the more ambitious aspects of decentralization until such time as the indigenous administrative apparatus was deemed capable of assuming greater responsibilities.
