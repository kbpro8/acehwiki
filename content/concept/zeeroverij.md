**Zeeroverij** (piracy) — and in particular *Atjehse zeeroverij* (Atjehese piracy) — was the practice of maritime piracy carried on from the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] coast throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which together with the Atjehese slave trade was the principal international grievance against the [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] and the proximate cause of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Atjehese piracy claimed numerous foreign merchant ships among its victims, drew the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]], and was the explicit object of the obligation laid on the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] to make a definitive end to the slave trade and piracy on the Atjeh coast.

## The Atjehese Piracy in the Nineteenth Century

[[J. Frijling|Frijling]] records that in the absence of effective Dutch authority in the Buitengewesten — see [[Niet-inmengingspolitiek|niet-inmengingspolitiek]] — both *zeeroverij* (piracy) and *landroverij* (land-piracy — rampok- and ketjoepartijen, plundering and extortion) assumed "ongehoorde afmetingen." Foreign merchant ships were among the victims. The Atjehese coast, in particular, was the principal base of the piracy; "de Atjehers dreven niet alleen een drukke slavenhandel, maar zij waren ook beruchte zeerovers. Talrijke schepen waren hun ten offer gevallen."

## The Foreign Reprisals of 1831 and 1844

In [[1831]] the American government had the Atjehse coastal population chastised by one of its warships — see [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] — "bij wijze van wraakneming — ontkennende der Nederlandse souvereiniteit." In [[1844]] the British government followed the American precedent — see [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]]. Both actions illustrate the international pressure on the Dutch to suppress Atjehese piracy and the foreign willingness to act unilaterally in the absence of effective Dutch authority.

## The Sumatra-contract of 1871 and the Dutch Military Measures of 1872

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] gave the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] a free hand over the whole of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] in exchange for an undertaking to make a definitive end to the Atjehese slave trade and piracy. After repeated urging by the British government in London, the Dutch government decided in [[1872]] to take military measures against the Atjehese piracy and slave trade — see [[Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy (1872)|Dutch military measures of 1872]]. The decision led directly to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]] of [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]].

## The Failure of "Limited" Measures and the Continuation of Piracy

The Dutch assumption that "limited" military measures would bring the Atjehers to reason was rapidly falsified. After the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the Kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[24 January]] [[1874]] the Dutch government, believing the Atjehers sufficiently impressed, recalled the troops to [[Batavia|Batavia]]; the result was that a year later troops had to be sent again. "Slavenhandel en zeeroverij werden op de oude voet voortgezet," Frijling records; "De offers aan mensenlevens waren vergeefs gebracht." The protracted "schier eindeloos gesukkel" of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] followed, and was only brought to an end by the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]] of [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] around the turn of the century.
