**Zeeroverij** (piracy) — and in particular *Atjehse zeeroverij* (Atjehese piracy) — was the practice of maritime piracy carried on from the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] coast throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which together with the Atjehese slave trade was the principal international grievance against the [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] and the proximate cause of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Atjehese piracy claimed numerous foreign merchant ships among its victims, drew the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]], and was the explicit object of the obligation laid on the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] to make a definitive end to the slave trade and piracy on the Atjeh coast.

## The Atjehese Piracy in the Nineteenth Century

[[J. Frijling|Frijling]] records that in the absence of effective Dutch authority in the Buitengewesten — see [[Niet-inmengingspolitiek|niet-inmengingspolitiek]] — both *zeeroverij* (piracy) and *landroverij* (land-piracy — rampok- and ketjoepartijen, plundering and extortion) assumed "ongehoorde afmetingen." Foreign merchant ships were among the victims. The Atjehese coast, in particular, was the principal base of the piracy; "de Atjehers dreven niet alleen een drukke slavenhandel, maar zij waren ook beruchte zeerovers. Talrijke schepen waren hun ten offer gevallen."

## The Foreign Reprisals of 1831 and 1844

In [[1831]] the American government had the Atjehse coastal population chastised by one of its warships — see [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] — "bij wijze van wraakneming — ontkennende der Nederlandse souvereiniteit." In [[1844]] the British government followed the American precedent — see [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]]. Both actions illustrate the international pressure on the Dutch to suppress Atjehese piracy and the foreign willingness to act unilaterally in the absence of effective Dutch authority.

## The Sumatra-contract of 1871 and the Dutch Military Measures of 1872

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] gave the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] a free hand over the whole of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] in exchange for an undertaking to make a definitive end to the Atjehese slave trade and piracy. After repeated urging by the British government in London, the Dutch government decided in [[1872]] to take military measures against the Atjehese piracy and slave trade — see [[Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy (1872)|Dutch military measures of 1872]]. The decision led directly to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]] of [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]].

## The Failure of "Limited" Measures and the Continuation of Piracy

The Dutch assumption that "limited" military measures would bring the Atjehers to reason was rapidly falsified. After the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the Kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[24 January]] [[1874]] the Dutch government, believing the Atjehers sufficiently impressed, recalled the troops to [[Batavia|Batavia]]; the result was that a year later troops had to be sent again. "Slavenhandel en zeeroverij werden op de oude voet voortgezet," Frijling records; "De offers aan mensenlevens waren vergeefs gebracht." The protracted "schier eindeloos gesukkel" of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] followed, and was only brought to an end by the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification]] of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] around the turn of the century.


## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**The Zeeroof** — Atjehnese piracy — was, in the source's narrative, the principal Dutch grievance against [[Acheh|Atjeh]] throughout the nineteenth century. The "zucht der Atjehers, schepen af te lopen en te plunderen en rooftochten te ondernemen op het gebied van aangrenzende volken" (inclination of the Atjehnese to run ships down and plunder them and to undertake raiding expeditions into the territory of neighbouring peoples) had, the source records, "ons de gehele vorige eeuw door moeilijkheden bezorgd" (caused us difficulties throughout the entire previous century).

### The 1824 undertaking

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] contained the impossible double commitment: the [[Netherlands]] would respect Atjehnese independence while simultaneously "de zeeroof zouden beteugelen en zorgen voor de veiligheid van de handel" (suppress piracy and ensure the safety of commerce). The two commitments proved "twee eisen, die moeilijk met elkaar in overeenstemming te brengen waren" (two demands difficult to reconcile with each other).

### The 1857 contract

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 undertaking]] was given operational form in [[1857]] when, after the dispatch of a warship, a contract was concluded with the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Atjeh]] stipulating "wederzijdse toelating van handel en tegengang van zee-, strand- en mensenroof" (mutual admission of trade and suppression of sea-, strand- and people-robbing). The contract was "niet nageleefd" (not honoured). Dutch, English, American and other ships continued to be plundered.

### The 1871 framing

The [[Sumatra-tractaat (1871)|Sumatra-tractaat of 1871]] was, the source insists, narrowly anti-piracy in its framing: "Aan verovering van Atjeh werd nog niet gedacht, alleen aan onderdrukking van zeeroof" (Conquest of Atjeh was not yet contemplated, only the suppression of piracy). The slide from suppression to conquest was, however, already implicit in the [[1871]] treaty.

### The Dutch, English, American plundering

The source records that "Nederlandse, Engelse, Amerikaanse en andere schepen werden geplunderd" (Dutch, English, American and other ships were plundered). The international character of the piracy's victims is, in the source's narrative, the immediate diplomatic pressure that produced the [[1871]] *Sumatra-tractaat* and the [[1873]] declaration of war.

### Significance

The [[Zeeroof (Atjeh)|zeeroof]] is, in the source's narrative, the principal Dutch grievance against [[Acheh|Atjeh]] throughout the nineteenth century. The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 undertaking]] to suppress it, the [[1857]] contract, the [[Sumatra-tractaat (1871)|1871 Sumatra-tractaat]], and the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|1873 declaration of war]] are all, in the source, sequential responses to the persistence of Atjehnese piracy.
