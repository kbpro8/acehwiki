**Niet-inmengingspolitiek** (non-intervention policy) — also called *onthoudingspolitiek* (policy of abstention) — was the policy followed by the Dutch government throughout the nineteenth century with respect to the native states of the Buitengewesten (the outer provinces of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Netherlands Indies]] outside Java), by which the government refrained from active intervention in the internal affairs of the native polities and confined itself to the maintenance of the existing state of affairs and the recognition of the "nominal" supreme authority of the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]]. The policy, identified by [[J. Frijling|Ir. J. Frijling]] as the principal cause of the protracted failure to suppress slavery and piracy in the Buitengewesten, is the central subject of Frijling's [[1946]] sketch *Een stukje geschiedenis der Indische Buitengewesten* and the principal political background to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].

## The Origin and Rationale

The policy of *niet-inmenging* arose from the practical impossibility, in the impoverished [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] of the post-Napoleonic period, of administering the vast outer territories of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Netherlands Indies]] with the resources available. The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|Verdrag van Londen]] of [[1814]] had restored to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] sovereignty over a territory extending from the 95th to the 142nd degree of longitude east — more than an eighth of the Earth's circumference measured along the equator. The [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie]], whose rights the Dutch state had inherited, had been able to enforce its authority over no more than ten per cent of the total area of the archipelago; over the whole of Java, but in the Buitengewesten only at a few widely separated posts. The army of officials needed for an actual administration was simply not to be had.

## The Consequences

In the absence of effective authority, the native princes and headmen fell into increasing mutual discord, "een strijd van allen tegen allen, leidende tot grenzenloze onderdrukking van bevolkingen." [[Slavernij in Nederlandsch-Indië|Slavernij]] flourished, and both [[Zeeroverij|zeeroverij]] (piracy) and *landroverij* (land-piracy — rampok- and ketjoepartijen, plundering and extortion) assumed unheard-of proportions. Foreign merchant ships were among the victims; the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]] were direct foreign reprisals against Atjehese piracy. Frijling records the irony that the policy, "voor de talloze vorsten en hoofden, kortom voor hen, die in die dagen tot de sterken behoorden," produced the very "Merdeka" — the unbounded freedom — that the later Indonesian nationalist movement would claim.

## The Intermezzo of the *Niet-inmengingspolitiek* in Atjeh

The *niet-inmengingspolitiek* was the explicit framing of the Dutch decision to recall the troops from Atjeh after the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the Kraton]] of [[24 January]] [[24 January]] [[1874]]. [[J. Frijling|Frijling]] records that "de Regering, menende, dat de militaire gebeurtenissen een diepe indruk op de Atjehers hadden gemaakt, wilde vasthouden aan haar onthoudingspolitiek en riep onze troepen naar Batavia terug." The result was that a year later troops had to be sent again, and "slavenhandel en zeeroperij werden op de oude voet voortgezet." The political failure of the *niet-inmengingspolitiek* in Atjeh inaugurated the protracted "schier eindeloos gesukkel" of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].

## The Resolution

The [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification of Atjeh]] by [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] around the turn of the century, and the subsequent reforms by which Van Heutsz preserved native self-government under a thin layer of properly prepared Dutch administrators, marked the definitive abandonment of the *niet-inmengingspolitiek* and its replacement by an active, though economical, colonial administration. Frijling defends Van Heutsz against the charge of being a *geweldenaar en onderdrukker* — a violent man and oppressor — and praises his policy of preserving the native self-governments (*inlandse zelfbesturen*) under the supervision of a single, properly prepared Dutch official with multiple functions. This was the "ei van Columbus" — alternatively, the "ei van van Heutsz" — that resolved the problem that the *niet-inmengingspolitiek* had failed to solve.
