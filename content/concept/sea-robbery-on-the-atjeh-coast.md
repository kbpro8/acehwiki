**Sea robbery on the Atjeh coast** — the *zeeroof*, *strandroof* and *menschenroof* (sea-, strand- and man-robbery) denounced in the documents of the nineteenth century — was the chronic insecurity which [[J. Langhout|Joh. Langhout]] presents as the hallmark of Atjeh's last centuries of "[[Self-government and profit-province in Atjeh|zelfbestuur]]", provoking the American and British naval chastisements of [[1831]] and [[1844]], the Dutch measures of [[1840]], the anti-robbery clauses of the [[Treaty of 1857 between Netherlands and Aceh|treaty of 1857]], and finally the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty obligations]] on which the Dutch intervention of [[1873]] was grounded.

## Growth

From the freeing of [[1524]], Langhout relates, Atjeh extended at the cost of the surrounding countries; "naast een naar alle windstreken zich voortplantende uitzetting der grenzen van Atjeh, viel in die landen een zich snel voltrekkende demoralisatie van het Atjehsche volk te constateeren" — and the number of sea-robbers and "waterschuimers" who made the coasts of [[Sumatra]] unsafe far around grew alarming, especially at the end of the sixteenth century. After the Company's withdrawal into the [[Minangkabau]] coast ([[VOC-Minangkabau treaty and the founding of the Padang depot (1664)|1664]]), Atjeh for a century and a half was tormented by internal feuds; sea-robbery and plundering again ran high. "Thanks to that self-government", Langhout writes sarcastically, "the insecurity in the surroundings of Atjeh's coasts increased more and more: sea-robbery, strand-robbery, plundering of merchant ships, whereby but too often the crews fell victim, increased hand over hand."

## The incidents of 1830–1852

- **1830–1831**: an American merchant ship was cut off by the Atjehnese coastal population; in [[1831]] an American man-of-war appeared before [[Koeala Batië]] to show "hoe in het vervolg zeeroof zou worden gestraft" (see [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American chastisement of Kuala Batee (1831)]]).
- **June 1836**: the Dutch schooner *Dolphiijn*, with a money shipment of 30,000 guilders, was cut off between [[Natal]] and [[Padang]] by her own native crew, brought to Atjeh and burned; the Sultan kept the money and the guns (see [[Schooner Dolphijn incident (1836)|Dolphiijn affair (1836)]]).
- **1840**: the Atjehnese were driven from [[Baros]] and [[Singkel]] by the Netherlands Indian Government (see [[Singkel post reaching Atjeh border (1840)|Expulsion of the Atjehnese from Baros and Singkel (1840)]]).
- **1844**: English warships chastised Koeala Batië and Koeala Meureudoe anew for occurred plundering of merchant ships; "it availed little" (see [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British chastisement of Kuala Batee and Kuala Meureudoe (1844)]]).
- **1851**: a Neapolitan schooner was robbed on the Atjehnese coasts; **1852**: an English merchant ship.

## The treaty answers

The [[Treaty of 1857 between Netherlands and Aceh|treaty of 1857]] explicitly contained "bepalingen om den zee-, strand- en menschenroof tegen te gaan" ("provisions to combat the sea-, strand- and man-robbery"), with the lapse of all claims on account of former facts. The earlier [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 17 March 1824]] had obliged the Netherlands to regulate their relations with Atjeh so that "this native state, without losing anything of its independence, would not disturb the safety of trade and shipping" — an obligation which Dr. [[E. B. Kielstra]] judged "absolutely in conflict" with the stipulated independence, since it permitted at most a "chastisement" and no prevention. The road from blockade to blockade thence runs to the [[Blockade of the Atjeh coast (1873-1874)|blockade of 1873]], the weapons-and-victuals closures of the [[Dutch Policy of 'Concentration'|Concentration]], and the local actions against robber bands at [[Modjopahit]] (1882), [[Meulaboh]] (1883), [[Teunom]] ([[Nisero affair (1883–1885)|1883–84]]) and the boundary-raids of 1893 (see [[Pardon of Teukoe Oemar (30 September 1893)]]).
