**Oom Ambon** — "Uncle Ambon" — is the affectionate honorific by which the Ambonese professional soldier of the [[K.N.I.L.]] was known to the Dutch colonial community, and the title figure of *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* by [[G. W. T. Dames]] ([[1954]]). The *Oom* — literally "uncle" — was an old *soldaat 1e klasse* (soldier 1st class) whose title derived partly from age but much more from *gezag dat berustte op kennis, ervaring en persoonlijkheid* — authority resting on knowledge, experience and personality. Not every *soldaat 1e klasse* approaching pension was acknowledged as *Oom*; genuine *overwicht* (authority) was required. The book's title and prologue establish the figure as the moral centre of the colonial army's infantry and [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Marechaussee]].

## The title and the authority

"*Oom* was een oud soldaat 1e klasse. Dat hij *Oom* was, had natuurlijk met de leeftijd te maken, maar toch nog véél meer met het gezag, dat hij had." The *Oom* was expected to be an example for young *fuseliers*, to be able to replace the *korporaal*, and to take responsibility. The book's example is the *tamboer* of the 1e Compagnie of the 1e Bataljon at [[Magelang]] around [[1930]] — "die zelfs door de Generaal met *Oom* werd aangesproken" (who was even addressed as *Oom* by the General himself). The point is that the title was not a formality but a recognition of moral authority, extended even to a generaal addressing a drummer.

The title was paired with that of *[[Tante]]* — the *Oom*'s wife, whose influence ran through the whole brigade, sectie and compagnie. "Het gezag, dat een Oom behoort te hebben, zou hem nooit deelachtig worden, daar het met een Tante behoorde te worden gedeeld" — the authority an Oom ought to have would never be his, because it ought to be shared with a Tante.

## The opening dedication

The book's opening dedication poem — "Aan Prins Maurits heeft Oom trouw beloofd, / die trouw is door eeuwen bewaard; / door hem wordt in Onze Vader geloofd / en dat heeft ons zijn trouwbreuk bespaard" — establishes the *Oom* as a figure of centuries-old loyalty to the House of Orange. The poem is the book's framing of the *Oom*'s identity: a soldier whose loyalty to the Prins has been preserved through the centuries, and whose *trouw* (loyalty) has saved his descendants from *trouwbreuk* (broken faith).

## The figure in the book

The *Oom* of the book is not a single soldier but a type, instantiated in named figures: [[Nussy]] (the dardanel and spoorzoeker of the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]]), [[Thijs de Fretes]] (the *woudloper* and jager of [[Bakongan]]), [[Tanamal]] (the natural shot), [[Salawono]] (the M.W.O. Ridder), [[Tamaela]] (the korporaal of the [[Souvereiniteitsoverdracht (27 December 1949)|transfer of sovereignty]]), and the unnamed *Oom* who is the subject of the book's closing chapters. The book's argument is that the *Oom*'s authority — resting on knowledge, experience and personality, not on rank — was what held the colonial army together.

## The "soldaat sadja" and the end

The book's closing arc is the disbandment of the [[K.N.I.L.]] after [[1949]] and the dissolution of the *[[tangsi]]* culture that had made the *Oom*. [[Nussy]] "was altijd soldaat gebleven, *soldaat sadja*" — always a soldier, nothing but a soldier — even after his transfer to the *mantri-politie* on [[Ambon]]. The phrase *soldaat sadja* is the book's closing identification of the *Oom*: a man who was nothing but a soldier, and who, when the army that had made him was dissolved, was left with nothing but that identification.

## Significance

*Oom Ambon* is the title figure and moral centre of *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]*. The book's argument is that the colonial army — for all its cutbacks, its poor treatment, and its eventual abandonment — was held together less by regulation than by the patient daily work of men like this, whose authority ran ahead of and outside the rulebook. The dissolution of the [[K.N.I.L.]] in [[1949]] is therefore not just the end of an institution but the end of a moral economy: the *tangsi* culture that had made the *Oom* was dissolved, and the Ambonese soldiers who had served the Dutch were left without the institution that had made them.
