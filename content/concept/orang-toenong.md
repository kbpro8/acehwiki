**Orang toenong** was the name by which the submitted (*onderworpen*, "friendly") populations of the [[IV Moekims|IV]], [[VI Moekims|VI]] and [[IX Moekims]] distinguished, in the telling of the Dutch official records, their hostile countrymen of the [[XXII Moekims]] — literally, in Acehnese usage recorded by the Dutch sources, *the people from over there*. All irregularities and enmities on the pacified territory were, so Meijer observed, invariably ascribed by heads and people alike to the *orang toenong*.

## Usage in the records

In [[Kolonel Meijer|H. F. Meijer]]'s *[[Atjeh van 26 December 1875 tot 4 September 1876]]* ([[1883]]) the term appears at every point at which the submitted population brushes against the war:

- The returned people of the [[IX Moekims]] fled to the mountains in April [[1876]] when threatened by an incursion of the *orang toenong*, and would not return until a Dutch fortification closed the way from the XXII to the XXV Moekims — the desire that led directly to the founding of [[Biloel Zuid]].
- The heads of the IV Moekims, pressed by this enemy, showed a double face: they asked Dutch protection from raiding and revenge by their hostile countrymen, and often kept up — at least by neutrality — the connection with them by ancient exchanges of betel-leaf and rice.
  - The populations themselves explained every irregularity and hostile act on their territory as the work of the *orang toenong*; as often, Meijer noted, the friendly people themselves took part.

## Significance

The expression records both the fault line inside Atjehnese society during the war and the colonial use made of it: the submitted populations' own word for their enemies made visible how thin the floor was on which the Dutch consolidation of [[1876]] rested.
