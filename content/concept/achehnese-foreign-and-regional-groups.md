**Kling**, **Pedireezen** and **Toenongers** were the principal ethnic and regional designations applied by [[Achehnese]] and [[Dutch]] observers to the foreign and regional components of the Achehnese resistance in the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] as described in Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96*. The *Kling* — in Achehnese usage, a foreign Muslim, usually of Indian (often Tamil or Hadhrami Arab) origin — denoted the transnational Muslim presence in Aceh, exemplified by the "kling" Habib Lhong. The *Pedireezen* — people from [[Pedir]], a region on the west coast of Aceh — were a regional subgroup often employed as mercenaries, stationed at [[Bt. Kesoemba]] and explicitly tolerated or rejected by other Achehnese leaders. The *Toenongers* — people of the [[XXII Moekims]] — were a regional subgroup whose territory was "by the Atjehers steadily considered as the heart of Groot-Atjeh" and was the source of hostile bands. Together these terms delineate the multi-ethnic and regional composition of the Achehnese resistance.

## Etymology and Meaning

The three terms have distinct origins. also *kling-kling*, *keling* is a Malay/Acehnese term of debated etymology — most probably from *Kalinga*, the name of an ancient Indian kingdom on the Coromandel coast, applied in the archipelago to any Indian Muslim (and sometimes more broadly to any foreign Muslim). In Achehnese usage the term had a pejorative tinge but was also applied neutrally to Indian Muslim traders and holy men, including the [[habib]]s of Hadhrami Arab origin (whom Achehnese usage sometimes conflated with Indian Muslims). *Pedireezen* is a Dutch compound: the west-coast Acehnese region of Pedir + the Dutch plural suffix for ethnic/regional demonyms, from *eeze* = "ish", as in *Amsterdameezen*. The *Pedireezen* are "people from Pedir". *Toenongers* is similarly: an alternative name for the [[XXII Moekims]] region + the Dutch plural suffix. The *Toenongers* are "people of Toenong" — i.e., of the XXII Moekims.

## Historical Context

In the first semester of [[1896]] the three terms delineated the principal ethnic and regional components of the Achehnese resistance. The *Kling* component — exemplified by *den kling Habib Lhong* and the other Habibs ([[Habib Lengkong]], [[Habib Samalanga]], [[Habib Koeala van Pedir]]) — brought transnational Islamic legitimacy and Hadhrami/Arab networks to the resistance, reinforcing the framing of the war as *sabil Allah*. The *Pedireezen* — mercenaries from the west-coast region of Pedir, stationed at [[Bt. Kesoemba]] — were a hired military component whose presence was contested among Achehnese leaders: *Ketjig Bintang van Lam Loempoer* "threatened to withdraw if Pedireezen were tolerated". The *Toenongers* — the people of the [[XXII Moekims]], the "heart of Groot-Atjeh" — were the source of the most determined hostile *benden*, including those of [[Panglima Polim]] and the principal oelamas [[Teungkoe Koeta Karang]] and [[Teungkoe Tanah Abée]]. The three groups — foreign Muslim, west-coast mercenary, and heartland Achehnese — together composed the multi-ethnic resistance that the Dutch faced in the spring of [[1896]].

## Significance

The three terms — Kling, Pedireezen, Toenongers — together delineate the multi-ethnic and regional composition of the Achehnese resistance, and thus the strategic problem facing the Dutch. The resistance was not a unitary "Achehnese" force but a coalition of foreign Muslim volunteers (Klings/Habibs), regional mercenaries (Pedireezen), and heartland Achehnese (Toenongers), each with its own motivations, chains of command, and political constraints. The Dutch attempt to detach individual hoeloebalang from this coalition — and to play off the regional subgroups against each other — was a sustained strategic effort. The survival of the Kling label in colonial-era Achehnese Malay — and the persistence of the Pedir and Toenong regional identities into modern Acehnese politics — testify to the enduring importance of these ethnic and regional distinctions.

## Account from *Het recht op den grond in Atjeh* ([[D. W. N. de Boer]], [[1931]]) — non-indigenous cultivators and the right of avail

[[D. W. N. de Boer]] recorded that the Atjehnese *[[Beschikkingsrecht in Atjeh|beschikkingsrecht]]* (right of avail over waste land) applied with markedly greater strictness to non-indigenous cultivators than to Atjehnese. He named two groups: the *Chineezen* (Chinese) and the *Kingaleezen* — the South Indian Muslims of the *Kling* designation — writing with characteristic caution of "niet-inheemsche Inlanders, resp. niet-Inlanders," non-indigenous natives, or non-natives as the case might be.

Such a settler was obliged not merely to pay the *[[Haq radja taléë|haq radja taléë]]*, as an Atjehnese clearer was, but in addition to enter into the *[[Thiplah tano|thiplah-tano]]* contract — literally "half of the land," Malay *sebelah tanah*. Against receipt of a *[[Pangkaj|pangkaj]]* (starting capital) consisting of a buffalo, the necessary seed, and a *belandja* of twenty guilders as an advance for provisions, he bound himself to cede to the *radja* either half of the ground he cleared or, each year, half of the half-crop; half of the *belandja* had itself to be repaid in instalments. So far as rice fields were concerned the contract was granted only if the immigrant undertook to work a minimum area of five *naleh* of seed, about 8,000 square metres or 0.8 hectare.

Against these burdens stood a substantial advantage: half of the cleared *[[Sawah|sawah]]* became the "property" of the clearer, so that on departure he might even sell that half, being obliged only to grant the *radja* concerned a right of preference.

De Boer added in a footnote that alienation of land to "persons not belonging to the indigenous population" had since been taken in hand by the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement]], under article 15 sub I in conjunction with article 29 sub 3 of the *[[Staatsblad 1919 No. 822|Zelfbestuursregelen]]*, referring also to *[[Politiek Beleid en Bestuurszorg in de Buitenbezittingen|Politiek Beleid]]*, pp. 111 ff. and 233 ff.
