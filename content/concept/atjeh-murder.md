**The "Atjèh murder"** (Dutch *Atjèh-moord*) is, in Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan*, the "typically Atjèhse form of venting internal difficulties" — a form of religiously motivated assassination, peculiar to Atjèh, in which a perpetrator in personal distress sought death at the hands of the authorities by attacking an unbeliever, and thereby, in his conviction, "assured himself in advance by perpetrating an attack on an unbeliever and which, in his conviction, would open the gates of paradise for him." Piekaar treats the phenomenon as inseparable from the *[[kaphé|kaphé]]*-hatred that animated Atjèhnese religious life, and as a key diagnostic of the state of Atjèhnese society under Dutch rule.

## The mechanism

The "Atjèh murder" operated as a form of religiously sanctioned suicide. The perpetrator, faced with difficulties internal to himself or his household, sought an honourable death — and, with it, the reward of martyrdom — by attacking a representative of the infidel power. "For the perpetrator, in this way sought an escape from his difficulties in death, of which he assured himself in advance by perpetrating an attack on an unbeliever and which, in his conviction, would open the gates of paradise for him." The act was, in this sense, simultaneously a private solution to private distress and a public blow against the *[[kaphé|kaphé]]*. Piekaar's gloss on the phenomenon underlines the inextricable link: "Because of the inextricable link which existed between these murders and the hatred which the Atjèher harbored toward the unbelievers (Atj. *[[kaphé|kaphé]]*), this decrease could be regarded as a favorable sign, as it seemed to indicate a diminished intensity of this hatred."

## The colonial reading

By the late 1930s, the colonial administration interpreted the diminishing frequency of "Atjèh murder" as the principal index of pacification. In Piekaar's introduction to the pre-war situation: "Cases of so-called 'Atjèh murder,' the typically Atjèhse form of venting internal difficulties, were hardly worth mentioning anymore." This was taken to indicate "a diminished intensity of this hatred" — and, with it, that "the violent character of Atjèhse religious fanaticism seemed to have made way for a more constructive religious orientation, which through an expansion and improvement of religious education sought to purify and deepen the religious life." The reading would prove tragically over-optimistic.

## The linkage to prang sabi

The "Atjèh murder" was, in essence, an individual discharge of the same religious duty that animated the collective *[[prang sabi|prang sabi]]*: the war against the infidel. The two were the micro- and macro-cosmic expressions of the same *kaphé*-hatred. Where the *prang sabi* was a mass mobilisation, the "Atjèh murder" was a private act of *prang sabi* on a single victim. Both depended, for their religious warrant, on the doctrine that fighting the infidel was "a pious duty imposed by Islamic law" — and both were transmitted and kept alive by the recitation of the *[[hikajat prang sabi|hikajat prang sabi]]*.

## The illusion of pacification

The [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion]] of [[March]] [[1942]] exposed the illusion. "In March 1942 the inflamed passions not only vented themselves on the unbelievers and their hangers-on, but also the shops and homes of believing Mohammedans fell prey to the plundering, because they did not belong to the own Acehnese population group." The pacification of the late colonial period — measured by the decline of the "Atjèh murder" — had suppressed the surface manifestations of *kaphé*-hatred without extinguishing the underlying mentality, which, "particularly among the older generation, the mentality still slumbered from which once the fanatic resistance of the [[Atjèh War|Aceh War]] was born." The "Atjèh murder" was, in this sense, the diagnostic of an illusion: the colonial belief that a generation of peace had remade Atjèhnese society.
