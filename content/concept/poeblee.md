**Poebléë** was the Atjehnese term for the sale of land. [[D. W. N. de Boer]], following [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]], recorded that while the sale of land in [[Aceh|Atjeh]] could not be called exactly rare, the *poebléë* of rice fields hardly occurred at all, since complete alienation of a rice field was regarded as a disgrace to the person concerned.

## Public opinion

Quoting *[[De Atjehers (Snouck Hurgronje)|De Atjehers]]*, Vol. I, p. 312: "Hoewel de verkoop van gronden in Atjeh niet bepaald zeldzaam genoemd kan worden… geldt hij, die zijn geerfde rijstveld geheel of gedeeltelijk verkoopt in de publieke opinie als een verkwister" — he who sells his inherited rice field wholly or in part passes in public opinion for a spendthrift. The practice was called *[[Peupia oemòng|peupia oemòng]]*, the breaking up of one's rice field; by it a man ruined the *[[Pangoelee hareukat|pangoeléé hareukat]]*, the master of all breadwinning.

## Restrictions on sale

Two restrictions bound the seller. Sale might not take place without the owners of adjoining land having been notified of the intention, these neighbours enjoying a right of preference on an equal bid with third parties — the *[[Nabuurrecht|nabuurrecht]]*, for which De Boer cited *De Atjehers*, Vol. I, p. 106, and *[[Adatrecht (Van Vollenhoven)|Adatrecht]]*, p. 202. And the holder of *[[Milik|milik]]* was bound by the prohibition on alienation outside the *[[Landschap (Atjehnese)|landschap]]*.

A non-indigenous cultivator holding half of a cleared *[[Sawah|sawah]]* under the *[[Thiplah tano|thiplah-tano]]* contract might, on departure, sell his half, being obliged only to grant the *radja* concerned a right of preference.
