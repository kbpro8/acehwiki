**Colophonium** — also *colophony*, in Dutch *hars* (resin) or *dennenhars* (pine resin) — is the solid resinous residue obtained from the steam distillation of pine balsam, of which it constitutes approximately 75% of the yield at the [[Baleq]] enterprise. As a colonial commodity, colophonium was — together with the [[Atjeh-terpentijn|turpentine oil]] that constitutes the other 20% of the yield — the principal product of the [[Baleq]] enterprise of *'s Lands Caoutchouchedrijf* in the [[Gajo-landen|Gajo-Landen]]-[[Atjeh]] border region.

## The yield of the Baleq enterprise

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet of 1930]] records that the modern French distillation installation brought into service at the [[Baleq]] enterprise in [[October]] [[1928]] produced — by the steam distillation of the balsam of [[Pinus Merkusii]] with two horizontal Jonkers steam boilers — a yield of approximately 75% colophonium (resin) and 20% turpentine. The production figures for the [[Baleq]] enterprise, as preserved in Table I of the pamphlet, show the growth of the resin (and turpentine) production from 5 tons of resin in [[1925]] to 356 tons of resin and 104 tons of turpentine in [[1929]].

## The market for colophonium

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the colophonium was *in kisten verpakt* (packed in cases) and *vindt vlot afzet in het land zelf, grootendeels bij de batikkerijen op Java, voor de rest bij enkele zeepfabrieken op Sumatra's Oostkust* — *finds ready sale in the country itself, mostly with the batik workshops on Java, for the rest with some soap factories on Sumatra's East Coast*. The colophonium was thus sold principally in the Netherlands Indies themselves — in contrast to the turpentine, of which a large part was shipped to Amsterdam by the [[Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij]] for European sale. The principal domestic market for colophonium was the batik industry of [[Java]] — which used the resin in the batik wax mixture — and the soap factories of the [[Oostkust van Sumatra]] — which used the resin in the manufacture of soap.

## The contrast with turpentine

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the colophonium — unlike the turpentine — *vindt vlot afzet in het land zelf* (finds ready sale in the country itself), and that the *kwaliteit toch van de hars van Pinus Merkusii verschilde niet bijzonder van die van andere Pinus-soorten, zoodat daarover geen speciaal oordeel gevraagd behoefde te worden* (the quality of the resin of Pinus Merkusii, after all, did not differ particularly from that of other Pinus species, so that no special judgement needed to be asked about it). The colophonium was thus a less problematic commodity than the turpentine — its quality was unremarkable, its market was secure, and the Afdeeling Handelsmuseum did not need to evaluate it. The contrast with the turpentine — whose odour, chemical composition and European market reception were the subject of the entire [[1930]] pamphlet — illustrates the way in which the colonial commodity research programme of the [[Afdeeling Handelsmuseum]] concentrated on the problematic commodities rather than on the unproblematic ones.

## Significance

Colophonium's significance in the historiography of colonial Aceh is as the principal domestic-market product of the [[Baleq]] enterprise — the commodity that, unlike the [[Atjeh-terpentijn|turpentine]], found *vlot afzet* (ready sale) in the Netherlands Indies themselves, principally in the batik industry of [[Java]] and the soap factories of the [[Oostkust van Sumatra]]. The colophonium's unproblematic market reception — and the contrast with the turpentine's problematic European market reception — illustrates the way in which the colonial commodity research programme of the [[Afdeeling Handelsmuseum]] concentrated on the problematic commodities, and the way in which the same raw material — the balsam of [[Pinus Merkusii]] — could yield both an unproblematic and a problematic commodity.
