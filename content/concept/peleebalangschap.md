**The Peleebalangschap** (plural: peleebalangschappen) was the intermediate indigenous administrative unit in [[Groot-Atjeh]], described by Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] as eenigermate overeenkomend met de districten (somewhat corresponding to the districts) of Java. The peleebalangschappen sat between the higher-level [[sagi]]'s (comparable to regentschappen) and the lower-level [[moekimschap]]pen (comparable to onderdistricten) in the three-tier administrative hierarchy of Groot-Atjeh. Each peleebalangschap was headed by a peleebalang (district chief), who exercised administrative and judicial functions over a group of moekims. The peleebalang thus served as the crucial intermediary between the broad territorial authority of the sagi and the localized community governance of the moekim, occupying a position that was both administratively essential and politically sensitive within the indigenous governance structure.

The peleebalangschap as an administrative unit reflected the layered nature of Atjeh's indigenous political organization, in which authority was distributed across multiple levels rather than concentrated at a single point. The peleebalang was not merely a subordinate official executing directives from above; the position carried its own prestige and authority, rooted in local traditions of governance and community leadership. The relationship between the peleebalang and the panglima sagi above was one of nested authority rather than simple subordination, with the peleebalang exercising considerable autonomy within his own domain while acknowledging the overarching authority of the sagi head. Similarly, the peleebalang's relationship with the moekim heads below involved a combination of supervision and representation, with the peleebalang serving both as a channel for directives from higher authorities and as an advocate for the interests of the communities under his jurisdiction.

## Administrative Functions and Jurisdiction

Each peleebalangschap encompassed a group of [[moekimschap]]pen, and the peleebalang exercised both administrative and judicial functions over this territory. The administrative responsibilities of the peleebalang included maintaining order, overseeing tax collection, facilitating communication between the colonial government and the local population, and coordinating public works and community initiatives. The judicial functions were equally important, as the peleebalang served as a court of first instance for many disputes arising within his jurisdiction, applying a combination of adat (customary law) and Islamic legal principles under the general oversight of the colonial administration. This dual administrative-judicial role was characteristic of indigenous governance throughout the Netherlands East Indies and reflected the integrated nature of authority in Atjeh's traditional political system, where the separation of executive and judicial functions that characterized European governance was unknown.

The scope of a peleebalangschap's jurisdiction could vary considerably depending on local conditions, including population density, geographic features, and the historical particularities of each area. Some peleebalangschappen were relatively compact, encompassing a small number of closely connected moekims, while others were extensive, covering dispersed communities across difficult terrain. This variation reflected the organic development of Atjeh's administrative geography over centuries, in which political boundaries were shaped by a complex interplay of military power, economic relationships, kinship networks, and religious affiliations. The colonial administration generally maintained these existing boundaries rather than attempting to impose a uniform territorial organization, both as a matter of administrative convenience and as a strategy for minimizing disruption to established governance patterns.

## Significance in the Bestuursreorganisatie

The peleebalangschap was discussed at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meeting on [[19 January]] [[1914]] as part of Gouverneur Swart's broader explanation of Atjeh's indigenous administrative structure to Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]]. The explicit comparison with the Javanese district was significant because it placed the peleebalangschap within a framework that the colonial administrators could readily understand and evaluate. The Javanese district system was the best-known and most thoroughly documented indigenous administrative structure in the Netherlands East Indies, and by invoking this comparison, Swart was effectively arguing that Atjeh possessed a governance system of comparable sophistication and organizational coherence. This was no mere academic exercise; the comparison had direct implications for the reform proposals under discussion, particularly regarding the feasibility of developing Inlandsche bestuurskrachten (Native administrative forces) in Atjeh and the question of whether Atjeh could sustain more ambitious forms of local autonomy.

The peleebalangschap also figured in discussions about erfelijkheid (heredity) in head appointments. As with the sagi heads, the appointment of peleebalang was subject to a tension between hereditary claims and the colonial administration's emphasis on competence. Gouverneur Swart noted that heredity was increasingly being set aside in favor of bekwaamheden (competence), a trend that was arguably even more significant at the peleebalangschap level than at the sagi level, given the peleebalang's more direct engagement with local communities and the correspondingly greater importance of administrative skill and local knowledge. The gradual shift toward merit-based appointments at this intermediate level of the hierarchy suggested that the colonial administration was actively reshaping the Inlandsch Bestuur to serve its own administrative priorities, even while preserving the formal structure of indigenous governance.
