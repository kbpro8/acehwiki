**Pinangnut (Betel Nut)** (Dutch: *pinangnoot*, also *betelnoot*) was a major indigenous export crop (Dutch: *inheems exportgewas*) of the residency of [[Atjeh]] in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]], cultivated in specially designed gardens (Dutch: *pinangtuinen*) by smallholder growers whose betel nut (Dutch: *betelnoot*) formed an essential ingredient of the betel quid (Dutch: *sirihpruim*) chewed throughout the archipelago and the Indian Ocean world. The nuts were largely exported to Penang, [[Java|Batavia]] and the South Indian ports, and were also processed locally to extract tannic acid (Dutch: *looizuur*). The cultivation and export of pinangnut declined in the late nineteenth century as other cash crops — notably [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]] — gained prominence, but pinangnut remained a fundamental smallholder resource of the [[Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]] landschapskas throughout the colonial period.

## The pinang palm and its cultivation

The betel or areca palm (*Areca catechu*, Dutch: *pinangpalm*) was planted in dedicated orchards (Dutch: *pinangtuinen*) on the well-drained soils of the Atjeh coastal plain and the lower slopes of the interior. The slender, gracefully curved palms grew to 20 metres in height, bore their first commercial crop at six to eight years of age, and continued to yield for 40 to 60 years. Each palm produced annually between three and six bunches (Dutch: *trossen*) of 100 to 250 nuts, the oval, slightly fibrous, orange-yellow fruits that — when split and dried — became the commercial *betelnoot* of the Asia trade.

Cultivation was almost exclusively a smallholder activity: the European estates of the East Coast preferred rubber, [[Palm Oil (Aceh)|palm oil]] and [[Robusta Coffee (Aceh)|robusta coffee]], and the pinang palm was left to the Acehnese village economy. The palms were typically interplanted with fruit trees, coconuts and pepper vines in the mixed-species home gardens (Dutch: *erfgronden*) characteristic of Atjeh smallholder agriculture, and the harvested nuts were either chewed directly with sirih leaves or sold to Chinese and Acehnese dealers for export.

## The sirihpruim and the betel-quid tradition

The *betelnoot* was the active ingredient of the betel quid (Dutch: *sirihpruim*), the mildly narcotic chew that was — and remains — a central social and ritual practice of the Acehnese and the broader Austronesian world. The quid was assembled by smearing a sirih leaf (Dutch: *sirihblad*, from the *Piper betle* vine) with a smear of slaked lime (Dutch: *gebluste kalk*), adding a slice of *betelnoot*, optionally a clove or a cardamom seed, folding the leaf into a neat packet, and chewing it slowly over an hour or more. The combined action of the lime, the alkaloid arecoline of the nut, and the aromatic oil of the sirih leaf produced a red saliva, a warm stimulation, and a characteristic black-staining of the teeth and gums that was traditionally regarded as a mark of beauty.

The sirih-betel service (Dutch: *sirih-set* or *pinang-set*) — the lacquered or brass tray with its containers for sirih leaves, lime pot, areca-nut cracker (Dutch: *pinangtang*), cloves and tobacco — was the indispensable centrepiece of every Acehnese reception, and the offering of the quid was the universal gesture of hospitality. The betel quid was also chewed as the closing act of every formal Acehnese meal, and was an obligatory element of betrothal and marriage ceremonies.

## Tannic-acid extraction and secondary products

The harvested nuts were either chewed directly with sirih leaves (Dutch: *sirihbladeren*) or processed to extract tannic acid (Dutch: *looizuur*). The fresh nuts were boiled in large vats, the resulting dark liquor evaporated to a thick paste, and the paste dried into hard cakes that were exported to European tanneries and dyeworks. The tannin content of the areca nut — about 15 per cent by dry weight — was sufficient to make the trade worthwhile even when the chewing-nut market was weak, and the by-product served as a useful industrial raw material for leather and textile production. The leaves and stems of the palm were also used locally for thatch and for the manufacture of small household items.

## Economic significance and decline

Pinangnut export contributed significantly to the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|economy of Atjeh]] during the early colonial period, providing income for local growers and traders. The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's [[1913]] survey of the [[Buitengewesten|Buitenbezittingen]] landschapskassen recorded a *pinanghasil* of f 55,000 for the [[Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]] landschapskas, making it — alongside the f 125,000 *peperhasil* of the same residency — one of the two "very important" agricultural revenues singled out by the Bureau in its overview of the Atjeh finances.

The export volume peaked before the rise of [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]] and the spread of factory-rolled cigarettes reduced demand for the betel quid. The decline of pinangnut mirrored broader shifts in agricultural priorities, notably the expansion of pepper and rubber plantations. By the [[1930s]] the great export market for betel nut had contracted, though the crop remained central to the village economy and to the cultural life of the Acehnese interior, where the *sirihpruim* continued to mark every social and ritual occasion.
