**Fort de Rock** — also spelled *Fort de Kock*, and identified in the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history]] as a Japanese military prison in [[Medan|Medan]] — was the site of Lt [[H. van Zanten|lieutenant H. van Zanten]]'s post-capture interrogation, court-martial and condemned detention in [[March 1943|March 1943]], and the staff history's principal narrative instance of the Japanese *krijgsraad* (court-martial) procedure applied to Dutch officers captured in the *terrein* after the [[Sumatra capitulation (28 March 1942)|capitulation of 28 March 1942]].

## The Van Zanten court-martial (March 1943)

After Lt [[H. van Zanten|Van Zanten]]'s capture on [[10 March]] [[10 March 1943|10 March 1943]] at the edge of the forest north-east of [[Takengon|Takengon]], he and the five Maréchaussees remaining with him were "op beestachtige wijze mishandeld en verhoord," with the Japanese repeatedly offering the lieutenant *lijfsbehoud* if he would act as their spy in the prisoner-of-war camps. He refused. "Hij werd tenslotte overgebracht naar Fort de Rock, waar hij door een zogenaamde krijgsraad ter dood werd veroordeeld."

The staff history records his bearing: "Luitenant Van Zanten bleef tot het laatste toe, ondanks alle vernederingen en mishandelingen, waardig en fier, niettegenstaande hij zeer goed begreep geëxecuteerd te zullen worden." The date and place of execution are not given in the staff history; the entry closes the Van Zanten episode.

## Significance

Fort de Rock is, in the staff history's Atjeh chapter, the principal narrative instance of the Japanese *krijgsraad* procedure applied to Dutch officers captured in the *terrein* after the formal capitulation. The staff history's use of *zogenaamde krijgsraad* — "so-called court-martial" — signals the compilers' view that the procedure was a quasi-judicial pretence for a predetermined execution. The Van Zanten case is the staff history's clearest single instance of a Japanese capital sentence passed on a Dutch officer whose only *offence* was the continuation of resistance after a capitulation he had not personally recognised — an *offence* the staff history frames as a moral duty rather than a crime.
