**Atjeh silk ikat weaving** was the production of silk cloth with *ikat* (resist-dye) patterned warps in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — a craft recorded in the *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid* catalogue through two specific items (catalogue numbers 65 and 66) described as silk tissues with ikatted warps from Atjeh. The use of imported silk suggests the integration of the sultanate's textile craft into the wider Indian Ocean silk trade.

## The catalogue entries

The *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid* catalogue records two specific items of Atjeh silk ikat weaving:

- **Item 65**: "Geimporteerd zijden weefsel. Atjeh. N. Sumatra." — Imported silk tissue. Atjeh. North Sumatra. The item is described in English as "Tissue of imported Silk Ikatted on the Warp. Acheh."; in French as "Tissu de soie importée. Avec ikat sur la chaine. Atcheh."; and in German as "Gewebe von importierter Seide. Ikattiert auf der Kette. Atjeh."

- **Item 66**: "Zijde ikatweefsel. Atjeh." — Silk ikat-tissue. Atjeh. English: "Silk Tissue with Ikatted Warp Strokes, Acheh." French: "Tissu de soie avec des bandes à l'ikat sur la chaine. Atcheh." German: "Seiden Gewebe, mit ikattierten Kettenstreifen. Atjeh."

The two entries together indicate that Atjeh was a producer (or at least a catalogued source) of silk *ikat* cloth, and that the silk used was in part imported — a fact that places the sultanate's textile craft in direct relation to the Indian Ocean silk trade.

## *Ikat* technique

*Ikat* (from the Malay-Indonesian verb *mengikat*, "to tie" or "to bind") is a resist-dyeing technique in which the yarns — most commonly the warp (*ketting* in Dutch, *chaine* in French) — are tightly bound at intervals before dyeing, so that the bound sections resist the dye and produce a pattern when the yarns are woven. The catalogue distinguishes between *ikatted warp* (Item 65: ikat on the warp) and *ikatted warp strokes* or *ikatted Kettenstreifen* (Item 66: bands of ikat on the warp). The technique produces the characteristic soft-edged patterns of ikat cloth.

## Imported silk

The explicit mention of "imported silk" (*geimporteerd zijde*, *soie importée*, *importierter Seide*) for Item 65 is significant. It indicates that the silk used in Atjeh ikat weaving was not (or not only) locally produced, but was brought in from outside the sultanate — most likely from China or from India via the same Indian Ocean trade routes that brought the *soeasa* alloy and the gemstones that figured in the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy's]] jewel negotiations. Atjeh's craft tradition thus drew on imported raw materials and transformed them through local technique into a recognisable product.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the silk ikat weaving tradition attests to the sultanate's place in the wider Indian Ocean textile economy — a producer of ikat-patterned cloth from imported silk, alongside its better-known pepper trade. The catalogue entries also document the existence of an Atjeh silk-ikat craft tradition at the moment of European colonial cataloguing, even if the technique itself must be considerably older.
