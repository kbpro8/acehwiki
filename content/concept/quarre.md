**The quarre** — also *quarre method* — is a French turpentine-tapping system borrowed from the turpentine industry of the [[Landes]] in south-western [[France]], and applied — as recorded in the [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet of 1930]] — to the tapping of [[Pinus Merkusii]] at the [[Baleq]] enterprise in the [[Gajo-landen|Gajo-Landen]]-[[Atjeh]] border region from [[1924]] onward. The *quarre* method is the principal technical instrument of the early factory-scale production of Indonesian turpentine, and its detailed description — preserved by the [[C. Brandts Buys|Brandts Buys]] monograph of [[1928]] — is the principal published source on the practical application of French turpentine-tapping methods in Atjehnese conditions.

## The method

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the *quarre* method involves tapping wounds 10 cm broad and at greatest depth 2½ cm deep, *om de drie dagen in opwaartsche richting ververscht* (refreshed every three days in an upward direction) *met behulp van een eenigszins gebogen timmermansbeitel* (with the aid of a slightly curved carpenter's chisel). The number of tapping wounds per tree depended on the thickness of the trunk: *bij een omtrek van 1-1½ m maakt men één wonde, bij 1½-2 m twee wonden en daarboven drie tapwonden* — *at a circumference of 1-1½ m one makes one wound, at 1½-2 m two wounds, and above that three tapping-wounds*.

## The French origin

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that the *quarre* is *een tapsysteem ontleend aan de terpentijnwinning in Frankrijk (de Landes)* — *a tapping-system borrowed from the turpentine industry in France (the Landes)*. The Landes — the forested coastal plain of south-western France, between [[Bordeaux]] and the [[Pyrenees]] — was the principal European turpentine-producing region in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and the *quarre* method was the standard French tapping method for the [[Pinus Pinaster]] (maritime pine) that was the principal Landes turpentine tree. The transfer of the *quarre* method from the Landes to Atjeh was part of the broader colonial transfer of metropolitan silvicultural techniques to the colonies.

## The Brandts Buys monograph

The [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet]] records that *een uitvoerige beschrijving van de quarre en van de daarmede behaalde resultaten* (an extensive description of the quarre and of the results obtained with it) *is o.a. te vinden op blz. 83 e.v. van de reeds vermelde Mededeeling no. 19 van het Proefstation voor het Boschwezen* (is, among other places, to be found on pp. 83 ff. of the already-mentioned Mededeeling no. 19 of the Proefstation voor het Boschwezen). The *Mededeeling* no. 19 — the [[C. Brandts Buys|Brandts Buys]] monograph *Bijdrage tot de kennis van Pinus Merkusii Jungh et de Vr., meer in het bijzonder in de Gajo-landen* — is thus the principal published source on the *quarre* method as applied at the [[Baleq]] enterprise. The pamphlet's Fig. II — captioned *Sumatra-den getapt met de "quarre"* — illustrates the application of the method, and the pamphlet's Fig. III illustrates the *Ververschen van de tapwonde* (refreshing of the tapping wound).

## Significance

The *quarre*'s significance in the historiography of colonial Aceh is as the principal technical instrument of the early factory-scale production of Indonesian turpentine — the French tapping method that was transferred from the Landes to the [[Gajo-landen|Gajo-Landen]]-[[Atjeh]] border region in [[1924]], and that supplied the raw balsam for the [[Baleq]] enterprise from [[1924]] onward. The method's detailed description — preserved by the [[C. Brandts Buys|Brandts Buys]] monograph of [[1928]] and by the [[Atjeh-terpentijn (Spoor, 1930)|Atjeh-terpentijn pamphlet of 1930]] — is the principal published source on the practical application of French turpentine-tapping methods in Atjehnese conditions, and the method itself is the principal example of the colonial transfer of metropolitan silvicultural techniques to the Netherlands Indies.
