**Arabica Coffee (Aceh)** (Dutch: *Arabica-koffie*, Latin: *Coffea arabica*) was the finest-quality coffee variety grown in the highland interior of the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] and later of the colonial residency of [[Atjeh]], cultivated in limited quantities by indigenous smallholders (Dutch: *inlandsche cultiveurs*) rather than by European estates. Although it fetched premium prices in European markets in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]], its high susceptibility to leaf disease (Dutch: *bladziekte*) confined it to a niche role in the colonial coffee economy of [[Sumatra]], where the more disease-resistant [[Robusta Coffee (Aceh)|robusta]] came to dominate the plantation landscape after [[1900]].

## Cultivation and challenges

Arabica coffee was traditionally planted in the cool, well-drained highland soils of the [[Gajoland|Gayo uplands]] around [[Takèngon|Takèngön]] and the slopes above the [[Westkust van Atjeh|West Coast of Atjeh]], where the climate approximated the Ethiopian highlands in which the species originated. Indigenous growers raised the bushes beneath the shade of protective trees (Dutch: *beschermboomen*), weeded by hand, and harvested the ripe red cherries (Dutch: *koffiebessen*) in the annual picking season. Yields were modest, but the bean's fine acidity and aromatic profile made Acehnese arabica one of the most prized coffees of the Indies.

The principal obstacle to expansion was the fungus *Hemileia vastatrix*, the coffee leaf disease (Dutch: *koffiebladziekte*) that had devastated plantings across [[Java]] and [[Ceylon]] in the [[1870s]] and [[1880s]]. Even in the relatively isolated Gayo highlands, periodic outbreaks of leaf blight (Dutch: *bladziekte*) reduced productivity and discouraged European investors from establishing arabica estates. The colonial agronomists of the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] therefore steered capital toward [[Robusta Coffee (Aceh)|robusta]] on the lower-lying East Coast, leaving arabica largely to Acehnese smallholders.

## Market position and colonial policy

Arabica from the Atjeh highlands was gathered by Chinese and Acehnese middlemen at the weekly markets (Dutch: *pasars*) of [[Takèngon|Takèngön]], [[Bireuen|Bireuën]] and the coastal ports, then shipped via [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]] and [[Belawan]] to the coffee auctions of Amsterdam and Rotterdam. Its premium price rewarded careful sorting and sun-drying, and small lots of "Atjeh-Arabica" regularly topped the catalogues of the colonial coffee trade in the [[1890s]] and the first decade of the twentieth century.

The [[Agrarische Wet 1870]] (Agrarian Law of 1870) opened the way for European leasehold estates (Dutch: *erfpacht-ondernemingen*) on indigenous land, but the leaf-disease risk meant that little European capital was directed at arabica in Atjeh. Instead, the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] encouraged arabica smallholder cultivation through extension work and seed distribution by the [[Buitengewesten|Buitengewesten]] agricultural service. By the [[1920s]] the completion of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] and the [[Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen|Atjeh tram]] had made it easier to move arabica beans to the coast, but the great expansion of the Atjeh coffee economy had already shifted to robusta.

## Decline and legacy

By the [[1930s]] arabica accounted for only a small fraction of Atjeh's coffee exports, having been overtaken by [[Robusta Coffee (Aceh)|robusta]] on the East Coast and by [[Rubber Industry in Atjeh|rubber]] across the residency as a whole. Nevertheless, the smallholder arabica gardens of the Gayo highlands survived the colonial reorientation and formed the genetic and cultural basis of the present-day Aceh arabica trade. The crop remains a tangible link between the pre-colonial spice-and-coffee economy of the [[Sultanate of Acheh]] and the modern cash-crop landscape of upland Aceh.
