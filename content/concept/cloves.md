**Cloves (Aceh)** (Dutch: *kruidnagelen*, Latin: *Syzygium aromaticum*) were the dried flower buds of the clove tree, an indigenous export product (Dutch: *inheems exportproduct*) imported into the residency of [[Atjeh]] from the [[Molukken|Moluccas]] and from Zanzibar and almost exclusively re-exported (Dutch: *uitsluitend wederuitgevoerd*) through the colonial port of [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]] and the [[Sabang|free port of Sabang]] to European and Peninsular Indian markets in the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]]. Although the [[Aceh|Atjehnese]] interior did not itself produce cloves in commercial quantities, the residency served as a redistribution hub for the Indian Ocean spice network, and a substantial import of Zanzibar clove waste (Dutch: *kruidnagel-afval*) was used locally to flavour the chewing tobacco (Dutch: *tjootabak*) wrapped in the chewing sticks known as *inlandse strootjes*.

## Trade flow

Cloves arrived at the ports of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] from two principal sources. The first was the [[Molukken|Moluccas]] — the historic centre of clove production since the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] monopoly of the [[17th century|seventeenth century]] — which supplied whole, high-quality buds for the European confectionery and pharmaceutical trade. The second was Zanzibar, which by the late [[19th century|nineteenth century]] had overtaken the Moluccas as the world's largest clove producer and supplied both premium cloves and the so-called *kruidnagel-afval* (clove waste) that the Atjeh trade used as a flavouring.

After landing at [[Koeta-Radja|Kuta Raja]], [[Sabang]] or [[Belawan]], the cloves were sorted by Chinese and Acehnese middlemen, repacked in mats or tins, and re-exported to [[Java|Batavia]], [[Padang|Padang]], Penang and the European markets. The colonial customs regime treated cloves as a transit commodity (Dutch: *doorvoer-artikel*), and the residency's import and export statistics therefore record roughly equal quantities entering and leaving Atjeh, with only a small balance retained for local consumption.

## Local use: chewing sticks and the *inlandse strootjes*

A distinctive feature of the Atjeh clove trade was the substantial local demand for clove waste (Dutch: *kruidnagel-afval*), the broken and lower-grade buds rejected for the European market. This waste was ground and mixed with tobacco to flavour the chewing sticks (Dutch: *inlandse strootjes*) widely chewed by the [[Atjeh|Atjehnese]] population, often as part of the betel quid (Dutch: *sirihpruim*) that also incorporated [[Pinangnut (Betel Nut)|pinangnut]]. The flavour of clove-flavoured chewing tobacco (Dutch: *tjootabak*) was a marker of Acehnese taste and a small but steady driver of the local spice trade.

## Historical significance

The clove trade illustrated the interconnectedness of the Indian Ocean spice network, with [[Aceh]] acting as a redistribution hub between the Moluccas, Zanzibar, the [[British Empire|British]] straits settlements and Europe. This role had medieval roots — the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] in the [[16th century|sixteenth]] and [[17th century|seventeenth centuries]] had served as the great entrepôt for the spice trade between the Maluku archipelago and the Ottoman, Mughal and European worlds — and the colonial transit trade of the early twentieth century was a direct descendant of that role. By the [[1930s]] the consolidation of the spice trade through direct steamship lines between the Moluccas and Europe had reduced Atjeh's redistributive function, though Penang-based Chinese merchants continued to channel cloves through the [[Sabang|free port of Sabang]] until the Japanese occupation of [[1942]].
