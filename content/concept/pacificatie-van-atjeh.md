**The Pacificatie van Atjeh** — the *Pacification of Atjeh* — was the recurrent Dutch political project, pursued under every Dutch governor of [[Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] from [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] in [[1874]] to [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in [[1898]]–[[1904]], of bringing the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] to a conclusion by obtaining the submission of the Atjehese chiefs and the restoration of peace and order under Dutch sovereignty. The term, with its implication of a negotiated settlement, was the recurrent Dutch euphemism for the conclusion of the war; its most notorious instance was the telegraphed claim of [[Pruijs van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]] of [[September 1882|September]] [[1882]] — "*Pacificatie van Atjeh verzekerd*" (Pacification of Atjeh secured) — that was, in the event, wholly premature.

## The Recurrent Project

The Pacificatie van Atjeh was the recurrent Dutch political project throughout the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Every Dutch governor pursued it; every Dutch governor reported, at some point in his tenure, that the war was nearly over and that the time for civil government had come. The pattern was set by [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] in [[January 1874|January]] [[1874]], when he proposed to step into the Sultan's place after the death of the Sultan and to inaugurate direct Dutch rule. It was continued by [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Pel]] in [[1875]], who judged his task fulfilled with the construction of the thirty-eight fortifications around [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]]; by [[Diemont|Diemont]] in [[1877]], whose governorship saw the visit of [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]] and the optimistic report that the war was as good as over; by [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] in [[1879]]–[[1881]], whose conquest of the [[22 Moekims|22]] and [[26 Moekims|26 Moekims]] was followed by his displacement by civil government under [[Pruijs van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]].

## The Pruijs van der Hoeven Telegrafie (September 1882)

The most notorious instance of the Pacificatie van Atjeh was the telegraphed claim of [[Pruijs van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]] of [[September 1882|September]] [[1882]] — "*Pacificatie van Atjeh verzekerd*" (Pacification of Atjeh secured). The claim, made shortly before Pruijs van der Hoeven's departure, was wholly premature. The numerous engagements and ambushes continued until his departure; the situation collapsed under his successor [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]]; and the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[1884]] was the necessary consequence. The telegrafie became, in Kruijt's brochure, the byword for the Dutch civil government's self-deception in Atjeh.

## The Van Teijn Pursuit (1886–1891)

Under [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]'s governorship (1886–1891), the Pacificatie van Atjeh took the form of the pursuit of the Sultan's submission and the [[Restoration of the Sultanate|restoration of the Sultanate]] under Dutch sovereignty. The pursuit, continued throughout Van Teijn's tenure, led to nothing; the deaths of [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] and [[Panglima Polim|Panglima Polim]] in [[January 1891|January]] [[1891]] removed the principal addressees of the conciliation; and Van Teijn departed in [[April 1891|April]] [[1891]] disappointed.

## The Pahlawan-klucht (1893–1896)

Under [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]]'s governorship (1892–1896), the Pacificatie van Atjeh took the form of the [[Pahlawan-klucht|Pahlawan-klucht]] — the arming of [[Teuku Umar|Teuku Umar]] as *[[Panglima Prang Besar|Panglima Prang Besar]]* and the use of Atjehese auxiliaries against Atjehese enemies. The Pahlawan-klucht was, in its way, the most extreme form of the Pacificatie van Atjeh — the substitution of an Atjehese auxiliary for the Dutch military effort. Its collapse in the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]] demonstrated the failure of the substitution.

## The Final Pacification (1898–1904)

The final Pacificatie van Atjeh — the actual pacification, as distinct from the recurrent Dutch project — was carried through by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] after [[1898]], on the advice of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]. It rested on the systematic application of the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] on the coasts, the suppression of the religious leadership by the *[[Corps Maréchaussée (Atjeh)|corps maréchaussée]]*, and the rigorous military operations against the Muslemin. This final pacification — which vindicated the doctrines of [[Scherer|Scheren]], [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]], and [[Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] himself — was the conclusion of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] that had begun in [[1873]].

## Kruijt's Judgement

Kruijt's judgement of the Pacificatie van Atjeh is double-edged. The project itself — the conclusion of the war by submission and the restoration of peace — was the legitimate aim of every Dutch governor. The means by which it was pursued — the premature optimism of the reports, the disregard of the actual situation, the substitution of Atjehese auxiliaries for Dutch military effort, the failure to apply the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] consistently — was, in Kruijt's analysis, the principal cause of the protraction of the war. The final pacification under Van Heutsz, on the advice of Snouck Hurgronje, vindicated the strategic analysis that Scherer, Van Teijn, and Kruijt himself had articulated.

## Significance

The Pacificatie van Atjeh is the recurrent Dutch political project of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Its pursuit under every Dutch governor — from Van Swieten in [[1874]] to Van Heutsz in [[1898]]–[[1904]] — and its repeated premature announcements — most notoriously by Pruijs van der Hoeven in [[September 1882|September]] [[1882]] — together constitute one of the principal patterns of the war. The final pacification under Van Heutsz — which rested on the systematic application of the *scheepvaartregeling* and the marechaussée campaigns, on the advice of Snouck Hurgronje — was the vindication of the strategic analysis that had been articulated by Scherer, Van Teijn, Van Heutsz, and Kruijt.

## Subjection, pacification and the transitional period

The distinction between *onderwerping* (subjection) and *pacificatie* was fundamental. Atjeh had, in the argument derived from [[Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje]], first to be conquered, thus subjected, and then permanently occupied, thus pacified — "but between subjection and pacification, between starting point and end point, lay a period of transition."

The end-point was defined by Snouck's own formulation: a genuinely pacified Atjeh, in which the chiefs "did not yet have to await orders from that Authority." The means was "the combination of means of pacification derived from sound principles of government with the unavoidably hard measures that must break the resistance."

## The double method

The doctrine required two things simultaneously. Even after the conquest and occupation of a district, the remaining individual or grouped enemies must be struck so hard and so long that the Acehnese peasant would be restrained by *fear* from maintaining any relation whatever with the Acehnese *[[djahat]]*. But at the same time the same peasant must be instilled with *trust* in Dutch authority and Dutch power, and with the certainty of complete protection against those bands. To achieve that it was equally necessary to win the confidence of the well-disposed and indispensable chiefs, since only with and through the chiefs could the population be reached. It was precisely on this second requirement, du Croo argued, that [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] failed.

## Swart's principles

Swart's own maxim, printed as the epigraph of du Croo's concluding chapter, was: "Show the native that you trust him, but at the same time see to it that he realises that you have the power and the strength to impose your will on him, to make him obey."

His subsidiary principles, formulated for administrative work in scarcely subjected districts where pacification had to be built up from below, were: that only by giving trust can trust be obtained, while never forgetting to ask whether the man is worth trusting; that a proper administration of justice must be provided, giving decisions as just as possible *"with regard to the [[adat]]"* and always with the *[[oelama]]* behind the green table, so that the *toean* presiding over the *[[Moesapat]]* should be judged *[[adil]]* (just); that roads should be built everywhere, since well-executed road-building opened a hermetically closed country and promoted trade and intercourse; and that the administrator should travel without military escort.

To these he added the requirement that the chiefs must have complete confidence in the administrator while at the same time being "held him in awe" — knowing that they cannot play with him and can never dare to break their word to him — but that the administrator must in return give them what they need, "dus bovenal steun."

## The material programme

The pacification in its constructive aspect comprised the [[Het Pacificeerende Bestuurswerk 1908-1918|pacifying administrative work]]: the repayment of unlawfully withheld incomes and securities, the support of the *[[oelèëbalang]]s* and their return to their *gampongs*, the issue of rice and seed-*padi*, generous advances for the purchase of plough cattle, the improvement of *sawah* irrigation and of the *[[loeëng]]s*, the encouragement of pepper, oil-palm and rubber cultivation, the building of roads and of the [[Gajòweg]], the extension of the [[Atjehtram]], the founding of *[[volksonderwijs]]* including girls' schools and the [[Normaalcursus]] at [[Koeta Radja]], and the care of public health.

## Du Croo's ten propositions

Du Croo summarised the outcome in ten propositions:

1. The great blessing of Swart's ten-year period was that the government remained and could remain for ten consecutive years in the hands of a Governor with such outstanding administrative talents.
2. In the long series of years from [[1873]] to [[1898]] the Acehnese had gained no better experience of Dutch administration than a clear absence of line and direction, both military and civil; only the period of [[Karel van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]] formed a favourable exception, and it was too short to leave a lasting result.
3. By balanced and calm government alongside vigorous action and, where necessary, the hard and immediate suppression of resistance and unwillingness, Swart brought the Acehnese to the conviction that Dutch government was and remained overwhelming in every field, while at the same time winning the confidence of chiefs and population, so that he was "beside being feared, also respected and beloved."
4. By scrupulously following the lines laid down by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] he succeeded in gradually giving the population the rest the Acehnese so much needed. That could not be achieved quickly or without shocks, because the wounds inflicted in a forty-year implacable struggle were still too fresh, while new wounds were caused in suppressing the last attempts at resistance — "that could not be otherwise, but in spite of it Swart ultimately succeeded in achieving peace and quiet."
5. Because he could so completely win the confidence both of the *adat* chiefs and of the simple *gampong* man, the Acehnese attached himself personally to Swart — "a typically Acehnese characteristic is: attachment to, and the serving of, a person." Through that he succeeded in bringing both chiefs and population to trust the Dutch administration, so that the well-known phrase in the *[[Memorie van Overgave]]* of Swart's first civil successor — "everything in Atjeh that walks about on two legs hates the European" — was in its generality "a gross exaggeration and incorrect — everything depends on the person."
6. Swart's long administration formed an excellent school of government both for his officers and for his administrative officials, provided they served under him long enough; then they made Swart's administrative method their own and followed the road he had laid out when they themselves later held responsible leading positions in Atjeh. Governor [[Van Aken]] — "a figure likewise generally esteemed and beloved" — was the best example; both his and Swart's periods were undoubtedly the most fruitful for Atjeh, though Van Aken's was unfortunately of relatively short duration, since he was appointed member of the [[Raad van Nederlandsch-Indië]] and died shortly afterwards.
7. The qualities the Acehnese valued in Swart above all were his honesty and his justice, qualities he also required of his subordinates.
8. Swart had, by governing and pacifying, made Atjeh ripe for civil government in its full extent, for the handover to a civil Governor, provided that successor continued to govern in accordance with Swart's lines and possessed the tact and skill to leave the military element a generous place for the time being. The best of the civil successors saw this and acted accordingly, practising gradualness in the advancing de-militarisation and leaving a few vulnerable and very sensitive spots under a civil-and-military *gezaghebber*.
9. Beside these administrators working in Swart's spirit there were among his civil and military successors some who did not come from the Van Heutsz–Swart school and who were imperfectly acquainted with Atjeh and its history; they did not follow the guidelines and thereby delayed the process of progress by some years, "which made itself painfully felt."
10. Atjeh was indeed an exceptional province, and it is thanks to Swart's administration and to that of the successors formed in his school that Atjeh could take in the Indies community "the place … which was its due: the place of a normal and normally governed member!"

## The completion of 1936

Du Croo dated the closing stone of the pacification eighteen years after Swart's retirement, in the speech Governor [[Van Aken]] delivered at the handover of the government to Resident [[J. Jongejans|Jongejans]] on Saturday [[7 March]] [[1936]]: "…I also express my particular thanks to the officers exercising administrative authority … and the finest compliment I can pay to you, Sir, as Provincial Military Commander (Colonel [[J. C. Bongers]]), and to your officers in this field, is that I now consider the sub-divisions [[Alaslanden]], [[Lam Meulò]], [[Meulaboh]] and [[Tapa' Toean]] entirely ripe for civil administration, and that only considerations of economy have restrained me from making proposals to that effect." Those four were the last sub-divisions still governed by officers. "The pacification was complete; Swart's great life's work was accomplished."

## Reservations

The word itself was contested. [[I. Feenstra]], writing at Swart's departure in [[1918]], granted that Swart was "a pacifier, a bringer and bearer of peace and quiet," but insisted that "there was not one single pacifier": the peace was the work of whole shifts of builders relieving one another, and "is the much-mentioned pacification not merely a small part of the taut curve of progress, of the path we tread together and along which we ask in vain for height marks and milestones?"

Swart's own conviction was: "It is my sacred conviction that things will never again go wrong in Atjeh" — though, du Croo noted, he forgot to add the condition "provided that the leading civil officials and military men do not spoil my work." That condition was not met in [[1924]]/[[1925]], "and then here and there ([[Westkust van Atjeh|the West Coast]]) things did indeed go briefly wrong. But yet not so badly wrong that it could not be set right within a few months and by a few Atjeh men of action."

## Significance (du Croo, 1943)

The concept of pacification as Swart and du Croo understood it was administrative rather than military: not the defeat of an enemy but the creation of the conditions under which a province could be governed like any other. It is the organising idea of the whole of du Croo's book and the foundation of the title *Pacificator van Atjeh*.
