**Inlandsch Bestuur** (Native Administration) was the system of indigenous governance in the Netherlands East Indies, operating under Dutch oversight. In the context of Atjeh, the Inlandsch Bestuur was a central topic of the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]], particularly regarding the rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied (directly ruled territory) where a properly organized Inlandsch Bestuur was acknowledged to be lacking. The Inlandsch Bestuur encompassed the entire apparatus of indigenous governance—from the highest-ranking Inlandsche hoofden (Native heads) who governed large territories to the village-level keuchiks who administered individual gampong—operating under the supervision and ultimate authority of the European colonial administration. This system represented the colonial state's pragmatic accommodation with indigenous political structures, utilizing existing traditions of local governance as instruments of colonial administration rather than replacing them entirely with European models.

The Inlandsch Bestuur was not a monolithic institution but varied significantly across different regions of the Netherlands East Indies, reflecting the enormous diversity of indigenous political traditions that the colonial state had incorporated into its administrative framework. In Java, the Inlandsch Bestuur was organized around the well-established bupati (regent) system, with its hierarchical structure of regentschap, district, and onderdistrict providing a familiar and relatively standardized framework for governance. In the Buitenbezittingen, the picture was far more varied: in some areas, such as Palembang with its marga system, indigenous governance was organized around territorial units with clear political structures; in others, the colonial administration encountered more fragmented or less hierarchical systems that required different approaches to incorporation. In Atjeh, the Inlandsch Bestuur took a distinctive form shaped by the territory's unique history of resistance, its Islamic traditions, and the protracted process of military conquest.

## Discussion at the Koeta-Radja Meetings

Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] asked at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meeting whether steps could be taken to organize the Inlandsch Bestuur in the directly ruled areas of Atjeh. This question reflected a recognition that the absence of a well-organized Inlandsch Bestuur in the rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied was not merely an administrative gap but a fundamental obstacle to the kind of bestuursdecentralisatie (administrative decentralization) that the reform program envisioned. Without a functioning indigenous administrative apparatus through which decentralised authority could be exercised, the colonial government would remain dependent on direct European oversight for even the most routine administrative tasks, a situation that was both inefficient and inconsistent with the broader goals of the reorganization. De Graaff's inquiry thus touched on one of the core challenges of colonial governance: how to devolve authority to indigenous institutions while maintaining the oversight necessary to ensure that colonial interests were served.

Gouverneur [[H. N. N. A. Swart]] responded with confidence that with proper opleiding (training), the necessary Inlandsche bestuurskrachten (Native administrative forces) would be obtained in [[Groot-Atjeh]] within a few years. Swart's confidence was based on his assessment that the existing [[sagi]]-[[peleebalangschap]]-[[moekimschap]] hierarchy provided a viable framework upon which a more organized Inlandsch Bestuur could be constructed. The three-tier structure, which he had explicitly compared to the well-established Javanese regentschap-district-onderdistrict system, demonstrated that Atjeh possessed indigenous governance traditions that were sufficiently structured and hierarchical to support further development. The key requirement was the provision of training to indigenous officials, enabling them to perform their administrative functions more effectively within the colonial framework while maintaining their legitimacy in the eyes of the local population.

## Exclusion from Reform Discussion

However, De Graaff also excluded the question of training Inlandsche ambtenaren (Native officials) for Atjeh from the reform discussion, citing the existing bestuursverhoudingen (governance relationships) that made standard approaches inapplicable. This exclusion was significant because it revealed the limits of the colonial administration's willingness to invest in the development of Atjeh's indigenous governance structures. While the broader reorganization aimed at strengthening the Inlandsch Bestuur throughout the Buitenbezittingen as a necessary foundation for bestuursdecentralisatie, Atjeh's particular circumstances—including the complex and still-evolving relationship between the colonial government and indigenous communities, the continued security concerns in some areas, and the unusual mix of rechtstreeks bestuurd gebied and Inlandsche Zelfbesturen—were deemed to require a different approach. The decision to exclude Atjeh from the standard training provisions effectively meant that the development of the Inlandsch Bestuur in Atjeh would proceed on an ad hoc basis rather than as part of a systematic reform program.

## Indigenous Officials and the Gewestelijke Raad

The Inlandsch Bestuur in Groot-Atjeh was organized through the [[sagi]]-[[peleebalangschap]]-[[moekimschap]] hierarchy, with Gouverneur Swart explaining that erfelijkheid (heredity) in head appointments was increasingly being set aside in favor of competence. [[G. J. van Dongen]] raised the question of whether Inlandsche ambtenaren could take seats in the proposed [[Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra]], highlighting the tension between the desire to incorporate indigenous officials into the new governance structures and the colonial reluctance to grant them full participatory rights. Van Dongen's question was probing and significant: it went to the heart of the relationship between the Inlandsch Bestuur and the proposed regional governance structures, asking whether indigenous officials would be granted a genuine role in the deliberative processes of the new gewest or whether they would remain subordinate to European administrators. The question reflected a broader debate within the colonial administration about the appropriate balance between incorporating indigenous perspectives and maintaining European control, a debate that was particularly acute in Atjeh given the territory's history of resistance and the still-contested nature of Dutch authority.
