**Sagi** (plural: [[sagi's]]) refers to the three bondgenootschappen (alliances) into which the territory of [[Groot-Atjeh]] was divided during the [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1903]]). Each [[sagi]] comprised a specific number of [[moekims]] (districts) and was led by a hereditary chief bearing the title [[panglima]]. The three [[sagi]]—the [[XXV Moekims]], the [[XXII Moekims]], and the [[XXVI Moekims]]—together encompassed the territory around [[Koeta Radja]] where the sultan of [[Atjeh]] exercised direct authority.

## Structure and leadership

The [[sagi]] system reflected the traditional political structure of [[Atjeh]]. Rather than being unified under a strong central sultanate, the region was divided among independent territorial chiefs—the [[oelèëbalangs]]—who governed over groups of [[moekims]]. These chiefs formed alliances designated by the number of [[moekims]] they originally comprised:

- The [[XXV Moekims]] (led by [[Sri Tjeet Abas]])
- The [[XXII Moekims]] (led by [[Panglima Polim]])
- The [[XXVI Moekims]] (led by [[Toekoe Nja Bantah]] and [[Toekoe Tjoet Lamreng]])

The heads of these [[sagi]] were hereditary rulers with the title [[panglima]]. Their authority was substantial—often greater than that of the sultan himself—particularly in the case of [[Panglima Polim]], who regarded himself as an equal of the sultan and whose influence radiated over all the landscapes of [[Groot-Atjeh]].

## Role in the Aceh War

The [[sagi]] structure shaped Dutch military strategy throughout the war. The Dutch initially sought to control all three [[sagi]] through a combination of military posts, negotiation, and alliance. The [[XXV]] and [[XXVI Moekims]] were gradually brought under control through a combination of military action and negotiated submission—particularly after the campaigns of [[Generaal Van der Heyden]] in [[1877]]–[[1879]] and the alliance efforts of [[Toekoe Djohan]] in [[1893]]–[[1894]].

The [[XXII Moekims]], however, remained the core of resistance. Its leader, [[Panglima Polim]], never submitted until [[1903]], and the Dutch government itself acknowledged that this [[sagi]] had always been the focal point of resistance since the war began. Only the sustained active campaign led by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] from [[1898]] onward—targeting not only the [[XXII Moekims]] but also the [[VII Moekims]] connecting them to [[Pedir]]—finally broke the resistance.

## Political significance

The persistence of the [[sagi]] system—despite the collapse of the sultanate and despite Dutch attempts to restore it—demonstrates that [[Atjeh]] was not a unified state but a federation of independent territorial units. The Dutch strategy of negotiating with a single sultan or arming a single ally (such as [[T. Oema]]) failed precisely because it ignored the distributed nature of authority in the [[sagi]] system. Only when the Dutch adopted a policy of sustained direct action against all resistance centres—guided by [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]]'s analysis—did pacification become possible.
