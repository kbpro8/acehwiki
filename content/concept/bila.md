**Bila** (from Arabic *diya*, blood money) was the Acehnese customary payment exacted for killing or wounding — a central institution of [[Adat|Acehnese customary law]]. The concept of blood money was not foreign to native customary law in other parts of the Eastern Archipelago; in [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] it appears to have been introduced through the medium of Islam, though not without modifications.

## Amount

The ordinary *bila* for causing death in [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] was **500 Spanish (Mexican) dollars**. For wounding the amount was proportionally less.

## Islamic Law vs. Acehnese Practice

The [[Hikum|Mohammedan law]] gives the right of retributive vengeance for wilful murder only to the heir of the victim, or in cases of wounding to the wounded man himself. The execution of vengeance was made subject to the supervision of the public authorities. Yet in Acheh it remained an established rule that blood feuds were decided without any interference by the territorial authorities, simply under the direction of the *panglima [[Kawom|kawom]]* (tribal chief). Exceptions occurred only through the authority of an unusually energetic [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] or unusually influential [[Teungku|ulama]].

## Collection of Blood Money

The highland blood-debtor, to whom a period of one or two years was granted for paying off the *diët*, would go on a journey "to collect subscriptions" (*chö' ripë*) from all his well-to-do fellow tribesmen, sometimes coming down into the lowlands to apply for contributions from those of whose tribal connection the recollection was merely dim.

## Account from *Atjèh* by J. Kreemer (Deel II)

[[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]] provides the following account of *bila* (blood revenge) in Atjèh.

**Bila** (Gayo: *bèla*, Alas: *bilo*) was the Atjèhnese practice of blood revenge — the right to demand the "blood-debt sacrifice" (*bila göb*). *Toentöt bila* meant the right to demand the blood-debt sacrifice. Originally exercised by all members of the wronged person's *[[kawöm|kawöm]]* (clan), *bila* was the violent form of revenge, distinguished from *[[diët|diët]]* (blood money), which was the non-bloody composition.

**Three cases for blood revenge**: in Atjèh, *bila* was especially applicable in three cases: (1) manslaughter (*pöh*) or wounding (*ta'*); (2) adultery (*dina* / *moemoekah* / *meureusam*) — for which the *bila* required killing BOTH guilty parties (*pöh doea kadoeo*); and (3) theft — catching a thief in flagrante delicto (*pöh pantjoeri*).

**The beungköng ordeal**: a traditional ordeal for a death-guilty offender who escaped blood revenge involved the *beungköng* (a particular draped garment), an iron cuirass, and a *reuntjöng sigeupöh*. The offender was placed in the middle of a square with his blood relatives on one side and those of the killed woman and her husband on the other three; he had to hack at a boundary post, after which the offended party could fell him — unless he escaped to his relatives' side. This was known only by tradition in Kreemer's time.

**Sië keubeuë and si geupöh**: a thief caught in flagrante (*pöh pantjoeri*) could be killed, but the killer had to prove the deceased was really a thief through the *peusah pantjoeri* procedure (verification by the Oelèëbalang). Once confirmed, the Oelèëbalang declared the thief *sië keubeuë* ("buffalo meat," i.e. *vogelfrei* / outlawed). The equivalent term *si geupöh* was applied to one who had incurred blood-debt and fled (murderer, adulterer, or thief).
