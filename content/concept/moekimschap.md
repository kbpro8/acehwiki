**The Moekimschap** (plural: moekimschappen) was the lowest indigenous administrative unit in [[Groot-Atjeh]], described by Gouverneur [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. N. A. Swart]] as comparable to the onderdistricten (sub-districts) of Java. The moekim was a cluster of villages organized around a mosque, reflecting the Islamic character of Atjeh's community structure. This religious dimension was fundamental to the moekim's identity and function: the mosque served not only as a place of worship but as the focal point of community life, the site of dispute resolution, and the symbol of collective identity. The keuchik (moekim head) exercised authority over a group of gampong (villages), coordinating communal affairs, representing the community to higher authorities, and maintaining the adat (customary law) and Islamic norms that governed local life.

The moekim system was deeply rooted in the Islamic tradition of community organization, reflecting the profound influence of Islam on Atjeh's political and social structures. Unlike the more secular administrative units of Java, the moekim was explicitly conceived as a religious community as well as a political and administrative one. The centrality of the mosque in the moekim's organization meant that religious leaders—the imam, the ketib, and other ulama—played important roles in governance alongside the keuchik, creating a system in which religious and administrative authority were closely intertwined. This integration of religious and political functions was one of the distinctive features of Atjeh's governance compared to other regions of the Netherlands East Indies, and it posed particular challenges for the colonial administration's efforts to standardize and rationalize indigenous governance structures.

## Onderafdeelingen Organized by Moekim

Specific moekim-based onderafdeelingen in the Gouvernement Atjeh included the XXII Moekims (under Civiel Gezaghebber [[G. F. B. Watrin]]), the XXV Moekims en de Zuidelijke Nederzettingen (under Controleur [[G. J. van Dongen]]), and the XXVI Moekims (combined with Oeléë-Lhenë under Controleur [[F. W. A. L. de Nijs]]). The numbered designations (XXII, XXV, XXVI) referred to the number of moekim units within each administrative subdivision, providing a precise accounting of the territorial extent and organizational complexity of each onderafdeeling. This practice of naming administrative units after their constituent moekims reflected the fundamental importance of the moekim as the basic building block of Atjeh's administrative geography, a unit so well-defined and widely recognized that it could serve as the standard measure of a region's size and complexity.

The three onderafdeelingen mentioned at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meetings represent only a portion of the moekim-based administrative divisions in the Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden. Other divisions existed throughout the territory, each organized around clusters of moekims that varied in number depending on local population density and historical development. The fact that these onderafdeelingen were administered by officials of different ranks—Civiel Gezaghebbers and Controleurs—reflected the varying strategic importance and administrative complexity of different regions within the Gouvernement. Areas deemed to require greater military oversight, such as the XXII Moekims under the military officer Watrin, were placed under Civiel Gezaghebbers, while more pacified regions could be entrusted to civilian Controleurs.

## Position in the Administrative Hierarchy

The moekim system formed the base of the three-tier indigenous administrative hierarchy of Groot-Atjeh: [[sagi]] → [[peleebalangschap]] → moekimschap, analogous to the Javanese regentschap → district → onderdistrict structure. This hierarchy was explained by Gouverneur Swart at the [[Koeta-Radja]] meeting in response to Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]]'s inquiry about indigenous institutions comparable to Palembang's marga's. The comparison with Java's onderdistrict was particularly apt, as both the moekimschap and the onderdistrict served as the primary point of contact between the indigenous population and the colonial administrative apparatus. It was at the moekim level that colonial policies were implemented, taxes were collected, labor services were organized, and disputes were resolved, making the keuchik's role both essential and demanding.

The moekim's position at the base of the administrative hierarchy also meant that it was the unit most directly affected by the tensions and contradictions of colonial governance. The keuchik was expected to serve both as a representative of his community's interests and as an agent of the colonial state, a dual role that could create difficult conflicts of loyalty. The colonial administration's increasing emphasis on competence over erfelijkheid (heredity) in head appointments was particularly relevant at the moekim level, where the quality of local leadership had the most immediate impact on the population's experience of governance. The moekim system's resilience despite decades of military conflict and administrative reorganization testified to its deep roots in Atjeh's social and religious life, as well as its practical utility as a framework for local governance.
