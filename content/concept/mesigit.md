**Mesigit** — Atjehnese/Dutch spelling of standard Indonesian *masjid*, from Arabic *masjid* (مسجد), "mosque" — is the term used in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] (and elsewhere in the Indies) for the Friday congregational mosque, as distinct from the smaller village prayer-house (*[[Meunasah|meunasah]]* in Atjeh, *langgar* or *tadjoeg* on Java). The term and its architectural form are recorded in Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]]'s chapter *De invloed van den Islam* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]).

## Kernkamp's account of the Indonesian mosque

Kernkamp's account of the *mesigit* opens with the architectural form: "In general, in an Eastern land, the architecture strikes the stranger as one of the most characterising Muslim aspects. That applies then especially to the places of worship, the mosques, which in the core lands of the Islam still determine the cityscape of the great centres much more strongly than is the case with our churches. This is naturally above all to be attributed to the there generally used pointed minarets, from whose upper gallery the call to the communal ritual worship is galmed out. In the Indies, too, some mosques have such a minaret. Most, however, lack them and are recognisable by the peculiar, mountain-like form of the roof, which consists of 3 to 5 storeys with open intermediate spaces. The call to prayer often takes place from an opening in the roof."

The Atjeh-specific note: "Because of the slight penetrating power of the human voice in the densely overgrown tropical nature, and for example in Atjeh also because the mosque is usually situated outside the settlement, however, often also a sort of drum is used to urge the faithful to the observance of their duties."

## The mosque interior

Kernkamp's account of the *mesigit*'s interior gives the typical Indonesian form: "The internal space of the buildings, in the Indies, gives little occasion for admiration. The colourful marble claddings of Constantinople will be sought there in vain. Usually the internal space, behind a spacious front gallery where of old justice was spoken, consists only of a single room, of which at most a part is screened off to give women the opportunity to attend the Friday service unseen. There lie some long, narrow mats and carpets, which after the service are again put away, on which the pious are to take their places in rows. The Quran-lecterns are sometimes indeed finely carved. There further hang some lamps, there stands a simple pulpit, and further there is a niche in the wall that indicates the direction to Mecca. Further, one has at one's disposal services for communal meals of a religious character, a staff for the preacher, and a small library of pious works and sermons. Sometimes there also belong to the inventory long strips of white goods (*moesala*), which serve as prayer-rugs. All this movable good, too, is outside the trade (*[[Wakap|wakap]]*)."

## The Atjeh great mosque

The "only more or less pompous mosque, in Koeta Radja, built in Byzantine style" — the Dutch-built replacement [[Meuseugit Raya|Meuseugit Raja]] of [[Kutaradja|Koeta Radja]], constructed to replace the original mosque destroyed in the [[Burning of Meuseugit Raja (1873)|burning of 1873]] — is, in Kernkamp's account, the single exception to the general rule that Indonesian mosques are architecturally unremarkable. The Dutch-built Meuseugit Raja cost more than *f* 200,000; the Atjehners refused to use it; [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] pronounced it *\"een ledig monument onzer dwaasheid\"* (see [[Snouck Hurgronje's ledig monument verdict]]).

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the *mesigit* is significant as the institutional centre of the Friday congregational *[[Sembahjang|sembahjang]]* (ritual prayer), the Friday sermon, and the communal meals of religious character that Kernkamp describes. The [[Meunasah|meunasah]] serves the daily prayer-life of the Atjeh village; the *mesigit* serves the weekly Friday prayer of the Atjeh town. The personnel of the *mesigit* — the *imam*, the *chatib* (preacher), the *bilal* (caller), and the chief *[[Panghoeloe|panghoeloe]]* (mosque director) — together constitute the principal public religious officials of the Atjeh Muslim community.
