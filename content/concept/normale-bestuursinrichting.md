**Normale bestuursinrichting** (normal administrative organisation) was the name given in [[1881]] to the Dutch colonial policy that replaced the unified military government of [[Karel van der Heijden|generaal K. van der Heyden]] in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] by a normalised civil administration on the model of the long-pacified possessions: the separation of the civil and military supreme command — the *tweehoofdig gezag* (two-headed government) — introduced in April [[1881]] and reversed on [[19 August]] [[1884]], when [[Demmeni|kolonel Demmeni]] once more united the civil and military governor's office in one hand. In the analysis of [[J. J. W. E. Verstege|Overste Verstege]] ([[1886]]) the normale bestuursinrichting was the political mistake from which the later [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration]] necessarily followed: it exchanged the just-conquered sword for a mocked "police stick" and *opbeurde* ("raised up", i.e. restored and strengthened) the Atjehsche people for a new resistance.

## The cabinet letter of 11 March 1881

The change was fixed by the *Kabinetsbrief* (cabinet letter) of [[11 March]] [[1881]], litt. N., of the Governor-General [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]] to the civil and military Governor of Atjeh, luitenant-generaal [[Karel van der Heijden|K. van der Heyden]], announcing the decision of the [[Council of Netherlands India|Raad van Indië]] of [[11 September]] [[1880]] (unanimously) that the time had come to introduce on Atjeh a normal administrative arrangement. The system of vigorously concluding the war — pursued, so the letter acknowledged, with so much energy by Van der Heyden — had aimed to break the enemy's power, destroy his means, and by his total submission enable normal conditions, without which full pacification, elevation and development of the land were impossible. The abolition of the abnormal state would, the Governor-General argued, give the chiefs and the population no stronger proof of the Dutch intention to remain on Atjeh and of the irrevocability of the [[Annexation proclamation of Atjeh (1874)|annexation]]; it would "put the crown on the building erected by the heroism and devotion of the Indies army" under the general's energetic direction — who was again offered the Landvoogd's appreciation and the thanks of the government upon his replacement by the civil governor [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruijs van der Hoeven]]. Verstege, who printed the contents at length, objected that of all proofs of the irrevocability of the annexation, precisely this untimely normalisation was the least convincing, being forfeited the moment the Atjehnese learnt — as they did — that the Dutch were henceforth to be governed from behind palisades.

## The two-headed government in practice (1881-1884)

The two-headed government was introduced in April [[1881]] (Verstege dates the execution [[9 April]] [[1881]]; compare [[Establishment of civil government in Aceh (6 April 1881)]]), with Van der Heyden handing over the civil administration to Pruijs van der Hoeven after the warning, closing his parting speech, that "groote voorzichtigheid blijft geboden om de vrucht van jaren niet in gevaar te brengen" ("great caution remains required not to endanger the fruit of years"). In his reports of April and May [[1881]] the new governor still called the state of Atjeh "zeer bevredigend" (very satisfactory). On [[22 May]] [[1881]], however, he ordered that patrols should no longer visit the kampongs, that they should be bound to fixed roads, and that the arrest of armed Atjehers outside a 500-metre ring around the fortifications (bentings) was strictly forbidden; some time later the patrolling was stopped altogether, on the ground that it kept the Atjehers in constant unrest. The garrison troops were increasingly hindered in their field of action — a consequence, Verstege noted, of the two-headed government and of the subordinate position of the military commander. Several Dutch posts were abolished although the well-disposed population kept asking for their retention for its own safety. The inevitable outcome was that the so-called rooverbenden (robber bands) grew in number and boldness; instead of using the appointed military force to expel them, the civil governor entrusted that task to a [[Gewapende Politie|gewapende politie]] (armed police) — a body unsuited for it and not used anywhere else — through which the so-called bands of robbers or maraudeurs, as they were then styled in official language, increasingly grew into hostile bands that resumed the guerilla war with more success and zeal than ever. Thus, in Verstege's summary, a fictitious, for the Dutch authority unhealthy state of peace was systematically created — condemned by the English in the Straits in the most vehement terms, with a demand to make an end to such *beunhazerij* (bungling) — and thus the basis was systematically laid for the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration of 1 March 1885]].

## Assessments and end

The truthful reporting of the [[P. F. Laging Tobias|Gouverneur Laging Tobias]], who took over from Pruijs van der Hoeven on [[16 March]] [[1883]] — Pruijs himself having asked and obtained his discharge, apparently convinced before the Indian government was that Atjeh was for the time being no place for a civil governor — brought the conviction that the splitting of the highest authority in Atjeh had been a great mistake. When Laging Tobias found no support for his effort to bring the Atjeh question to a solution by the [[Herstel van het sultanaat|restoration of the sultanate]], and increasingly observed that the state of Atjeh was spoilt for good and deteriorating further, he proposed to unite the civil and military administration again in one hand; to his disinterested initiative it was due that this was finally, alas at the eleventh hour, proceeded with. On [[19 August]] [[1884]] kolonel [[Demmeni]] entered office as civil *and* military governor. The armed police as used under Pruijs vanished; the Koloniaal Verslag of [[1885]] noted with relief that this police no longer existed.
