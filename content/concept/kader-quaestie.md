**The Kader-quaestie** (cadre question) was the term used to describe the problem of finding sufficient European officer and non-commissioned officer cadres to lead a massively expanded native army in [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]], a challenge that the military administration itself regarded as nearly insoluble and that became a central obstacle in the colonial defence debate of [[1916]] and [[1917]].

## Nature of the Problem

The introduction of [[Inlandsche Militieplicht]] (native compulsory military service) would require a correspondingly large expansion of the European cadre — the officers, non-commissioned officers, and specialist instructors needed to train, lead, and administer native conscript units. The [[Indische Legerbestuur]] (Indian Army administration) considered this requirement "een uiterst moeilijk, ja bijna onoplosbaar vraagstuk voor de invoering van een militie-leger" (an extremely difficult, almost insoluble problem for the introduction of a militia army). The existing European personnel of the [[KNIL]] were insufficient in number to serve as the cadre for a substantially larger force, and the prospect of attracting additional European volunteers to serve as instructors and officers in the tropics was uncertain at best.

## Objection in the Parliamentary Committee

The cadre question was raised as a major objection in the *Voorloopig Verslag* (preliminary report) of the parliamentary committee that examined the defence proposals of [[J. Van der Weyden]]. Committee members argued that without a credible solution to the cadre shortage, any scheme for native militia service would remain purely theoretical. This parliamentary objection gave the Kader-quaestie a formal standing in the legislative process and forced defence advocates to address it directly.

## The Comparison with the Atjeh-vraagstuk

[[Van der Maaten]] drew an explicit and striking comparison between the Kader-quaestie and the [[Atjeh-vraagstuk]] (Aceh Question):

> "In werkelijkheid staat het met de Indische kader-quaestie als indertijd met het zoogenaamde Atjeh-vraagstuk. Evenmin als dit een vraagstuk, laat staan een voor ons onoplosbaar vraagstuk, bleek te zijn, toen men eindelijk rechtuit den eenig logischen weg bewandelde, evenmin is de kader-quaestie een vraagstuk, als men 'tout le monde et son père' maar niet laat meepraten, doch eenvoudig aan een practisch ervaren man met helder hoofd en Indische zaakkennis de regeling der kadervorming opdraagt."

Van der Maaten's argument was that just as the [[Aceh War]] had been prolonged by indecision, excessive consultation, and the refusal to adopt the logically necessary course of action, so too would the cadre question prove tractable once it was entrusted to a practically experienced man with clear judgment and knowledge of Indian affairs — rather than being left to a process in which "tout le monde et son père" (everyone and his father) was allowed to have a say. The comparison implicitly endorsed the decisive methods that had ultimately brought the [[Aceh]] war to a conclusion.

## The "Hollandsche Koopmansgeest"

Van der Maaten identified the "Hollandsche Koopmansgeest" (Dutch merchant spirit) — the habitual tendency of Dutch authorities to settle defence matters "op een koopje" (on the cheap) — as a root cause of both the cadre shortage and broader patterns of defence neglect. This commercial frugality, he argued, had led to chronic underinvestment in the European personnel infrastructure of the [[KNIL]], creating the very cadre deficit that was now cited as an argument against military expansion.

## The Standing Army and Landstorm Question

The Kader-quaestie was further complicated by the related question of whether a native militia should replace or supplement the existing *staande leger* (standing army). [[Van der Maaten]] warned that replacing the standing army with a militia of only thirteen years' total service, without a *landstorm* (reserve) component, would produce an army incapable of resisting a more sensible enemy. The cadre question thus intersected with the broader structural debate over the proper composition of [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]] defence forces, in which the preservation of a professional European-led core was seen by some as indispensable.

## Significance

The Kader-quaestie occupied a pivotal position in the defence debate of [[1916]]–[[1917]], functioning both as a genuine practical constraint and as a rhetorical obstacle deployed by those reluctant to commit resources to colonial military reform. Van der Maaten's comparison with the [[Atjeh-vraagstuk]] reframed it from an insoluble problem into a challenge requiring decisive leadership rather than endless deliberation. The question remained unresolved at the time of writing and continued to shape discussions about the feasibility of [[Inlandsche Militieplicht]] and the future structure of the [[KNIL]].
