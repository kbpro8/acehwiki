The **djakeuet** is the Atjehnese Islamic **rice tithe** — the *zakat al-fitr* or *zakat on rice* — levied on the rice harvest in the [[rijstbouw in Atjeh|Atjeh rice economy]]. The *djakeuet* is a religious obligation, distinct from the secular taxes of the colonial state, and is documented by [[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]] in his [[Kreemer's De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden (1918)|*De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* (1918)]] as one of the principal deductions from the rice harvest.

## The Minimum Harvest

The *djakeuet* is due only on harvests above a specified minimum. Kreemer records the regional variation in this minimum:

- In [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]: 7½ *goentja* (= 75 *naléh* = 1200 *arè*).
- On the Oostkust: 6 *goentja*.
- In [[Pidie|Pidie]]: differently again (the figure is not given in the surviving text).
- In [[Gajoland|Gajo]] for the Meergebied: 3 *koentjo*.
- In [[Gajoland|Gajo]] elsewhere: 4½ *koentjo*.
- In the [[Alasland|Alasland]]: 10 *parah* (where 1 *parah* = 1 *koentjo*).

The minimum is thus the threshold above which the *djakeuet* becomes due; below the minimum, no *djakeuet* is owed. The minimum varies by region according to the local measure-system and the local conception of a subsistence harvest.

## The Rate

The rate of the *djakeuet* is uniformly 1/10 of the harvest — that is, one-tenth of the rice harvested is owed as *djakeuet*. Kreemer expresses this as:

- In Atjeh: 1 *naléh* for every *goentja* harvested (since 1 *goentja* = 10 *naléh*).
- In Gayo: 1 *gantang* for every *koentjo* harvested.
- In the Alasland: 1 *parah* for every 10 *parah* harvested (i.e. 1 *koentjo* in 10).

The 1/10 rate is the canonical Islamic *zakat* rate on agricultural produce, derived from the *Quranic* obligation of *zakat* and from the traditions of the Prophet Muhammad. The *djakeuet* is thus the Atjehnese form of the universal Islamic *zakat al-fitr*.

## The Practice

Kreemer notes, however, that the *djakeuet* is "volstrekt niet regelmatig opgebracht" — by no means regularly paid. The actual payment is, in practice, "geheel aan de vrijgevigheid der betrokkenen overgelaten" — wholly left to the generosity of those concerned. The *djakeuet* is thus a religious obligation that is recognised in principle but is not enforced in practice; its payment depends on the individual's piety and on the social pressure of the local community.

This practical laxity in the payment of the *djakeuet* is a recurrent theme in the Dutch colonial literature on the religious life of the Netherlands Indies Muslims, and is one of the features of the Atjehnese religious practice that distinguishes it from the more formalised *zakat* systems of the Ottoman or the Arab worlds.

## Place in the Atjeh Rice Economy

The *djakeuet* is one of the principal deductions from the rice harvest, alongside the sharecropper's share (under the [[mawaih|mawaih]] arrangement) and the labourers' wages (under the *kòh peung-oepah* arrangement). The *djakeuet* is paid out after the harvest has been measured (*seumeukat*) — the operation by which the *djakeuet* is set aside and the sharecropper's share (if any) is calculated.

The recipient of the *djakeuet* is, in the Atjehnese practice, the religious official of the local community — typically the *teungkoe* or the *imeum* of the *meunasah* (the village prayer-house). The *djakeuet* is thus, in principle, a contribution to the support of the religious infrastructure of the village, and is one of the channels by which the rice-economy of the village is connected to its religious life.

## Significance

The *djakeuet* is the principal Islamic rice-tax of the Atjehnese rice-economy, and is one of the principal deductions from the rice harvest. The *djakeuet* is significant for the encyclopedia of Aceh history as a documented part of the Atjeh rice-economy, recording the Atjehnese form of the universal Islamic *zakat al-fitr* and its regional variation in the minimum harvest, the rate, and the practice. The practical laxity in the payment of the *djakeuet* is one of the documented features of the Atjehnese religious practice, distinguishing it from the more formalised *zakat* systems of other Muslim societies.
