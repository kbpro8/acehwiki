Figures, places, and events in Acehnese history associated with the year 1941.

## People

- [[R. J. Wilkinson]]

## Account from *Atjèh and the War with Japan* (1949) — 1941

**The year 1941** was, in Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan*, the year of [[Resident J. Pauw|Resident Pauw]]'s government conference — the first tentative experiment in representative administration in Atjèh — and, from [[8 December|8 December]], of the [[Pacific War|Pacific War]], which placed Atjèh, "considered safe behind strong Singapore, in a strategically advanced position," and set in motion the cascade of defeats that would culminate in the [[fall of Penang|fall of Penang]] on [[19 December]] [[1941]] and, four months later, the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of Atjèh]].

## The government conference

"In 1941, namely, Resident Pauw took the initiative for a government conference, where every sub-division was represented by an Indonesian administrator appointed by the Resident." The conference was the first institutional expression of the long-deferred aspiration, in nationalist circles, for an [[Atjèh Council|Atjèh Council]] (a body that the Japanese would, in November 1943, finally establish). It was the last colonial attempt to give the population, through its administrators, "some say in the management of general interests concerning the entire Residency." Within months the experiment would be overtaken by war.

## 8 December: outbreak of the Pacific War

"The outbreak of the war with Japan on 8 December 1941 also seemed initially not to influence the favorable picture of the general political situation in Aceh." The early optimism would prove unfounded. "All factors, however, for creating an atmosphere of unrest, which — objectively viewed — contained the possibility of a catastrophic discharge of the tensions, which smoldered beneath the surface … were present." The same outbreak of war, according to the *Pewarta Deli* report Piekaar cites, prompted the central board of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] — "only fourteen days after the outbreak of the war in the Pacific" — to "decide … to cooperate with Japan in order to expel the Dutch." On [[31 December]] [[1941]], in the Tjalang subdistrict, the controleur O. K. L. Pelger could still report: "After the declaration of war on 8 December 1941, the normal cooperation among the groups in the Tjalang ressort continues, one tolerates the other, there is no hatred, the traditional tranquility of the rural ressort prevails."

## 19 December: the fall of Penang

"The unexpectedly rapid Japanese advance in the Malay Peninsula (19 December 1941 fall of Penang, 28 December Ipoh, 11 January 1942 Kuala Lumpur, 15 February Singapore) immediately put Aceh, considered safe behind strong Singapore, in a strategically advanced position." The [[fall of Penang|fall of Penang]] was the strategic pivot: from its capture, "regular air reconnaissance flights took place over Aceh," and "the propaganda conducted by radio-Penang, which was listened to much more generally than we had desired, contributed to" the Indonesian population's realisation "earlier than the Dutch, who against better knowledge continued to believe in the miracle, the inevitability of a Japanese occupation." Penang was, in addition, the indispensable base for the construction of the [[F-organisatie|F-organisatie]]: "Already after the fall of Penang on 19 December 1941, the construction of this organization could be begun."

## The end of the old order

The closing weeks of 1941 marked the end of the long Dutch pacification. "After the declaration of war on 8 December 1941," colonial Atjèh was, in effect, a country at war — though it would not yet know, for another three months, the form the war would take. The pre-war illusion that the Atjèhnese had "finally and definitively submitted to Dutch rule" was extinguished in the cascade of December defeats.

## Account from *Nederlands-Indië contra Japan (1959)* — 1941

**The year 1941** was, in the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), the year of the second phase of the Dutch pre-invasion force drawdown in [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] — the year in which 28 brigades of garrison infantry were dissolved in a single reduction (15 February 1941), in which the city guards were raised, and in which the [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Palmer van den Broek]]–[[C. J. Bekkers|Bekkers]] command transition at the [[2de Garnizoensbataljon|2de Garnizoensbataljon]] was effected. The year [[1941]] is the *terminus ad quem* of the Dutch pre-invasion drawdown, and the year of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]]'s death.

## The 15 February 1941 reduction

On 15 February of that year, the detachments at [[Blang Indië|Blang Indië]], [[Lam No|Lam No]], [[Lho'Nga|Lho'Nga]], [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]], [[Padang Tidji|Padang Tidji]], [[Meureudoe|Meureudoe]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] and [[Boeloe Blang Ara|Boeloe Blang Ara]] (totalling 28 brigades) had to be dissolved. Of the brigades thereby released, a column of 15 brigades had to be formed immediately for assignment elsewhere outside Atjeh. The 2de Garnizoensbataljon at Kotaradja was reinforced with 8 brigades from the dissolved Sabang war detachment; the 3de Garnizoensbataljon was reduced to its [[Sigli|Sigli]] (8) and [[Lho Seumawé|Lho Seumawé]] (4) detachments; the 1ste Garnizoensbataljon staff was moved to [[Medan|Medan]]. The 3de Garnizoensbataljon no longer had any troops and was formally dissolved.

## The city guards and the Maréchaussee shortfall

In the course of 1941, city guards were established at Kotaradja (6 brigades), Sabang (3 brigades), Sigli (3 brigades), Takengon (3 brigades), Langsa (3 brigades) and Koeala Simpang (3 brigades). A portion of the city guardsmen — Atjehnese — had to be dismissed soon afterwards owing to proven untrustworthiness; the proficiency of the remainder was insufficient by the outbreak of war. The Maréchaussee's shortfall reached 200 men; the brigade strength was reduced from 20 to 15 carbines.

## The Palmer van den Broek–Bekkers transition

From July 1941, Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek acted as commander of the Korps Maréchaussee, previously commander of the 2de Garnizoensbataljon, whose command over the latter corps he handed over to Lieutenant-Colonel C. J. Bekkers, who had not previously served in Atjeh. The transition — placing a non-Atjeh-experienced officer in command of the Kotaradja garrison — is the staff history's principal instance of the loss of Atjeh experience at the top of the 2de Garnizoensbataljon.

## The death of Panglima Polem

The death of Panglima Polem in 1941 (he had been decorated, among other things, with the Knight's Cross of the Order of Oranje Nassau for his great support to our administration) placed our government before a great problem. The death of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] in [[1941]] — the *sagihoofd* of the [[XXII Moekims|XXII moekims]] whose conduct as a legendary hero had sustained Dutch authority in the Seulimeumse — was the principal Dutch political loss of [[1941]]. The succession of his eldest son — politically almost outspokenly anti-Dutch in orientation — was the principal Dutch political risk of [[1941]].

## Significance

The year [[1941]] is, in the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|staff history's]] Atjeh chapter, the *terminus ad quem* of the Dutch pre-invasion drawdown, and the year of the structural loss of Atjeh experience at the top of the 2de Garnizoensbataljon. The staff history's emphasis on the [[15 February]] [[15 February 1941|15 February 1941]] reduction, on the Maréchaussee shortfall of 200 men, on the Palmer van den Broek–Bekkers transition, and on the death of [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] frames [[1941]] as the year that closed the structural preconditions of the Dutch pre-invasion garrison: the year by which the Atjeh garrison had been reduced to about one-third of its [[1 January]] [[1 January 1940|1 January 1940]] strength, the year by which the Maréchaussee's established strength had been reduced to 15 carbines per brigade, and the year by which the political anchor of the Seulimeumse had been lost.
