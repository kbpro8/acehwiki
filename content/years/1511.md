**The year 1511** was the year of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka]] — the foundational event of the Portuguese empire in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the proximate cause of the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]] as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malaka for the next hundred and thirty years. In [[1511]], Governor of Portuguese India [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Affonso d'Albuquerque]] sailed from [[Cochin|Cochin]] on [[2 May]] [[1511]] with a fleet of nineteen ships, called at the Atjeh pepper ports of [[Pedir|Pedir]] and [[Pasei|Pasei]] on the way, anchored before Malaka in mid-June [[1511]], and took the city after assaults on [[25 July]] [[1511]] and [[10 August]] [[1511]].

## The conquest

The full account of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest]] is given in the entry on the conquest itself. In summary: [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Albuquerque]] sailed from [[Cochin|Cochin]] on [[2 May]] [[1511]] with nineteen ships, having received a letter from the captive Portuguese factor **Ruy d'Aranjo** urging that the trade of the city could only be secured by force. He called at the Atjeh pepper ports of [[Pedir|Pedir]] and [[Pasei|Pasei]] on the way, where he alarmed the rulers by seizing Muslim merchant vessels. He anchored before Malaka in mid-June [[1511]], demanded the surrender of the prisoners and compensation for the damaged goods of the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]], and added the demand that he be allowed to build a fort. The first assault on [[25 July]] [[1511]] failed; the second, renewed on [[10 August]] [[1511]], took the city after a few days of street fighting.

Albuquerque immediately built the **Fortaleza de Malaca** with materials from the royal tombs and from demolished mosques, introduced a Portuguese administration and a new coinage, and seized the tin mines as crown property. In Europe, Pope **Leo X** ordered a general thanksgiving with a procession to the Roman churches of Santa Maria del Popolo and Sant'Agostino.

## The strategic consequence

The conquest's chief political consequence was the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. The deposed sultan of Malaka retired to **Bintang** and later to [[Djohor|Djohor]], from where he and his successors harassed Portuguese shipping and blockaded Malaka in turn. The sultans of Atjeh — pressing from the west along the north Sumatran coast — became the most dangerous Muslim rivals of the Portuguese. The great Achehnese siege of Malaka of [[1 February|1-17 February]] [[1575]] nearly succeeded; the [[1587]] treaty between Atjeh and the Portuguese, by which [[Aru|Aru]] was again assigned to [[Djohor|Djohor]], opened a generation of relative peace; the Dutch arrival at Atjeh in [[1599]] reopened the strategic contest that Albuquerque's conquest had inaugurated.

## Significance

The year [[1511]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the foundational year of the Portuguese empire in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the proximate cause of the rise of [[Acheh|Acheh]] as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malaka. The [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest of August 1511]] established the strategic framework within which all subsequent Malay-Portuguese and Malay-Dutch relations unfolded: the Portuguese held the central chokepoint of the Strait, the Malay sultans of Malaka's successor states (first Bintang, then Djohor) sought to recover it, and the sultans of Atjeh sought to inherit its commerce. The arrival of the Dutch at Atjeh in [[1599]] — the event that opens Van der Woude's chapter — was thus a continuation by new hands of the Muslim-Christian contest that Albuquerque's conquest had inaugurated. The eventual fall of Malaka to the Dutch and their Djohor allies in [[1641]] would close the cycle that Albuquerque had opened in [[1511]].

## Account from *Over Maleise Literatuur* (1947) — 1511

In [[C. Hooykaas|Hooykaas]]'s survey of Malay literature, the [[1511]] Portuguese conquest of Malacca is the foundational event of the Portuguese empire in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the proximate cause of the rise of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malacca. Hooykaas, in his *Toelichting tot de Sadjarah Malajoe*, records the Portuguese arrival at the Atjeh pepper ports on the way to Malacca: "Eerst zette hij koers naar Pedir (Pidie) op Noord-Sumatra. Daar trof hij een Portugees aan, een van SEQUEIRA'S ontsnapt was en wist mee te delen, dat te Pasai nog meer Portugese vluchtelingen waren" (First he set course for Pedir (Pidie) on North Sumatra. There he found a Portuguese, one of SEQUEIRA's who had escaped, and learned that there were more Portuguese refugees at Pasai).

The [[1511]] conquest thus directly involved the Atjeh pepper ports of [[Pidie|Pedir]] and [[Pasai|Pasei]] — the same ports that, in the early seventeenth century, would be conquered by [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] of Atjeh. The Atjeh pepper ports were, in [[1511]], already part of the Portuguese-Atjehnese-Malay triangle that would dominate the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

*Source: Over Maleise Literatuur*, by Dr. C. Hooykaas, Hoogleraarte Batavia; Tweede Druk (Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1947), Toelichting tot de Sadjarah Malajoe, aantekening bij 34/5-6, p. 264.
