The year **1415** is the date of the Chinese reports cited in Dr. [[F. H. van Naerssen|Van Naerssen]]'s chapter *De oudste cultuurstroomen* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as confirming that, by the early fifteenth century, Islam had spread over the whole north coast of Sumatra — including the future territory of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — and was also established on the east coast as far as the little sultanate of [[Aru]]. Van Naerssen's wording: "According to Chinese reports from 1415, Islam has already spread over the whole north coast of Sumatra and is also established on the east coast as far as the little realm of Aru with its own Sultan. As the preserved tombs show, Goedjarat remained here the source of culture."

## The 1415 Chinese reports

The 1415 Chinese reports cited by Van Naerssen are part of the wider corpus of Ming-dynasty accounts of the tribute missions to the Sumatran coast — accounts that constitute, alongside the [[1297]] Cambay-type tombstone of the founder of [[Samudra]] and the 1419 grave of [[Malik Ibrahim]] at Gresik, the principal external documentary evidence for the early Islamisation of the archipelago. The 1415 reports confirm that, by the early fifteenth century, the religious geography of the Sumatran coast had been transformed: where [[Marco Polo]] in [[1292]] had found only [[Perlak]] to be Muslim, by 1415 the whole north coast — including the future Atjeh — had been Islamised, and the east coast sultanate of [[Aru]] had its own Muslim Sultan.

## Atjeh connection

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the 1415 date marks the moment at which the territory of Atjeh can be documented as lying within the wider Islamic world. The three centuries between the [[1297]] grave of the founder of [[Samudra]] and the [[1550]] [[Conversion of Minangkabau via Atjeh (c. 1550)|conversion of the Minangkabau via Atjeh]] are, on Van Naerssen's account, the period of Atjeh's gradual rise to dominance over the north Sumatran coast — a rise that the 1415 reports confirm was already well under way by the early fifteenth century.
