**The year 1944** was a pivotal moment both in the Japanese occupation of [[Atjèh|Aceh]] and in the wider theatre of the [[Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies]] during [[World II]]. In [[September]] [[1944]] the occupation’s ideological programme shifted from the [[Asia Timoer Raja|Asia Timoer Raja]] concept to the [[Indonesia Merdéka|Indonesia Merdéka]] slogan, while the forced‑labour, rice‑requisitioning and political police systems reached their most oppressive form. At the same time, Allied forces intensified their Pacific campaign, foreshadowing the collapse of Japanese control in the archipelago, a process later reflected upon in the Dutch naval periodical *Onze Vloot* (published in [[February]] [[1946]]). The year also witnessed the Koiso promise of independence, the final construction of the [[Takéngón|Takéngón]]–[[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang Kédjérèn]] road, a series of Allied raids on [[Sabang|Sabang]], and a decisive kempeitai purge of the *oelèëbalang* class.

## The Koiso promise and the shift to Indonesia Merdéka

Prime Minister [[Koiso|Koiso]] declared on [[7 September]] [[1944]] in the 85th session of the Japanese parliament that the “East Indies” (Hindia Timoer) would be granted future independence. The announcement was meant to “bolster wavering confidence” and to spur greater effort, with “[[Indonesia Merdéka|Indonesia Merdéka]]” becoming the watchword of propaganda. For three days flags flew throughout the residency; in [[Koetaradja|Koetaradja]] a mass meeting on [[9 September]] [[1944]] pledged “to cooperate with the Japanese army for the defense of the fatherland … so that as soon as possible an independent East Indies may be obtained.” The *[[Atjèh Shimbun|Atjèh Shimbun]]* issued a 22‑page special edition devoted to the celebration of Idoe’lfitr and the independence promise. This inaugurated the third of Piekaar’s four occupation periods, the [[Indonesia Merdéka|Indonesia Merdéka]] period.

## Labour and rice requisitioning regime

Forced‑labour and rice‑requisitioning systems were formally established in [[1944]]. Residents’ decrees of [[8 April]] [[1944]] nos. 53 and 54 required registration of all labour forces aged 16–45 and created the *Atjèh‑shii‑kéa‑hókó‑dan* (“Perkoempoelan pekerdja² oentoek oemoem didalam Atjèh‑shii dalam hal membina Asia Timoer Raja”) to allocate forced labour equitably. Rice purchase was overhauled through the creation of a “B(agi) D(an) K(oempoel)‑organization” in every sub‑division, demanding compulsory deliveries of 22 000 tons of peeled rice, 5 400 tons of maize, and assorted *katjang kedelee*, *katjang hidjau*, *oebi* and sago. The construction of the [[Takéngón|Takéngón]]–[[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang Kédjérèn]] road, opened on [[20 July]] [[1944]], mobilised “7 000 volunteers … daily.”

## Allied raids on Sabang

On [[25 July]] [[1944]] Allied forces bombarded [[Sabang|Sabang]] “from the sea and from the air,” during which Hr. Ms. *Tromp* “penetrated into the bay of Sabang.” The Resident’s proclamation of [[26 July]] [[1944]] reassured the population, noting that “the vastness of the seas and airspace had indeed enabled the enemy to outwit the vigilance of the Japanese army, but that he had been repelled with heavy losses.” The raid signalled that the war had reached Atjèh’s doorstep.

## Sessions of the Atjèh Council

The [[Atjèh Council|Atjèh Council]] convened its 2nd session on [[4 April]]–[[5 April]] [[1944]] (jointly with a *gun‑chó* conference) and its 3rd session on [[19 October]]–[[20 October]] [[1944]]. The agenda consistently focused on “food supplies, the promotion of food production, and the mobilisation of labour.” On [[10 June]] [[1944]] the 2nd annual conference of the [[Maibkatra|Maibkatra]] took place in Koetaradja, reinforcing the propaganda line.

## Kempeitai purge of the *oelèëbalang* class

In [[May]] [[1944]] the kempeitai intensified actions against the [[Treffers organisation|Treffers organisation]], arresting [[Teukoe Moehamad Hasan|Teukoe Moehamad Hasan]] and [[Teukoe Ali Keureukón|Teukoe Ali Keureukón]], the gun‑chó and acting gun‑chó of Koetaradja. [[Teukoe Moehamad Hasan|Hasan]] and [[Teukoe Sulaiman|Teukoe Sulaiman]] were executed; [[Teukoe Radja Djoema'at|Teukoe Radja Djoema'at]] also perished. The purge crippled the *oelèëbalang* elite, pre‑conditioning the social revolution that would follow the Japanese capitulation.

## Account from the Dutch staff history (Onvlee episode)

According to the Dutch staff history *Nederlands‑Indië contra Japan* (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), [[L. C. G. F. Onvlee|Lt L. C. G. F. Onvlee]] was designated for transport to Japan on [[2 March]] [[1944]]. All designated personnel had to surrender their kit for burning. Onvlee confided in the Dutch camp commander of his Birma POW camp and temporarily handed over the *vaandel* (colours) of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussee]] to the commander, a notable episode of the flag’s temporary loss and recovery.

## Broader military developments

Allied amphibious doctrine, as discussed by contemporary Dutch military observers (see page 415 of *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers van 1665‑1945*), emphasized the need for a professional marine force. This insight informed the post‑war formation of the modern [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps]]. The same source records that Japanese garrisons across the Dutch East Indies, including [[Aceh]], were increasingly strained by supply shortages and guerrilla activity, weakening their control ahead of the [[1945]] surrender.

## Situation in Aceh

In [[1944]] Japanese troops remained stationed in [[Aceh]], but chronic shortages of food, ammunition, and medical supplies, combined with growing resistance from local guerrilla groups, eroded their effective authority. These conditions foreshadowed the rapid collapse of Japanese power in the region the following year.

## Publication details

Captain‑Lieutenant A. Kroese authored an article in *Onze Vloot*, a Dutch naval magazine documenting maritime and colonial military affairs during [[World II]]. The piece analysed the “*vertraging*” (delay) imposed by the Japanese advance on Dutch strategic planning, drawing on experiences of the [[Koninklijk Nederlandsch-Indisch Leger]] and the [[Marinebataljon]] in [[1944]]. It was published in [[February]] [[1946]], offering a contemporary Dutch perspective on the challenges faced during the occupation.

## Significance

The events of 1944 marked the tipping‑point of the Japanese occupation of Atjèh. The propaganda shift to [[Indonesia Merdéka|Indonesia Merdéka]] signalled the failure of the [[Asia Timoer Raja|Asia Timoer Raja]] project; the labour and rice regimes reached their zenith of severity; and the kempeitai purge broke the back of the *oelèëbalang* class. Simultaneously, Allied operations and the deteriorating Japanese position in Aceh illustrated the broader collapse of the occupation across the archipelago. The later reflections published in *Onze Vloot* contributed to post‑war Dutch historiography and influenced the development of the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps]].
