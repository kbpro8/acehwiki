**The year 1509** was the year of the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Diogo Lopez de Sequeira]] mission to [[Malaka|Malaka]] — the first Portuguese visit to the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] coast and the proximate antecedent of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka of 1511]]. In August [[1509]], Sequeira left [[Cochin|Cochin]] with a fleet of five ships under instructions from King Manuel I of Portugal to call at [[Madagascar|Madagascar]], sail to Malaka, demand tribute and load spices. On the way, he called at the pepper ports of [[Pedir|Pedir]] and [[Pasei|Pasei]] on the north coast of Sumatra — the first European visit to the Atjeh coast — where he obtained permission to trade but took on no cargo.

## The Sequeira mission

The [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] is the opening episode of European-Atjeh contact and the immediate antecedent of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka of 1511]]. At [[Pedir|Pedir]] and [[Pasei|Pasei]], Sequeira obtained permission to trade but took on no cargo, "zonder lading in te nemen". From there he proceeded to [[Malaka|Malaka]], where the sultan received the Portuguese civilly and granted them leave to set up a factory. After two months, however — alarmed by reports of Portuguese behaviour elsewhere in the East and pressed by his Muslim merchant community — the sultan prepared to attack the fleet. The plot failed, but several Portuguese were killed on shore and others taken prisoner in the factory, including the factor **Ruy d'Aranjo**. The ships withdrew, taking reprisals on Malay craft they encountered on the way home.

## The strategic consequence

The failure of the Sequeira mission at Malaka was the immediate antecedent of [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Affonso d'Albuquerque]]'s [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest of 1511]]: it was Aranjo's letter from captivity that persuaded Albuquerque that the trade of Malaka could only be secured by force. The Atjeh coast call at Pedir and Pasei in [[1509]] — though without immediate commercial consequence — established the first European commercial presence in the Atjeh region and anticipated the pepper trade that the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers' visit of 1599]] would develop ninety years later.

## Significance

The year [[1509]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the *terminus a quo* of European-Atjeh contact and the proximate antecedent of the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka of 1511]]. The Sequeira mission's Atjeh coast call at Pedir and Pasei established the first European commercial presence in the Atjeh region, and the mission's failure at Malaka set the Portuguese on the path to the conquest of Malaka and the construction of the *Estado da Índia* that would define the Portuguese empire in the East for the next century and a half. The [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Dutch arrival at Atjeh in 1599]], with which Van der Woude's Atjeh chapter opens, was thus the resumption — by new hands and at a new commercial stage — of the European commercial penetration of the Atjeh coast that Sequeira had inaugurated in [[1509]].
