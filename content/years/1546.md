## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**1546** is the year in which, according to *Nederlanders over de Zeeën*, the Muslim coastal kingdom of [[Demak]] on [[Java]] — which had spread "van Malakka tot Bali" (from Malacca to Bali) in the first half of the sixteenth century — finally failed to "verdelgen" (exterminate) the last trace of Hinduism in Java's Eastern Hook. The sultan of Demak "sneuvelt" (fell in battle) in this year during his attempt. The dating is recorded in the introductory survey of pre-Company Asia in Chapter II of *Nederlanders over de Zeeën*.

### The wider Moslim-state context

[[1546]] is given in the source as the chronological pendant to the rise of the great Muslim sultanate of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in [[Noord-Sumatra|North Sumatra]] during the sixteenth century. Where the kingdom of [[Madja-pait|Madjapahit]] had once dominated the Indonesian Archipelago from the fourteenth century, by the sixteenth century small but vigorous Muslim states had taken its place. In North Sumatra, "begint in de 16e eeuw het grote rijk van Atjeh zijn vleugels uit te slaan" (in the sixteenth century the great kingdom of Atjeh begins to spread its wings). Atjeh was to dominate both shores of [[Straat Malakka|the Strait of Malacca]] and repeatedly to bring the Portuguese fortress there into dire straits. In Java, by contrast, the [[1546]] failure of Demak left the field to a successor inland state, [[Mataram]], which would prove harmful to indigenous maritime enterprise.

### Significance

[[1546]] is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because it dates the Java-side failure of the Demak sultanate against which the simultaneous rise of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] is measured in *Nederlanders over de Zeeën*. The contrast — Atjeh's wings spreading, Demak's sultan falling — frames the sixteenth-century divergence of Muslim Sumatra from Muslim Java that would shape the entire Dutch-Atjehnese relationship three centuries later.
