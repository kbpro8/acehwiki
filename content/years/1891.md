**[[1891]]** was a pivotal year in the Dutch colonial administration of [[Acheh]] and in the contemporary press covering the region. In [[1891]] the management of the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh‑tramway]]—the small line between [[Oelèe Lheuë]] and [[Kota Radja]] (built [[1875]], converted to a tramway [[1882]]) together with the *ceintuur‑trambaan* (belt‑tramway) along the [[Postenlinie|postenlinie]] built in [[1885]]—reverted from the [[Departement der Burgerlijke Openbare Werken|Department of Civil Public Works (B.O.W.)]] back to the [[Departement van Oorlog|Department of War]]. The transfer marked the recognition that, despite the [[1882]] attempt to recast the line as a civil tramway, the tracks remained essentially military instruments serving the campaign against the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] resistance.

At the same time, the *[[Atjeh Marineblad]]* continued its publication, providing further reports on Dutch military activities in [[Atjeh (Aceh)]]. The 1891 issues contain accounts of skirmishes in [[Groot Atjeh]] and logistical updates for the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]].

The 1891 reversion closed the first chapter of the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh‑tramway]]’s history—the chapter of small, local lines around [[Kota Radja]]—and prepared the ground for the second chapter that opened with the [[Toekoe Oemar's verraad (1896)|betrayal of Toekoe Oemar in 1896]] and the long‑line construction beginning in [[1897]]. The latter, by following the Dutch extension of authority into the [[Atjeh's Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh Dependencies]], transformed the Atjeh‑tram from a perimeter‑defence system into a network of conquest.

## Significance

The [[1891]] transfer back to the [[Departement van Oorlog|War Department]] is a small but telling index of the relative weight of military and economic considerations in the early history of Dutch [[Acheh]]. For the entire first quarter‑century of its existence, the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh‑tramway]] was an instrument of military occupation rather than of economic development—a fact that the Encyclopaedisch Bureau’s account explicitly foregrounds when it observes that the Atjeh‑tram “was built with exclusively politico‑military intentions, without sufficient regard, at the time of the decision to build, for the economic demands that such a connecting route ought to meet.” The concurrent publication of the *[[Atjeh Marineblad]]* illustrates how the colonial administration sought to disseminate information about its military campaigns to both officials and the broader public.
