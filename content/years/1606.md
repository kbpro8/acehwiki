**The year 1606** was the year of [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff's Malaka campaign]] — the first major Dutch siege of the Portuguese fortress of [[Malaka|Malaka]], conducted by Bewindhebber-Admiraal [[Cornelis Matelieff|Cornelis Matelieff de Jonge]] with a fleet of eleven ships that had left the [[Texel|Texel]] on [[12 May]] [[1605]] and appeared before Malaka on [[30 April]] [[1606]]. The campaign produced the first Dutch-Malakan accord of [[17 May]] [[1606]], the indecisive naval battle against the Portuguese viceroy's relief fleet of [[18 August]] [[1606]], the second accord of [[23 September]] [[1606]] granting the Dutch the right to build a fort on Djohor territory, and the destruction of the Portuguese fleet on [[22 October]] [[1606]]. The year is the principal military-naval year of Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The arrival before Malaka (30 April 1606)

[[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] had left Texel on [[12 May]] [[1605]] with nine ships, having received secret instructions to attack Malaka — instructions that, as Van der Woude notes, "verloren is gegaan". At [[Mauritius|Mauritius]] he conferred with Admiraal [[Steven van der Haghen|Steven van der Haghen]] about the state of the Indies; both agreed that Malaka should be the first target. The fleet's destination was kept secret from the crews until the [[Nicobaren|Nicobaren]], where Matelieff finally announced the plan and overcame the men's reluctance with the promise of prize money. The fleet appeared before Malaka on [[30 April]] [[1606]].

## The first accord (17 May 1606)

Matelieff called on the sultan of [[Djohor|Djohor]] for support. The sultan arrived on [[17 May]] [[1606]] with "een oorlogsvloot van meer dan vijftig galeien en fusten, bemand met drie duizend koppen" — a force that "van weinig of geen beteekenis was". On the same day the first accord was concluded: the sultan promised to assist in the conquest of the city, the Dutch were granted freedom from tolls and a monopoly of the trade, the booty was to be divided, the city would become Dutch property and the surrounding territory Djohor's. The Dutch attack on the city failed; Matelieff was forced to fall back on a blockade to starve the garrison out.

## The naval battles (18 August and 22 October 1606)

On [[14 August]] [[1606]] Matelieff received word that a Portuguese relief fleet — twice the size of his own, under the Viceroy of India — was approaching from Atjeh. Matelieff engaged the Portuguese on [[18 August]] [[1606]]; both sides lost two ships; the Viceroy himself was among the dead. Matelieff, short of ammunition, retired to Djohor to repair his fleet. Returning to Malaka on [[20 October]] [[1606]], the Dutch attacked the remaining seven galleons and three galleys on [[22 October]] [[1606]]; four galleons were captured, the other three the Portuguese themselves set on fire, and many prisoners were taken.

## The second accord (23 September 1606)

During the campaign Matelieff had also concluded the second accord with the sultan of Djohor on [[23 September]] [[1606]], confirming the first accord and granting the Dutch the right to build a fortified post somewhere on the sultan's territory — a right that, as Van der Woude notes, was never realised. The strategic aim — a fortified Dutch post at [[Batoesawer|Batoesawer]] supported by a permanent fleet in the Strait of Malaka — would frame Dutch policy in the Strait for the next decade, until the [[Atjeh's attack on Djohor (May 1613)|Achehnese attack on Djohor of 1613]] destroyed the strategic basis of the Dutch-Djohor alliance.

## Significance

The year [[1606]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal military-naval year of the early Dutch-Indies relationship and the year of the first major Dutch siege of the Portuguese fortress of Malaka. The [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign]] showed both what the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] could achieve (the destruction of the Portuguese fleet) and what it could not (the conquest of a fortified city without a sustained blockade and a land force). The two accords with the sultan of Djohor of [[17 May]] and [[23 September]] [[1606]] defined the formal Dutch-Djohor alliance that would frame Dutch policy in the Strait until the [[Atjeh's attack on Djohor (May 1613)|Achehnese attack of 1613]]. The campaign also marked the strategic turn away from [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — which Van der Woude treats as having lost most of its commercial significance for the Dutch by [[1606]] — and toward [[Djohor|Djohor]] as the Dutch' chief Malay ally.
