## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].

**1871** was the year in which the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|Sumatra-tractaat]] between the [[Netherlands]] and [[Great Britain|England]] was signed, the diplomatic prelude to the [[Atjeh-oorlog ([[1873]]-[[1904]])|Atjeh-oorlog]] two years later. By placing the whole of [[Sumatra]] — including the independent [[Acheh|Atjeh]] sultanate — within the Dutch sphere of influence, the treaty removed the principal external constraint that had hitherto preserved Atjehnese sovereignty, and thereby set the stage for the Dutch declaration of war of [[1873]].

### The Sumatra-tractaat

The treaty, formally an Anglo-Dutch arrangement consolidating earlier understandings, provided that the whole of [[Sumatra]] would fall within the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] sphere of influence. In exchange, the [[Netherlands]] conceded to British subjects the same commercial rights as those enjoyed by Dutch subjects throughout Sumatra. Minister [[P. P. van Bosse|Van Bosse]] delivered "zeer geruststellende verklaringen" — very reassuring statements — in the [[Tweede Kamer]] about the consequences of the instrument he had just signed.

### Parliamentary reception

The reassurance proved short-lived. As [[W. J. van Welderen Rengers|Van Welderen Rengers]] (lib.) observed in the [[Tweede Kamer]] more than two decades later, in colonial affairs "het allerminst het spreekwoord [geldig]: *c'est le premier pas qui coûte*" — it is least of all true that the first step costs the most. The first step, the signature of the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|Sumatra-tractaat]] in [[1871]], had been taken easily; it was the subsequent steps, dictated by prestige, that cost money and lives. Some chambermembers at the time had indeed feared "moeilijkheden met Atjeh" — difficulties with Atjeh — but had been quieted by Van Bosse's reassurances.

### Significance

[[1871]] is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|Sumatra-tractaat]] of that year is the diplomatic hinge on which the entire tragedy of the [[Atjeh-oorlog ([[1873]]-[[1904]])|Atjeh-oorlog]] turns. By recognising Atjeh as falling within the Dutch sphere, the treaty simultaneously supplied the Dutch government with the international warrant for subjugation and removed the principal external counterweight — British interest — that had previously deterred action against the sultanate.

## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].

**1871** is the year in which the [[Netherlands|Dutch government]] concluded a treaty with [[Great Britain|Britain]] by which Dutch possessions on the coast of [[Nieuw Guinea|Guinea]] were ceded to Britain in exchange for a free Dutch hand on [[Sumatra]]. The treaty is the immediate diplomatic prelude to the [[Atjehoorlog|Atjehoorlog]] of [[1873]]-[[1904]]. Conquest of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] was, the source insists, "nog niet gedacht" (not yet contemplated) in [[1871]]; only the suppression of piracy was intended. The slide from suppression to conquest was, however, already implicit.

### The 1871 treaty

The [[1871]] treaty — known in the [[Netherlands]] as the *Sumatra-tractaat* — settled the lingering Anglo-Dutch competition in the Indonesian Archipelago. The [[Netherlands]] ceded its possessions on the coast of [[Nieuw Guinea|Guinea]] to Britain; in exchange, Britain released the [[Netherlands]] from the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 undertaking]] to respect Atjehnese independence. The Dutch hand on Sumatra was now free.

### The Dutch position

The source is explicit that, in [[1871]], conquest of Atjeh was not yet contemplated: "Aan verovering van Atjeh werd nog niet gedacht, alleen aan onderdrukking van zeeroof" (Conquest of Atjeh was not yet contemplated, only the suppression of piracy). The [[1871]] treaty was, on the Dutch reading, a narrowly anti-piracy instrument. The [[1872]] announcement of a Dutch envoy's visit to the sultan — to negotiate a new treaty — was consistent with this reading.

### The Singapore consuls

The [[1872]] announcement produced, however, an Atjehnese request — relayed through the resident of [[Riouw]] — that the Dutch mission be postponed on the pretext of internal discord in Atjeh that had first to be settled. The Dutch government acceded to the request, only to learn that the Atjehnese chiefs had used the delay in [[Singapore]] to visit the consuls of [[Italy]] and the [[United States]]; with the latter, "was zelfs een voorlopige overeenkomst gesloten" (a preliminary agreement was even concluded). The Dutch demand for clarification, and the unsatisfactory response, produced the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|declaration of war of 26 March 1873]].

### Significance

[[1871]] is the diplomatic hinge on which the [[Atjehoorlog|Atjehoorlog]] turns. The *Sumatra-tractaat* of that year freed the [[Netherlands]] from the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 undertaking]] and gave it a free hand on Sumatra. Without the [[1871]] treaty, the [[1873]] declaration of war would have risked British intervention; with the [[1871]] treaty, the war became a purely Dutch affair.

## Account in du Croo’s Generaal Swart (1943)

[[C. Lulofs]]'s figures identify the period **[[1871]]**–[[1890]] as the only one noteworthily distinguished, Atjeh apart, from the twenty-five preceding and sixteen following years in the deployment of Dutch troops in the [[Buitengewesten]].
