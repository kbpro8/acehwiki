**1873** was the opening year of the first large‑scale Dutch military operation against the [[Sultanate of Atjeh|Sultanate of Atjeh]]—the **April 1873 Atjeh Expedition**—and consequently the beginning of the protracted [[Aceh War|Atjeh‑krijg]]. The expedition marked the termination of the pre‑colonial order in Atjeh, the establishment of the colonial [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] in the following year, and the start of a forty‑one‑year arc of Dutch military, administrative, and infrastructural activity that would shape the region until the early twentieth century. The [[Na 1795 (1924)|*Na 1795*]] school-history textbook of F. Roorda fixed the [[1873]] *casus belli* in the canonical Dutch schoolroom memory as Atjehnese piracy (*zéerooverijen*) and the [[Sultan of Acheh|sultan]]'s negotiations to invoke foreign (Italian, Turkish, or American) assistance against the Dutch.

**1873** — as summarised by [[A. Kruisheer|Kruisheer]] in *ATJEH '96* — was also the year of the [[Dutch]] conquest of the [[Kraton|kraton]] of [[Aceh Sultanate|Atjeh]], the founding event of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], and the year to which [[J. A. Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]]'s [[Proclamation of the Regeeringscommissaris (12 April 1896)|Proclamation to the heads and population of Atjeh]] (Bijlage III, p. 339) of [[April]] [[1896]] would later return as the precedent for Dutch rule in [[Groot-Atjeh|Atjeh]]. The year saw the fall of the sultan's [[Kraton|palace]] at [[Koeta Radja]] to the Dutch expeditionary force and the flight and death of the sultan — "omdat hij naar slechte raadgevers geluisterd had" (because he had listened to bad advisers). The conquest inaugurated more than three decades of Dutch presence in Atjeh, against which the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]] of [[March]] [[1896]] and the second great crisis of the war would later be measured. The original Acehnese resistance to the Dutch in [[1873]]–[[1874]] was led by the predecessors of the famous [[Teungkoe di Tiro|di Tiro]] family of religious leaders.

## Background and diplomatic dispute

The first Dutch expedition against [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]] in [[1873]] was the climax of a long diplomatic dispute over Atjeh’s sovereignty. The [[Netherlands]] found itself opposed by other European powers—most notably the [[United Kingdom]] and the [[United States of America]]—which had begun courting the [[Sultans of Atjeh|Sultan of Atjeh]]. Dutch authorities therefore chose a military solution in [[1873]] and dispatched an expeditionary force against the Sultan’s capital.

## Account from *Na 1795* (1924) — the schoolroom *casus belli*

The [[Na 1795 (1924)|*Na 1795*]] school-history textbook of F. Roorda records the [[1873]] *casus belli* and the failure of the first expedition in a single canonical sentence that fixed the war in the Dutch national schoolroom memory:

> *"1873. Oorlogsverklaring aan Atjeh (wegens hun zéerooverijen en omdat de sultan vreemde hulp tegen de Hollanders wilde inroepen). De 1e expeditie mislukte, Köhler sneuvelde, maar de 2e expeditie onder Van Swieten bracht succes. De Kraton (paleis) van den sultan werd ingenomen, Kota Radja werd de zetel van ons gezag."*

The two causes adduced — Atjehnese piracy (*zéerooverijen*) and the [[Sultan of Acheh|sultan]]'s negotiations to invoke foreign (i.e., Italian, Turkish, or American) assistance against the Dutch — are the standard Dutch framing of the *casus belli*. The juxtaposition of the two — a moral accusation (piracy) and a strategic accusation (solicitation of foreign help) — is the rhetorical structure of the Dutch justification: the war was both a punitive action against piracy and a preventive action against the intrusion of a foreign power into the Dutch sphere.

The textbook's two clauses on the expeditions' outcome — "*De Kraton (paleis) van den sultan werd ingenomen*" ("The Kraton (palace) of the sultan was taken") and "*Kota Radja werd de zetel van ons gezag*" ("Kota Radja became the seat of our authority") — fix the two consequences of the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the Kraton]] of [[24 January]] [[1874]]: the strategic objective achieved, and the colonial capital founded. The name *Köhler* — paired with the verb *sneuvelen* (to fall in battle) — is the rhetorical fix of the failure of the first expedition.

## Account from *Van Son genealogy* (1951) — a documented veteran of both expeditions

The [[Geschiedenis en genealogie van het geslacht Van Son (1951)|Van Son genealogy]] records the [[1873]]–[[1874]] service of [[Livinus Diederik Mattheus van Son|Livinus Diederik Mattheus van Son]] — a 2nd lieutenant (promoted to 1st lieutenant on [[21 August]] [[1873]]) of the 9th Battalion Infantry and the *expeditionnaire Bataillon Barisan van Madura* — who took part in both the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first]] and [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second]] expeditions. The genealogy is the explicit primary witness to the manufacture of the [[Atjeh-medaille|Atjeh-medaille]] from captured Atjehnese cannon:

> *"In beide rangen nam hij bij het 9e Bataillon Infanterie en bij het expeditionnaire Bataillon Barisan van Madura, deel aan de 1e en 2e Expeditie tegen Atjeh in de jaren 1873 en 1874, waarna hij gerechtigd werd tot het dragen van het Eereteeken voor belangrijke krijgsbedrijven met gesp, als ook van de Atjeh medaille (vervaardigd uit het in Atjeh veroverde geschut)."*

The genealogical record — a family document, not an official medal citation — is therefore a rare primary witness to the practice of striking campaign medals from captured enemy metal. The cannon in question were presumably taken from the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] or its dependencies in the course of the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second expedition]] of [[November]] [[1873]] – [[January]] [[1874]].

## Outbreak of the Aceh War (January–March 1873)

In [[January 1873]] the Acehnese envoys left [[Riouw]] for [[Singapore]] on [[25 January 1873]]. Their telegram, reported by [[W. H. Read|Consul‑General Read]] on [[16 February 1873]], indicated that they sought assistance from the American and Italian consuls against Dutch encroachment. On [[18 February 1873]] the Minister of Colonies authorised the dispatch of a powerful naval force. The commissioner [[F. N. Nieuwenhuysen|F. N. Nieuwenhuysen]] departed Batavia on [[1 March 1873]], the expedition sailed on [[22 March 1873]] and arrived at Atjeh on [[5 April 1873]].

## First Atjeh Expedition (April–May 1873)

The **April 1873 Atjeh Expedition** began with a landing on [[8 April 1873]] west of the mouth of the Atjeh River. The operation immediately encountered difficulties:

- [[Battle of Kotta Mogat|defeat at Kotta Mogat]] on [[8 April 1873]].
- [[Battle of the Missigit (10 April 1873)|battle of the Missigit]] on [[10 April 1873]].
- Death of [[J. H. R. Köhler|General Köhler]] on [[14 April 1873]].

A council of war on [[18 April 1873]] decided against further attack, and the transport fleet withdrew from the roadstead on [[29 April 1873]]. The expedition’s failure was noted by contemporary observers as a “*hardnekkige godsdienst‑oorlog*” (a stubborn religious war) that would continue for the next three decades.

## Preparations for a second expedition (June–October 1873)

In the months that followed the Dutch government prepared a larger force for a second campaign. Troops were levied, equipped with breech‑loading rifles, and artillery was reinforced with newer breech‑loading guns. The expeditionary force comprised **6 354 infantry** plus cavalry, artillery, and engineers, and was placed under the retired Lieutenant‑general [[Jan van Swieten|Lt‑gen. J. van Swieten]].

## Second Atjeh Expedition (November–December 1873)

The **September 1873 Atjeh Expedition** (often referred to as the **Second Atjeh Expedition**) arrived at the Atjeh roadstead between [[20 November 1873]] and [[29 November 1873]]. Key actions included:

- Landing on [[9 December 1873]] east of the Atjeh River (little resistance).
- Occupation of [[Kota Moesapi|Kota Moesapi]] on [[13 December 1873]].
- Storming of the fortifications at Tiban by [[G. M. Verspyck|General Verspyck]] on [[14 December 1873]].
- Reaching the mouth of the Atjeh River on [[15 December 1873]].
- The [[Bombardment of Pedir (29‑31 December 1873)|bombardment of Pedir]] on [[29 December 1873]]–[[31 December 1873]].

The expedition succeeded in capturing the Sultan’s capital, the *Dalam* (also called [[Kota Radja]]), in early [[1874]], after which Sultan Mahmud Syah withdrew to the interior and continued guerrilla resistance until his death in [[1874]].

## Military personnel and engineering contributions

Among the officers who volunteered for the second expedition was [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Tweede Luitenant C. J. Snijders]] of the [[Korps Ingenieurs, Mineurs en Sappeurs|Korps Ingenieurs, Mineurs en Sappeurs]]. By *Koninklijk Besluit* of [[10 June 1873]] No. 33 he was selected, received instruction in a new optical telegraph system at [[Zeist]], and embarked on the steamship *Conrad* from [[Nieuwediep]] on [[10 July 1873]]. The ship was inspected by [[Prince Hendrik]] before sailing, stopped for repairs at [[Gibraltar]] (where officers visited [[Tangier]]), and proceeded via [[Port Said]] and [[Aden]] to reach [[Batavia]] on [[31 August 1873]]. Snijders later served at the *Hoofdbureau der Genie* in [[Weltevreden]] and at the *Staf der Genie* in [[Surabaya]] (arriving [[24 September 1873]]), before his eventual transfer to [[Atjeh]] in [[October 1874]].

## Cartographic and infrastructural developments

The Dutch administration produced the *[[Map of Groot Atjeh]]* around [[1873]] to aid planning of troop movements and supply lines. In the same year the *[[Atjeh‑tram|Atjeh‑tramway]]* project began; the first railway spur (the “spoorbaantje”) between [[Oelèe Lheuë]] and [[Kota Radja]] was laid in [[1875]], two years after the 1873 expedition, illustrating the link between military conquest and subsequent infrastructural expansion.

## Political events in the Netherlands (1873)

- Liberal defeats in elections at Dokkum, Deventer, Almelo, and Amsterdam; anti‑revolutionary gains.
- The Marine budget was rejected on [[11 December 1873]] (37 vs 30 votes), leading to the discharge of Minister of Marine [[Brocx|Brocx]]; his portfolio was temporarily transferred to the Minister of Colonies.
- [[A. W. P. Weitzel|Minister of War A. W. P. Weitzel]] took office on [[6 October 1873]].
- A reorganisation of the administration of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra’s Oostkust]] was decreed on [[15 May 1873]] following the closure of the [[Expedition to Deli (1872)|expedition to Deli]].

## Religious and cultural incidents

In [[April 1873]] Dutch troops stormed the [[Meusigit Raya|mosque of Meusigit Raja]] at [[Koeta‑Radja|Koeta Radja]], burning and later demolishing the structure. The event, recorded in the *Oost‑Indisch Journael* (1947), is seen as the symbolic opening of the Aceh War. The aftermath included a letter from [[generaal Van Swieten|Van Swieten]] dated [[8 February 1874]] and the subsequent rebuilding of the mosque, illustrating the colonial state’s religious politics in Atjeh.

## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].

**1873** was the year in which the [[Netherlands]] declared war on the [[Sultan of Acheh|sultan of Atjeh]], opening the [[Atjeh-oorlog (1873-1904)|Atjeh-oorlog]] that would last, with brief interruptions, until the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacificatie]] of [[1904]] and beyond. The declaration was the immediate fruit of the [[Sumatra-tractaat (1871)|Sumatra-tractaat]] of [[1871]] and was justified to the [[Tweede Kamer]] on three grounds: piracy, internal warfare, and fear of foreign interference.

### The declaration of war

In [[1873]] the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] government declared war on the [[Sultan of Acheh|sultan of Atjeh]]. The official *motivering* (justification) advanced three causes: alleged piracy emanating from Atjehese waters; the alleged prevalence of internal warfare within the sultanate; and — pre-eminent in the government's view — fear of foreign interference, especially after it became known that the [[Sultan of Acheh|sultan]] had been in contact with the Italian and American consuls in [[Singapore]] during the abortive Dutch-Atjehnese negotiations.

### Reception in the Tweede Kamer

The chamber's reception was mixed. [[H. B. van Kol|Van Kol]] (s.d.) later asked whether Atjehese piracy really was of such alarming proportions, since the colonial reports of the years immediately preceding the declaration did not single out Atjeh in this regard. The colonial report for [[1897]] itself noted that prahus were still robbed and crews murdered on the [[Mentawai-eilanden]] and that even [[Straat Madoera]] was unsafe after sunset. The second reason, internal warfare, was equally unconvincing to critics, since the Netherlands routinely declined to intervene in inter-tribal wars in regions already formally under Dutch sovereignty.

### Kuyper's "straf des hemels"

The third reason — fear of foreign interference — was, the government insisted, sharpened by the opening of the [[Soezkanaal|Suezkanaal]] in [[1869]], which had increased the strategic and economic value of [[Straat Malakka]] as the shipping lane between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. [[Abraham Kuyper|Kuyper]] (a.r.) was so little convinced by the official *motivering* that, when cholera broke out among the Dutch troops the following year, he called it a "straf des hemels" — a punishment from heaven.

### Significance

[[1873]] is the foundational year of the colonial Atjehnese war. Every subsequent parliamentary debate on Atjeh — the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentratie]] of [[1884]]-[[1896]], the [[Toekoe Oemar's defection (1896)|defection of Toekoe Oemar]] in [[1896]], the [[Van Daalen's Gajo-Alas-Batak expedition (1904)|expedition of Van Daalen]] in [[1904]], the [[Avondpost "Wekeer" articles (October 1907)|"Wekeer" affair]] of [[1907]] — refers back to the decision taken in [[1873]].

## Significance

The events of [[1873]] constitute the watershed that transformed Atjeh from a semi‑autonomous sultanate into a Dutch colonial possession. The dual expeditions inaugurated a three‑decade war, prompted the creation of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] in [[1874]], and set in motion a series of infrastructural projects (railway, tramway, cartography) that facilitated Dutch economic exploitation and scientific surveying—most notably the mineral surveys beginning with Rinaud’s [[1885]] coal exploration. The year therefore marks both the violent rupture of the pre‑colonial order and the foundation of the colonial administrative and economic framework that would dominate Aceh until the early twentieth century. The [[Na 1795 (1924)|*Na 1795*]] schoolroom framing of the *casus belli* — piracy and the solicitation of foreign help — fixed the war in the Dutch national memory as both a punitive and a preventive action, and the genealogical record of [[Livinus Diederik Mattheus van Son|L. D. M. van Son]]'s receipt of the [[Atjeh-medaille|Atjeh-medaille]] struck from captured cannon documents, in family-memory form, the conversion of the captured artillery of the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] into the wearable insignia of the Dutch colonial soldier.

## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].

**1873** is the year in which the [[Netherlands]] declared war on the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Atjeh]] and launched the [[Atjehoorlog|Atjehoorlog]] — the longest colonial war in modern Dutch history. The declaration of war was issued on [[26 March]] [[1873]]. The first military expedition, under [[General Köhler|generaal Köhler]], landed ten days later with 3,600 men; Köhler was killed in the assault on the fortified royal residence, and the force, feeling itself too weak to continue, returned. A second expedition, 8,000 strong under [[General Van Swieten|generaal Van Swieten]], landed in December.

### The 26 March declaration

The immediate Dutch grievance was the discovery, in [[1872]], that the Atjehnese chiefs had used a diplomatic postponement to visit the consuls of [[Italy]] and the [[United States]] in [[Singapore]], and that with the latter "was zelfs een voorlopige overeenkomst gesloten" (a preliminary agreement was even concluded). The Dutch demand for clarification from the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Atjeh]] produced no satisfactory response, and on [[26 March]] [[1873]] the declaration of war followed.

### The first expedition (April 1873)

The source records: "Tien dagen later landde generaal Köhler met 3600 man" (Ten days later general Köhler landed with 3,600 men). The first expedition's objective was the assault on the "versterkte vorstenverblijf" (fortified royal residence) — the kraton of the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Atjeh]]. Köhler was killed in the assault. The force, "zich te zwak voelde om door te zetten" (feeling itself too weak to continue), returned. The first expedition was thus a Dutch defeat.

### The second expedition (December 1873)

A second expedition, 8,000 strong under [[General Van Swieten|generaal Van Swieten]], landed in December [[1873]]. After a bombardment of the royal residence, it was surrounded; the residence proved to have been abandoned. By proclamation of the commander, [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] was declared government territory; the fortified kraton was renamed "[[Koeta Radja|Kota-Radja]]". The chiefs of several coastal lands were "overreed tot erkenning van de Nederlandse soevereiniteit" (persuaded to recognise Dutch sovereignty). The source notes, however, that "Het bereikte resultaat werd ver overschat" (the achieved result was greatly overestimated).

### The Pel interlude

[[General Van Swieten|Van Swieten]] returned with the main force to [[Batavia]], leaving 3,000 men under [[Pel]]. Pel's instructions were to bring the chiefs to submission by amicable arrangement, to exercise patience, and to maintain a waiting posture. The Atjehnese, "die zich volstrekt niet verslagen gevoelden" (who did not feel themselves at all defeated), soon resumed their attacks.

### Significance

[[1873]] is the foundational year of the [[Atjehoorlog|Atjehoorlog]]. The [[26 March]] declaration, the April failure of the first expedition, and the December apparent success of the second together set the pattern for the next thirty-one years: the Dutch would repeatedly declare the war over, repeatedly underestimate Atjehnese resistance, and repeatedly find that the conquest had to be done again.

**1873** was a pivotal year in the history of [[Atjeh]], marking the beginning of the [[First Aceh Expedition]] and the subsequent military conflicts between the Dutch forces and the Acehnese. Key events included the [[Landing on Atjeh's Coasts]] on [[8 April]] [[1873]], the [[Reconnaissance of the Kraton]] on [[9 April]] [[1873]], the [[First Conquest of the Great Missigit]] on [[10 April]] [[1873]], and the [[Second Conquest of the Great Missigit]] on [[14 April]] [[1873]]. Later in the year, the Second Aceh Expedition began with another landing on [[9 December]] [[1873]].

## Account from *Atjeh* by H.C. Zentgraff (1938)

[[H.C. Zentgraff|Zentgraff]] opens his account with the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|First Aceh Expedition]]: on [[8 April]] [[1873]] *gen.-maj.* [[J.H.R. Köhler|Köhler]] was killed beneath a tree at the edge of the *mesdjid* at [[Koetaradja]] — the tree long known as the *Köhler-boom*, which stood until [[1936]]. *Bij den aanvang van dien krijg hadden wij eene koloniale ervaring welke eene periode van bijna twee en een halve eeuw omvatte* — Holland, with two-and-a-half centuries of colonial experience, was nevertheless *bad starters* in Atjeh. A second expedition was prepared for later the same year.

## Account from *De Atjeh-Oorlog* by J.A. Kruijt (1896)

**1873**, in [[J. A. Kruijt|Kruijt]]'s *De Atjeh-Oorlog* ([[1896]]), was the opening year of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] — the year of the [[Declaration of War on Aceh (1873)|Dutch declaration of war]] in [[March]] [[1873]], the disastrous [[Eerste Atjeh-expeditie (April 1873)|first expedition]] of [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]] under [[J. H. R. Köhler|General Köhler]], the beginning of the East Coast administration under [[C. H. Bogaert|Bogaert]], the proclamation of the blockade on [[21 June]] [[1873]], and the assembly of the [[Tweede Atjeh-expeditie (1873-1874)|second expedition]] that departed for the Atjeh roadstead towards the end of [[November 1873|November]] [[1873]].

The [[Declaration of War on Aceh (1873)|Dutch declaration of war on Atjeh]] in [[March]] [[1873]] followed the discovery that the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Sultanate of Atjeh]] was on the point of concluding an agreement with a foreign power. The Dutch government, regarding its supremacy on [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] as at stake, chose to make foreign intervention in Atjeh impossible by force of arms. The satisfaction demanded of the Sultan was not obtained, and the war was declared.

The first expedition — comprising "*buiten de Marine, ruim 3000 man*" (more than 3,000 men besides the Marine) — landed on [[8 April]] [[1873]] under heavy enemy resistance. On [[10 April]] the troops advanced inland, took the [[Meusigit Raja|groote missigit]] (great mosque) after bloody fighting, but could not locate the [[Kraton of Aceh|kraton]]. On [[14 April]] [[1873]] General Köhler was killed in action. The column retired on [[17 April]] and re-embarked on [[26 April]] [[1873]]. "*De eerste expeditie was mislukt*" (The first expedition had failed).

On [[7 May]] [[1873]] the Dutch flag was raised on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] in the little state of [[Edi|Edi]] — only days after the Dutch flag had disappeared from the coast of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. Under the able leadership of [[C. H. Bogaert|Lieutenant Bogaert]], who appeared in [[June 1873|June]] [[1873]] as *stationscommandant* for the East Coast, Dutch influence rapidly extended over the whole East Coast. Edi alone was permitted to import and export and became the commercial centre of the whole East Coast. By [[21 June]] [[1873]] the blockade of the Atjeh coast was officially proclaimed.

While the [[Tweede Atjeh-expeditie (1873-1874)|second expedition]] was being assembled at [[Batavia|Batavia]] in the second half of [[1873]], the Atjehers made vain attempts to obtain help from foreign powers. [[Habib Abdul Rachman El-Zahir|Habib Abdul Rachman el-Zahir]], despatched to [[Constantinople|Konstantinopel]] for this purpose, returned empty-handed to [[Penang|Penang]], where the *[[Atjehsche Raad van Achten|Atjehsche Raad van Achten]]* (Atjeh Council of Eight) was formed; its peace proposals were rejected by the Dutch. At the end of [[1873]] [[Pedir|Pedir]] was chastised by the [[Koninklijke Marine|Marine]] for its hostility. Towards the end of [[November 1873|November]] [[1873]] the ships of the second expedition arrived at the Atjeh roadstead.

*De Atjeh-Oorlog: Kort Overzicht van Oorsprong, Verloop en Gevolg*, door J. A. Kruijt. 's-Gravenhage: Loman & Funke, 1896. Chapters I–III, pp. 6–13.

## Account from *Een Vaderlandsch Belang* by H. Borel (1878)

**[[1873]]** was the opening year of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], in which the Dutch colonial government launched two expeditions against the sultanate of [[Atjeh (Borel account)|Atjeh]] — the [[First Aceh Expedition (April 1873)|first]] under [[Generaal Köhler]] ending in catastrophic failure, and the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873–1874)|second]] under the recalled [[Jan van Swieten|generaal van Swieten]] that ended the year in a costly bivouac before the [[Kraton of Atjeh|Kraton]]. In Borel's account the year is one of shock, hasty reorganisation, and the beginnings of the strategic errors that would prolong the war for the next five years. The Dutch discovery that the Atjehneezen were "woest, dapper, vrijheidlievend" rather than the easy adversary of colonial assumption set the terms of the entire conflict.

## The First Expedition and Köhler's Death (April [[1873]])

The year opened with the [[First Aceh Expedition (April 1873)|first Dutch expedition]] against Atjeh, commanded by [[Generaal Köhler]]. Dutch forces were repulsed before the [[Kraton of Atjeh|Kraton]] and Köhler himself was killed outside its walls. The news of the defeat made an "enormous indruk" in both Indië and Nederland; the Dutch nation became engaged with the war and demanded vengeance. The Atjehneezen, Borel judged, had gained time to prepare even greater obstacles, and the government realised that, "want door een tweede defeat zou ons prestige in Indië geheel vernietigd zijn," only a much larger expedition could restore Dutch prestige. Köhler's failure Borel treats as "slechts een voorspel" of the drama to come.

## The Atjeh-enquête and Preparations (April–October [[1873]])

The failure of Köhler was followed by the [[Atjeh-enquête|onzalige enquête]], a parliamentary inquest that Borel mentions with disapproval. In October [[1873]] artillery tests were conducted at Tjikarang on Java, where the new [[Achterlaadkanonnen van 12 c.M.|achterlaadkanonnen van 12 c.M.]] — the first breech-loading rifled guns ever sent on an Indische expedition — were trialled. They had "uitstekende diensten bewezen" and would be among the few pieces of materieel that Borel consistently praises. The Indische regeering began assembling three brigades on Java and Sumatra's Westkust for a vastly stronger second expedition. The [[Tactische aanbevelingen en wenken (1873)|Tactische aanbevelingen en wenken]] booklet was printed by the Departement van Oorlog and distributed to officers, prescribing the maxim that "Slechts eene krachtige vervolging kan de overwinning vruchtbaar maken."

## Appointment of Van Swieten and the Padang Decision (November [[1873]])

In November [[1873]] the gepensioneerd luitenant-generaal [[Jan van Swieten|van Swieten]] was appointed [[Appointment of Jan van Swieten as opperbevelhebber (1873)|opperbevelhebber der zee- en landmacht]] and tevens regeerings-commissaris te Atjeh, with "bijna onbeperkte volmacht." The appointment of a retired officer was controversial — the [[Indische Leger|Indische leger]], which no longer knew him personally, found him "geenszyns populair." [[Generaal Verspijck|generaal Verspijck]] was added as tweede bevelhebber, but his diverging inzichten were "niet gekend" and he was to play only an ondergeschikte rol. Three brigades were fully equipped, but van Swieten decided that two would suffice and left the third — the [[Padangsche brigade|Padangsche brigade]] — entirely at [[Padang]]. Borel calls this "eene groote strategische fout," since Verspijck had proposed landing it at the [[Kroeng Raba-baai]] for a pincer movement on the Kraton, a plan proved feasible by [[Generaal Pel|generaal Pel]] three years later. Troops and koelies were unnecessarily crowded onto too few transport ships, causing "groote ellende" and feeding a cholera outbreak that cost "vele menschenlevens."

## Landing at Pedro-punt and the Battle of Lemboe (December [[1873]])

On [[6 December]] [[1873]] the [[Landing at Pedro-punt (6 December 1873)|landing at Pedro-punt]] took place east of [[Kwala Gigieng|Gigieng]], "waar de vijand ons stellig niet verwacht had." The landing was "uitstekend bedacht en zeer goed uitgevoerd"; the Atjehneezen suffered "zware verliezen" during the march along the strand and the village [[Penajoeng]] on the right bank of the [[Atjeh-rivier]] was occupied as the Dutch base. After eight idle days the Atjehneezen had entrenched everywhere, and on [[25 December|25]]–[[26 December]] [[1873]] the [[Battle of Lemboe (25–26 December 1873)|bloody reconnaissances at Lemboe]] exposed the kind of warfare to come. Strong columns rukten zuidwaarts and met enemy stellingen "van alle zijden hevig beschoten"; for hours Dutch troops were exposed on the open [[Rice field|rice field]] to "moordend vuur." Lemboe was finally taken after a "bloedigen strijd," at a cost of nearly 150 gesneuvelden and gekwetsten, but "onbegrijpelijk genoeg" van Swieten ordered it immediately abandoned — the first of his strategic errors that Borel returns to throughout the book.

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893) — 1873 as the diagnostic pivot

[[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s [[1893]] tract *[[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|De onderwerping van Atjeh]]* — written twenty years into the war — fixes [[1873]] as the year against which every subsequent policy reversal is measured: the moment at which "eene rigoureuse blokkade over het geheele kustgebied van het rijk" lay "voor de hand" as the obvious war-measure, but from which the colony had "zich toen niet laten verleiden" — had not allowed itself to be seduced — to maintain, and instead opened the *kuststaten* to trade as they successively submitted. The historical survey of the tract is explicitly dated "sedert 1873" — "aan de hand van Atjeh's geschiedenis sedert 1873" — and the catalogue of eleven Governors of Atjeh that the tract surveys opens with [[Van Swieten|Generaal van Swieten]], the commander of the November [[1873]] Second Atjeh Expedition and the first military and civil Governor of Atjeh ([[1873]]–[[1874]]).

In Van Heutsz's narrative, [[1873]] is the year that fixes the operational question of the next two decades: whether the colony would sustain the rigorous closure of the entire coast that the war's opening had made available, or whether — as in fact happened — it would "de kuststaten weder voor den handel te openen naarmate zij onze souvereiniteit erkenden en onze vlag heschen" (open the coastal statelets to trade in proportion as they recognised our sovereignty and hoisted our flag). Van Heutsz's counter-factual, written in [[1893]], is severe:

> "Welnu, hadden wij ons toen niet laten verleiden om de kuststaten weder voor den handel te openen naarmate zij onze souvereiniteit erkenden en onze vlag heschen, maar was door ons de blokkade krachtig volgehouden van Tamiang op de Oostkust tot Troemon op de Westkust, zooals de Regeering in 1884 wilde, dan zouden de hoofden, zoowel van Groot-Atjeh als van de Onderhoorigheden, vooral na het succes onzer wapenen in 't einde van 1873 en 't begin van 1874 zich aan onzen wil hebben onderworpen, om aan den geweldigen dwang van die onverzettelijk doorgevoerde blokkade te ontkomen".

The counter-factual turns [[1873]] into the diagnostic pivot of the entire tract: had the rigorous *blokkade* of "Tamiang op de Oostkust tot Troemon op de Westkust" been sustained from [[1873]] onward — as the Regeering would later insist it should have been in [[1884]] — the *hoofden* of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and the [[Onderhoorigheden]] would, "vooral na het succes onzer wapenen in 't einde van 1873 en 't begin van 1874", have submitted to the "geweldigen dwang" of the unrelenting blockade. The counter-factual is the rhetorical foundation on which the 1893 tract's prescription of a marine-enforced closure and a [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] — both already available in [[1873]] as policy options — is built. [[1873]] is thus, in Van Heutsz's narrative, simultaneously the year of the colony's greatest operational opportunity and the year of its original operational mistake — the year the rigorous closure was *voor de hand* and was not taken.

## Account from *Verkenningen in Atjeh* (1923)

**1873** was the year of the first Dutch military expedition against the [[Aceh|Atjehnese]] sultanate — the opening of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] — and the year of the earliest Dutch contacts with the *[[Oeléebalang|oeléebalangs]]* of the eastern coast of Atjeh. [[J. A. Kruyt|J. A. Kruyt]], in *[[Atjeh en de Atjehers (Kruyt)|Atjeh en de Atjehers]]* ([[1877]]), records that he served aboard the man-of-war *Timor* from [[1873]] to [[1875]] and was present at the Netherlands government's first contacts with the princes of the eastern coast. The pepper-price at [[Penang|Penang]] in August [[1873]] was 200–250 pillar-dollars the *kojang*, against 90 three years earlier. [[Teukoe Tjhi Perlak|Teukoe Tjhi Perlak]] — *'het jong van Peusangan'* — was already back at [[Peureula (Perlak)|Perlak]] and stood beside his father as a military commander.

## Account in du Croo’s Generaal Swart (1943)

**[[1873]]** was the opening year of the [[Atjeh-oorlog]]: "it was in 1873 that the first general, Major-General [[Köhler]], fell in the first [[Atjeh-expeditie|Atjeh expedition]]", falling at [[Koeta Radja]] by the *[[missigit]]*, a few hundred metres from his grave. In [[1918]], forty-five years later, the last general left Atjeh. The *Eereteeken voor belangrijke krijgsbedrijven* for Atjeh covers [[1873]]–[[1890]]; from [[1873]] to [[1898]] the Acehnese, in [[Maurice Henri du Croo|du Croo]]'s judgement, gained no better experience of Dutch administration than a clear absence of line and direction. With the same troop strength needed from [[1873]] to hold scattered posts over a limited area, the whole archipelago was later conquered.

## Nineteenth-Century Historiography & Campaign Analysis

## The First Aceh Expedition

On [[22 March]] [[1873]], General [[J. H. R. Köhler]] issued the [[Proclamation of 22 March 1873]], demanding the submission of the [[Aceh Sultanate]] to the [[Dutch East Indies Government]]. The expeditionary force arrived off the Acehnese coast on [[5 April]] [[1873]] and debarked at [[Meuraxa]] on [[8 April]] [[1873]]. 

Fierce resistance from Acehnese defenders during the [[Battle of Baiturrahman Mosque]] on [[10 April]] and [[14 April]] [[1873]] resulted in the death of General [[J. H. R. Köhler]]. His successor, General [[E. C. van Daalen]], recognized the severe hazards posed by the approaching [[Southwest Monsoon in Aceh Operations]] and ordered the withdrawal of the [[First Aceh Expedition]] after [[16 April]] [[1873]].

## Institutional Reforms and the Second Expedition

The failure of the spring campaign in [[1873]] sparked political upheaval in [[Batavia]] and The Hague. On [[10 May]] [[1873]], the [[Jonckbloet Note of 10 May 1873]] was presented, and in June [[1873]] the government established the [[Bureau for War Equipments on Sumatra]] to organize a massive retaliatory armada.

Under the leadership of General [[J. van Swieten]], the [[Second Aceh Expedition]] landed at [[Koerong Raba Bay]] in December [[1873]]. By late December [[1873]], Dutch columns had captured [[Meuraxa]] and [[Pekan Badak]], preparing for the decisive assault on [[Kraton of Aceh]] in January [[1874]].
