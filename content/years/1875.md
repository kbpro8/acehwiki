**[[1875]]** was a watershed year in the Dutch colonial history of [[Atjeh]], marked by a series of administrative reforms, a severe health crisis, the inauguration of the first railway line in the region, and a decisive shift from punitive raids to a sustained occupation.  In the spring, [[J. L. J. H. Pel|General Pel]] issued his programmatic note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] and returned to the field on [[8 November]] [[1875]] after a brief leave; the Governor‑Generalship passed to [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Governor‑General Van Lansberge]] on [[26 March]] [[1875]]; a health commission was created by Indies decree of [[12 April]] [[1875]]; and the regulation of the [[heerendiensten|heerendiensten]] was confirmed on [[1 October]] [[1875]].  Simultaneously, the Dutch launched a renewed offensive under Colonel [[M. P. Meyer]], landing on [[26 December]] [[1875]] and beginning a programme of permanent fort construction that would shape the later [[Aceh War]].

## Governance and Administrative Reforms

### Van Lansberge’s Assumption of Office  

On [[26 March]] [[1875]] [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Governor‑General Van Lansberge]] succeeded [[J. Loudon|Mr. J. Loudon]] following the Royal Decree of [[17 December]] [[1874]].  In January [[1875]] the vice‑president of the [[Council of Netherlands India|Raad of Nederlandsch‑Indië]], [[O. van Rees|Mr. O. van Rees]], was removed from the commission for the improvement of Indian taxes after the Opperbestuur, advised by the [[Raad van State|Raad van State]] in [[November 1874]], judged that article 36 of the [[Regeeringsreglement|Regeeringsreglement]] prohibited such a dual role.

### Health Commission  

An Indies decree of [[12 April]] [[1875]] appointed a commission to investigate the “many and serious cases of illness” afflicting Dutch positions in Atjeh.  Its report of [[1 July]] [[1875]] prompted partial implementation, notably the ordering of works at the sea‑head on iron screw‑piles at [[Oleh‑leh|Oleh‑leh]] (completed in September [[1876]]) and the railway between Oleh‑leh and [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] (completed in November [[1876]]).

### Regulation of the Heerendiensten  

The decree of [[1 October]] [[1875]] confirmed the existing regulation of the [[heerendiensten|heerendiensten]], while pressure grew to abolish the *pantjensdiensten* (services owed by native chiefs).  The same month saw continued progress toward the abolition of slavery in the *buitenbezittingen* (outer possessions).

### Other Fiscal Measures  

By the wet of [[6 April]] [[1875]] (Stbl. n°. 61) a sum was earmarked for the construction of the Soerabaya—Pasoeroean—Malang railway line, reflecting broader colonial investment in infrastructure.

## Military Operations and Campaigns

### Pel’s Programme (30 April 1875)  

In his note of [[30 April]] [[1875]] to the Governor‑General, General Pel evaluated the situation after the successful capture of the “hoofd‑stelling” and secured sea communication, but lamented that “all those victories have as yet given few political results.”  He identified the war‑party—led by chiefs such as [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]], [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]], and [[Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang|Toekoe Rajoet Lamkapang]]—as a persistent obstacle.  Pel’s programme called for consolidation of the current position, resumption of hostilities when feasible, introduction of direct administration in occupied territories, and avoidance of imposing direct rule on regions that might willingly submit.

### Pel’s Leave and Return  

Promoted to major‑general by Royal Decree of [[18 February]] [[1875]], Pel departed for Java on [[13 June]] [[1875]] to recover his health.  He reported that the fortifications at [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] were “on all sides secured by an unbroken chain of fortifications” and that sea communication via the Atjeh‑rivier, the landing‑place at [[Oleh‑leh|Oleh‑leh]], and the newly laid road was fully assured.  Pel returned to Atjeh on [[8 November]] [[1875]] with instructions dated [[10 November]] [[1875]] to act on his earlier programme and to subjugate the [[VI Moekims|VI]], [[IV Moekims|IV]] and [[IX Moekims|IX]] Moekims as well as the eastern territory up to [[Kroëng Raja|Kroëng Raja]].  During his absence, Colonel [[G. B. T. Wiggers van Kerchem|G. B. T. Wiggers van Kerchem]] acted as temporary replacement.

### Colonel M. P. Meyer’s Renewed Offensive  

In the latter half of [[1875]] the Dutch shifted to a policy of permanent occupation.  Colonel [[M. P. Meyer]] was appointed commander of the 1875 offensive.  Under his direction the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Royal Netherlands Marine Corps]] (the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]]) landed on [[26 December]] [[1875]] at a strategically chosen beachhead, establishing a supply depot and immediately commencing the construction of coastal forts.  These fortifications, later documented in Cambier’s monographs, enabled a continuous Dutch military footprint and reduced the reliance on intermittent punitive raids.

The campaign continued through [[1876]], officially concluding on [[4 September]] [[1876]], and demonstrated a coordinated use of naval squadrons, marine detachments, and regular army battalions.  Contemporary patrol reports by [[J. B. Verhey|Verhey]] (continuing from [[1874]]) emphasize Meyer’s focus on a sustained presence rather than isolated actions.

## Health Crisis  

The year [[1875]] saw 957 deaths and 5 151 evacuations from cholera and other diseases.  Contemporary accounts attributed the “unfavourable situation” to poor drinking water, fallow rice fields, deep‑cutting lagunes, extensive ground‑moving, deforestation, and inadequate shelter against the variable climate.  The high morbidity forced healthy soldiers to perform exhausting duties, which in turn accelerated further sickness.

## Infrastructure: Railway and Fortifications  

### First Railway Line  

[[1875]] marked the inauguration of the first railway line in Atjeh, a modest track ordered by the [[Departement van Oorlog|Department of War]] linking the roadstead of [[Oelèe Lheuë]] to the principal Dutch military station at [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]].  According to the Encyclopaedisch Bureau, this “spoorbaantje” was built “shortly after the beginning of the [[Atjeh‑expeditie (1873)|Atjeh‑expedition]]” and thus predates the systematic construction of the later [[Atjeh‑tram|Atjeh‑tramway]] by more than a decade.

The line served as the seed for the extensive [[Atjeh‑tram|Atjeh‑tramway]] network: in [[1882]] the [[Departement der Burgerlijke Openbare Werken|Department of Civil Public Works (B.O.W.)]] took over management, converting it into a tramway; in [[1885]] a *ceintuur‑trambaan* (belt line) was added along the celebrated [[Postenlinie|postenlinie (line of forts)]] surrounding [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]]; and in [[1891]] control reverted to the [[Departement van Oorlog|War Department]].

### Fort Construction under Meyer  

The forts erected after the [[26 December]] [[1875]] landing formed the backbone of the Dutch permanent presence.  They protected supply lines, facilitated inland patrols, and anchored the later expansion of the railway and tramway systems.

## Biographical Note: Cornelis Jacobus Snijders  

The year [[1875]] also marked the end of Lieutenant‑Colonel [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Tweede Luitenant C. J. Snijders]]’ two‑year detachment in Atjeh.  He had arrived at [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] on [[8 November]] [[1874]] and participated in the [[Benting Kota Alam operation (13 November 1874)|Benting Kota Alam operation]] of [[13 November]] [[1874]].  Throughout [[1875]] the Mineurs en Sappers of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] maintained the sea‑head at [[Oleh‑leh|Oleh‑leh]] and the railway between Oleh‑leh and Kota Radja, despite the heavy disease losses.  Snijders returned to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] in [[1875]] and later served as [[Chef van de Generale Staf|Chef van de Generale Staf]] ([[1910]]–[[1918]]) and [[Opperbevelhebber van Land‑ en Zeemacht|Opperbevelhebber van Land‑ en Zeemacht]] during the [[First World War]].

## Significance  

[[1875]] encapsulates a turning point in the Dutch conquest of Atjeh: administrative centralisation under Van Lansberge, a concerted health response, the first railway imprint on the Acehnese landscape, and—most critically—the launch of Colonel Meyer’s sustained offensive that transformed Dutch tactics from episodic raids to permanent occupation.  The infrastructure projects of 1875 laid the material foundations for the later [[Atjeh‑tram|Atjeh‑tramway]], while the military reforms foreshadowed the intensified phase of the [[Aceh War]] that would dominate the final decades of the nineteenth century.
