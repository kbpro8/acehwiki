**1824** was a pivotal year in Southeast Asian colonial history, marked by the signing of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] on [[7 March]] [[1824]] in London, the issuance of royal decree No. 103 on [[27 May]] [[1824]] concerning Dutch marine personnel, and the launch of the Dutch “*expeditie naar Celebes*” (Celebes Expedition) that spanned [[1824]]‑[[1825]]. The treaty ceded [[Benkoelen]] and [[Padang]] to the [[British Empire]] and recognised Dutch authority over the [[Sultanate of Aceh]], while the decree and expedition reflected the broader scope of Dutch naval operations coordinated from the [[Dutch East Indies]] and affecting the strategic balance on [[Sumatra]], where Aceh remained a potent adversary. Awards for actions in Sumatra, including the [[Palembang]] campaign, were also recorded in the 1824 decree, illustrating the simultaneous engagement of the Dutch Empire in multiple theatres.

## Anglo‑Dutch Treaty and Territorial Adjustments

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] was signed on [[7 March]] [[1824]] between the [[British Empire]] and the [[Dutch East Indies]] (the Dutch colonial administration in the East Indies). Under its terms, the British obtained control of [[Benkoelen]] and [[Padang]], consolidating their presence on the western coast of [[Sumatra]], while the Dutch secured recognition of their sovereignty over the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] and other northern territories. This re‑allocation of spheres of influence shaped the geopolitical landscape of the region for the remainder of the nineteenth century, influencing subsequent diplomatic and military interactions with Aceh.

## Dutch Naval Administration and Decree No. 103

On [[27 May]] [[1824]] the Dutch colonial government issued royal decree No. 103, a detailed order concerning the organisation, pay, and disciplinary regulations of Dutch marine personnel stationed throughout the [[Dutch East Indies]]. The decree, recorded in *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers* (pages 174‑182), also listed the awarding of the [[Militaire Willemsorde|Military Order of William]] to several marines for earlier actions in [[Sumatra]], notably during the [[Palembang]] campaign. These awards underscored the importance the Dutch placed on rewarding military service in the archipelago’s contested zones.

## Celebes Expedition (1824‑1825)

Although geographically distant from Aceh, the Dutch “*expeditie naar Celebes*” commenced in [[1824]] and continued into [[1825]], targeting the island of [[Celebes]] (present‑day Sulawesi). The expedition aimed to suppress local resistance and secure Dutch trade routes in the central Indonesian archipelago. Coordination of the Celebes Expedition from Batavia (the capital of the [[Dutch East Indies]]) demonstrated the logistical capacity of the Dutch naval command, which simultaneously managed operations in Sumatra and the broader East Indies.

## Awards, Campaigns, and the Strategic Context

The 1824 decree’s award listings highlighted Dutch recognition of combat effectiveness in Sumatra, particularly during the [[Palembang]] campaign, a series of engagements aimed at curbing local uprisings and protecting Dutch commercial interests. These recognitions fed into the larger Dutch strategy of consolidating control over the island, a policy that later provided the administrative and military framework for the protracted [[Aceh War]] (1873‑1904).

## Significance

The events of [[1824]] exemplify the Dutch Empire’s capacity to conduct simultaneous diplomatic, administrative, and military actions across a vast maritime domain. The Anglo‑Dutch Treaty re‑defined colonial borders, the royal decree standardised naval governance, and the Celebes Expedition extended Dutch reach into central Indonesia. Together, these developments reinforced the logistical and command structures that would later support the Dutch war effort against the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] during the [[Aceh War]], marking [[1824]] as a cornerstone year in the colonial history of the region.


## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**1824** is the year of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London Treaty of 1824]] between the [[Netherlands]] and [[Great Britain|England]], by which the [[Netherlands]] undertook to respect the independence of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] while simultaneously suppressing piracy and ensuring the safety of commerce and shipping in the surrounding waters. The two commitments — independence and suppression — proved, in the retrospective of *Nederlanders over de Zeeën*, "twee eisen, die moeilijk met elkaar in overeenstemming te brengen waren" (two demands that were difficult to reconcile with each other). The year [[1824]] is the diplomatic seed of the [[Atjehoorlog|Atjehoorlog]] that began forty-nine years later.

### The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London Treaty of 1824]] settled, after the Napoleonic interlude, the colonial spheres of the two maritime powers. With respect to [[Acheh|Atjeh]] it was agreed, as the source records, "dat wij zijn onafhankelijkheid moesten erbiedigen, doch tegelijkertijd de zeeroof zouden beteugelen en zorgen voor de veiligheid van de handel" (that we must respect its independence, but at the same time suppress piracy and ensure the safety of commerce). The undertaking was to plague Dutch colonial policy for half a century. The source's verdict is direct: "Hier bleek namelijk voor de zoveelste maal in de koloniale geschiedenis, dat twee rijken niet onafhankelijk naast elkaar kunnen blijven bestaan, tenzij in beide een ongeveer gelijke standaard van beschaving en rechtzekerheid wordt gehandhaafd" (Here it became clear, for the umpteenth time in colonial history, that two realms cannot continue to exist independently side by side, unless in both an approximately equal standard of civilisation and legal certainty is maintained).

### The 1857 contract

The [[1824]] undertaking was given operational form in [[1857]] when, after the dispatch of a warship, a contract was concluded with the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan of Atjeh]] stipulating mutual admission of trade and the suppression of sea-, strand- and people-robbing. The contract was "niet nageleefd" (not honoured) by the Atjehnese side, in the Dutch reading. Dutch, English, American and other ships continued to be plundered.

### The 1871 prelude

The [[1824]] undertaking was finally superseded in [[1871]] when the [[Netherlands]] concluded a new treaty with [[Great Britain|Britain]] in which Dutch possessions on the coast of [[Nieuw Guinea|Guinea]] were ceded to Britain in exchange for a free Dutch hand on [[Sumatra]]. Conquest of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] was "nog niet gedacht" (not yet contemplated); the explicit aim was only the suppression of piracy. The slide from suppression to conquest was, however, already implicit in the [[1871]] treaty — and, behind it, in the impossible double commitment of [[1824]].

### Other events of 1824

The same year [[1824]] saw the founding of the *[[Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij]]* (NHM) by [[King Willem I]] — a body designed to promote Dutch national trade, shipping, shipbuilding, fishing, agriculture, factories and refineries. The NHM was, in the source's reading, seen in the Indies "als een herleefde V.O.C." (as a revived VOC) that would crush and swallow up private trade. The NHM would, in turn, become the principal carrier of government products under the [[Cultuurstelsel|cultuurstelsel]] after [[1830]].

### Significance

[[1824]] is the foundational year of the modern Dutch-Atjehnese relationship. The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London Treaty of 1824]] is the diplomatic instrument by which the [[Netherlands]] simultaneously guaranteed Atjehnese independence and undertook to suppress Atjehnese piracy — the impossible double commitment that, after forty-nine years of accumulating friction, would culminate in the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|Dutch declaration of war of 26 March 1873]].
