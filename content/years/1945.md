**1945** was, in Dr. A. J. Piekaar’s *Atjèh and the War with Japan*, the year of the [[Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945|Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945]], the [[proclamation of the Republic|proclamation of the Republic of Indonesia]] on [[17 August]] [[1945]], and the appointment of [[Teukoe Moehamad Hasan|Teukoe Moehamad Hasan]] as republican Governor of Sumatra. It marked the end of the three‑and‑a‑half‑year [[Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies|Japanese occupation]] of [[Atjèh|Aceh]], the brief re‑establishment of Dutch authority under the [[Amacab|Allied Military Administration Civil Affairs Branch]], and the opening of the Allied landings that triggered a rapid social revolution sweeping away the *oelèëbalang* aristocracy. The events of 1945 also signalled the broader termination of World War II in the archipelago and the commencement of the Indonesian National Revolution, which directly affected Aceh and the surrounding regions.

## Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945

Piekaar records that “when on 14 August, in the Muslim fasting month, unexpectedly the Japanese capitulation came, the preparations for the Allied invasion were in full swing.” The formal surrender is dated to [[15 August]] [[1945]]. In August the English fleet appeared before [[Sabang|Sabang]] and a temporary naval base was established there. The Dutch navy and the Dutch administration, acting as part of the [[Amacab|Amacab]], also entered the area. Thousands of enterprise workers from Atjèh, East Sumatra and [[Tapanoeli|Tapanoeli]] who had performed slave labour on Sabang “were transferred to the mainland.” On the mainland the Allied (English) occupation was limited to the ports of [[Medan|Medan]], [[Padang|Padang]] and [[Palembang|Palembang]]. The plans of the Chief Co Amacab, Governor of Sumatra [[A. I. Spits|A. I. Spits]], “had to remain unused.”

## Allied landing and the Amacab administration

The Allied entry, led by British forces, created a power vacuum that the Amacab filled. Dutch civil administration re‑asserted control in the main towns, yet the limited reach of the occupation left large parts of Aceh under de‑facto local authority. This situation set the stage for the rapid spread of nationalist sentiment and the subsequent social revolution.

## Proclamation of the Republic and the appointment of Teukoe Moehamad Hasan

The proclamation of the Republic on [[17 August]] [[1945]]—and the appointment of an Atjèhnese, Mr. [[Teukoe Moehamad Hasan|Teukoe Moehamad Hasan]], as republican Governor of Sumatra—“gradually changed the situation.” The combination aligned the residency firmly with the Republic and supplied the [[Poesa|Poesa]]–*oelama* party with a political instrument it had lacked. The *oelèëbalangs*, freed from Japanese pressure, immediately sought to restore their former power, rallying around [[Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah|Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah]] of [[Peusangan|Peusangan]], “the symbol of the resistance of the oelèëbalang party.” On [[7 September]] [[1945]]—the *[[hari raja puasa|hari raja puasa]]*—“*oelèëbalangs* from all of Aceh traveled to Koetaradja to offer him their congratulations.”

## Japanese retreat and the Masubuchi‑kikan

The Japanese retreat was as humiliating as the Allied entry had been triumphant. “As triumphant as the entry had been, so shameful was the evacuation. The years of pent‑up hatred of the fanatic population against the new oppressors was unleashed on the retreating troops.” In the final months of occupation, [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]]—released on [[3 July]] [[1945]] from the leadership of the economic affairs department—“took upon himself the task of creating a new 5th column organization, which shortly—in the event of an Allied invasion—would have to carry out its destructive work behind the Allied lines.” [[Said Aboe Bakar|Said Aboe Bakar]], [[Teukoe Panglima Pólém Moehamad Ali|Teukoe Panglima Pólém Moehamad Ali]], [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu'éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu'éh]] and [[Teukoe Moehamad Amin|Teukoe Moehamad Amin]] took leading parts in the new *[[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi‑kikan]]*—“the old F‑organization would resume its task as M‑organization.”

## The 4th session of the Atjèh Council and the Sumatra Council

The 4th session of the [[Atjèh Council|Atjèh Council]] met on [[20 April]] [[1945]]–[[21 April]] [[1945]] “again simultaneously with the semi‑annual *gun‑chó* conference,” and raised the rice‑delivery quota for the harvest year 1945/1946 from 22 000 to 33 000 tons. [[March]] [[1945]] saw the establishment of an advisory body for the whole of Sumatra, the Sumatra Council (*Sumatora‑chú‑sangi‑in*) at [[Bukittinggi|Bukittinggi]], of which [[Teukoe Nja' Arif|Teukoe Nja' Arif]] was appointed deputy chairman. The council, like the rest of the late‑occupation administration, “acquired more and more an unreal character.”

## Symbolic military events: the *vaandel* of the Korps Maréchaussee

According to the Dutch staff history (*Nederlands‑Indië contra Japan*), Lieutenant [[L. C. G. F. Onvlee|Onvlee]] was transferred to [[Bangkok|Bangkok]] in [[November]] [[1945]] where the disposition of the *vaandel* (colours) of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussee]] was settled. The *vaandel* had been temporarily handed over to the Dutch camp commander in [[March]] [[1944]]. On [[10 May]] [[1945]] the *vaandel* (minus its gold tassels) was returned to Onvlee’s possession. The narrative illustrates the chaotic handling of Dutch military symbols during the final months of the Japanese captivity.

## Liberation and transition (Aceh)

The Japanese surrender on [[15 August]] [[1945]] led to a brief re‑establishment of Dutch authority, which was swiftly contested by Indonesian nationalists. Allied forces entered the archipelago, and in Aceh the power vacuum allowed local nationalist leaders to emerge, later joining the broader struggle for Indonesian independence. The end of the *[[Jappentijd (Japanese occupation, 1942-1945)|Jappentijd]]* and the proclamation of independence on [[17 August]] [[1945]] inaugurated the [[1945]]–[[1949]] Indonesian Revolution, during which former members of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Marechaussee]] formed the *Andjing Nica* (5e Bataljon), one of the principal Dutch formations of the revolution. The *[[tangsi]]* culture, previously broken by the Japanese occupation, had largely disappeared by August 1945, and the subsequent conflict was fought by a reconstituted colonial army rather than the old [[K.N.I.L.]].

## Post‑war military reforms

The experience of the war prompted the Dutch to reorganise their amphibious capabilities, culminating in the creation of a modern [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps]] brigade. This development is referenced in contemporary Dutch military literature (see page 415 of *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers van 1665‑1945*).

## Significance

The year 1945 ended the Japanese occupation but did not restore Dutch authority in Aceh. The hesitant Allied re‑occupation gave the [[Poesa|Poesa]]–*oelama* forces time to consolidate the social revolution that, within months, swept away the *oelèëbalang* class throughout the residency and aligned the region firmly with the Indonesian Republic. The capitulation, the proclamation, the appointment of [[Teukoe Moehamad Hasan|Teukoe Moehamad Hasan]], and the chaotic handling of Dutch military symbols together mark, in Piekaar’s reading, the hinge between the Japanese and republican eras, while the broader events of 1945 set the stage for the Indonesian National Revolution and the post‑war restructuring of Dutch military forces.
