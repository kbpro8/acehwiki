**1940** was a pivotal year in the [[Netherlands East Indies]] and, in particular, in [[Acheh]], marked by the German invasion of the Netherlands on [[10 May]] [[1940]], the enactment of the *Regeling op den Staat van Oorlog* (War State Regulation) from the *Indisch Staatsblad* [[1939/582]], a substantial drawdown and re‑organisation of the Dutch garrison in Acheh, the publication of H. van Meurs’ *Literatuur‑Overzicht over het jaar 1939* and the commencement of major mineral exploitation (gold dredging on the [[Geudong Concession]] and peak oil production in [[North Sumatra]]). These developments together signalled the transition of the colonial administration from peacetime governance to wartime defence and foreshadowed the collapse of Dutch authority during the Japanese invasion of [[1942]].

## Garrison strength on [[1 January]] [[1940]]

At the start of the year the Dutch forces stationed in Acheh comprised:

- **Territoriale Staf** at [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]]
- **1ste Garnizoensbataljon** (27 brigades): staff Kotaradja; [[Tapatoean|Tapatoean]] 4, [[Blang Pidië|Blang Pidië]] 4, [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]] 4, [[Tjalang|Tjalang]] 4, [[Lam No|Lam No]] 3, [[Lokop|Lokop]] 4, [[Kotatjane|Kotatjane]] 4.
- **2de Garnizoensbataljon** (30 brigades): staff Kotaradja with 23 brigades; [[Lho'Nga|Lho'Nga]] 3, [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] 4.
- **3de Garnizoensbataljon** (34 brigades): staff Kotaradja; [[Padang Tiji|Padang Tidji]] 3, [[Sigli|Sigli]] 8, [[Meureudoe|Meureudoe]] 3, [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] 4, [[Bireuën|Bireuën]] 4, [[Lho Seumawé|Lho Semawe]] 4, [[Boeloe Blang Ara|Boeloe Blang Ara]] 4, [[Langsa|Langsa]] 4.
- **4de Garnizoensbataljon** (16 brigades): staff Kotaradja with 16 brigades (including two at [[Sabang|Sabang]] for police duties) and the “Mobiele Reserve.”
- **Korps Maréchaussee** (60 brigades): I Divisie (12 brigades at [[Blangkedjeren|Blangkedjeren]] and [[Koeala Bee|Koeala Bee]]); II Divisie (8 at [[Djeuram|Djeuram]] and [[Lam Ie|Lam Ie]]); III Divisie (15 at [[Takengon|Takengon]] and [[Lam Meulo|Lam Meulo]]); IV Divisie (6 at [[Tangsé|Tangsé]] and [[Geumpang|Geumpang]]); V Divisie (12 at [[Lho Soekoen|Lho Soekoen]], [[Paja Bakong|Paja Bakong]] and [[Panton Laboe|Panton Laboe]]); VI Divisie (7 at [[Bakongan|Bakongan]]).

**Total**: 107 brigades of garrison infantry (≈ 2 250 men) plus 60 brigades of Maréchaussee (≈ 1 200 men) = roughly **3 450 men**.

## 1940 drawdown and re‑organisation

The drawdown that began in [[1940]] unfolded in several stages:

- **April [[1940]]**: one company of the 4de Garnizoensbataljon was sent to the Riouw Archipelago; a second company followed in [[May]] [[1940]] to Java. Consequently the 4de Garnizoensbataljon (“Mobiele Reserve”) disappeared from Acheh.
- **May [[1940]]**, shortly after the German invasion of the Netherlands: outposts at [[Bireuën|Bireuën]], [[Langsa|Langsa]] and [[Kotatjane|Kotatjane]] (12 brigades) were evacuated and reassigned to wartime duties outside Acheh. The [[Westkustcolonne|Westkustcolonne]] of nine brigades was assembled from the Tapatoean, Meulaboh and Tjalang detachments; Tapatoean and Meulaboh were dissolved, and Tjalang was reduced by one brigade. The Westkustcolonne was dispatched to the Oostkust van Sumatra, eventually reaching [[Lawe Sigalagala|Lawe Sigalagala]] where a German internee camp was located. Its soldiers never returned, and their families were relocated to Kotaradja.
- A uniform reduction of **two men per brigade** was applied to all remaining Acheh brigades to form an additional company that was also sent to the Oostkust van Sumatra.

Staff records note that these transfers reduced the overall strength of the Acheh garrison by **one quarter**.

## Legal and administrative changes

The *Regeling op den Staat van Oorlog* entered into force on [[10 May]] [[1940]], granting emergency powers to the military hierarchy and shifting authority from civil to military command. Contemporary Dutch military reports state that the regulation “set the legal basis for the deployment of units such as the *Marinebataljon* and the *Legioen Mangko Negoro* in defensive operations across the archipelago, including the Aceh peninsula.” The regulation proved decisive during the rapid Japanese advance in [[March]] [[1942]].

## Military preparations and mobilisation

In response to the loss of the European homeland, the colonial administration issued directives for heightened defence readiness throughout the Indies, including Aceh. Reports in the *De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers* record increased recruitment, training of colonial troops, and the organisation of new reserve companies. Two marine companies of 125 men each were formed at the Marine Barracks Goebeng, creating a mobile reserve capable of rapid deployment across the archipelago.

## Seizure of German vessels at [[Surabaya]]

On [[10 May]] [[1940]] the marine detachment in *Soerabaia* captured three German ships that had entered the harbour, placing them under judicial seizure and averting potential sabotage. This action illustrated the immediate impact of the European war on colonial security and underscored the strategic importance of [[Surabaya]].

## Mineral‑economic developments

### M.A.E.M. Geudong Concession gold operation

Production at the [[Geudong Concession]] began in [[1940]] under the auspices of the [[M.A.E.M.]]. Two bucket dredges (6 ft³ each) with a combined capacity of 270 000 yd³ per month extracted gold and platinum. The deposit consisted of 55–60 % boulders and 40–45 % fine material. Two gold grades were identified: fine gold with higher silver content (easily amalgamated) and coarse gold with lower silver content (more difficult to amalgamate). Platinum output was measured in kilograms per month, and processing employed amalgamation and Wilfley tables.

### North Sumatra oil production

In [[1940]] total oil production in North Sumatra reached **920 755 tons** (each ton = 1 000 kg). The [[B.P.M.]]‑operated fields (Aru Bay, Telaga Said, Bulu Telang, Tamiang, S. Aru Bay) contributed 769 644 tons (83.7 %). The [[N.L.A.M.]]‑operated [[Peureula]] field in the [[Atjeh oil complex]] added 48 219 tons (5.2 %). The [[East Atjeh oil block]] recorded no output in [[1940]] (production would start in [[1941]]), as would the [[Pasé oil field]] and [[Pulu Pandjang]] fields. The Tamiang block alone supplied 709 625 tons (77.1 % of the North Sumatra total), marking the high‑point of the colonial mineral economy in Acheh before the Pacific War.

## Scholarly publication: Van Meurs’ *Literatuur‑Overzicht*

The year also saw the publication of H. van Meurs’ *Literatuur‑Overzicht over het jaar 1939* (issued by [[M. Nijhoff|M. Nijhoff]] in ‘s‑Gravenhage). In his *Voorbericht* Van Meurs asserted that the Institut published the volume “in de overtuiging, dat de bijzondere tijdsomstandigheden niet den minsten ongunstigen invloed mochten uitoefenen op het verschijnen van deze publicatie,” i.e., despite the German occupation that began in May [[1940]], the Dutch colonial scholarship persisted. The survey’s Sumatra chapter listed key 1939 publications on Acheh, including the Damsté edition of the [[Hikajat Eitia Toedjoh]], Jongejans’ *Land en volk van Atjeh vroeger en nu*, Doorman’s notice of the death of [[Toeanangkoe Mohammad Dawat]], and van der Linden’s commemoration of the third centenary of the Atjeh martyrs. The simultaneous military drawdown and scholarly output underscore 1940 as a year of both contraction and cultural resilience.

## Significance

The events of [[1940]] constitute a watershed in Acheh’s colonial history. The *Regeling op den Staat van Oorlog* provided the legal framework for the rapid militarisation of the archipelago, while the drawdown of the Dutch garrison and the formation of the Westkustcolonne stripped Acheh of much of its defensive capability. At the same time, the start of large‑scale gold dredging on the Geudong Concession and the peak oil production of 1940 represent the zenith of the colonial mineral economy, a prosperity that would be abruptly terminated by the Japanese invasion of [[1942]]. The publication of Van Meurs’ *Literatuur‑Overzicht* marks the last major Dutch scholarly effort on Acheh before the occupation of the Netherlands and the subsequent collapse of the colonial state. Together, these military, economic, and intellectual developments illustrate how 1940 was the *terminus a quo* of Dutch pre‑invasion authority in Acheh and a prelude to the profound transformations that the Pacific War would bring.
