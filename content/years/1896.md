**1896** was a year in which several notable events, material objects, and personal incidents intersected within the context of the [[Aceh War]] and Dutch colonial administration in [[Aceh]]. The year is marked by the revocation of the Dutch‑honour gold chain from the coastal [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], the ongoing stalemate between Dutch forces and Acehnese resistance, and the brief appearance of a local figure, [[Toekoe Oemar]], in a Dutch marine chronicle. These episodes together illustrate the fragility of colonial alliances, the material culture of Dutch‑Acehnese interaction, and the limited archival record of certain military operations.

## Events

- **Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours (1896)** — The Netherlands Indies government confiscated the gold chain bearing the Dutch coat of arms that had been granted to [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] of the [[VII Moekims]], in [[1888]]. The official Dutch description reads: *"hem in 1896 wegens ontrouw ontnomen"* — "taken from him in 1896 on account of disloyalty." The chain subsequently entered the collection of the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]], catalogued in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh), item 31.

## People

- [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]] — [[Uleebalang|Uleebalang]] of the [[VII Moekims]]; had his colonial honour revoked and the gold chain confiscated in [[1896]] for alleged disloyalty.
- [[J.B. van Heutsz|J.B. van Heutsz]] — Dutch military commander who would, from [[1898]], launch the decisive "short war" strategy in Atjeh; in [[1896]] the colonial position remained one of attrition and uncertain alliances with the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class.
- [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] — Dutch Arabist and advisor on Acehnese affairs; his analysis of Acehnese society—distinguishing the secular [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class from the religious leadership—was increasingly influential in shaping colonial policy by the mid‑1890s.
- [[Toekoe Oemar]] — Acehnese leader who defected from the Dutch on [[29 March]] [[1896]], triggering the critical days that preceded Van Heutsz's period of glory.
- [[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri]] — Religious resistance leader; his eldest son and successor was killed as commandant at the [[Battle of Aneu Galong (1896)]].
- [[Tgk. Mat Amin]] — Father of future resistance leader [[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]; fell as a *geloofsgetuige* (witness of the faith) at the Battle of Aneu Galong in [[1896]].
- [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] — Pretender-sultan compelled in [[1896]] to break up his "court" at [[Keumala]] and lead a wandering life owing to a quarrel with local chiefs.
- [[Dominee Thenu]] — Dutch chaplain who arrived in Aceh in [[1894]] and spoke to the troops before battle for the first time in [[1896]].

## Places

- [[VII Moekims]] — Territory whose [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] was declared disloyal; the confiscation of the chain signalled a rupture between the colonial state and this coastal district.
- [[Koeta Radja|Kutaraja]] — Dutch headquarters; the seat of the military and civil administration that ordered and executed the revocation.
- [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavia]] — Destination of the confiscated chain; the Batavian Society's treasure room became the repository for objects divested from their original Acehnese owners through military or political action.
- [[Keumala]] — The pretender-sultan Tuanku Muhamat Dawot's seat, abandoned in [[1896]] after a quarrel with local chiefs.

## Concepts

- [[Uleebalang]] — The class of territorial aristocrats whose shifting allegiances defined the politics of the middle and late phases of the [[Aceh War]].
- [[Postenlinie]] — The line of forts around [[Kota Radja]] abandoned after Toekoe Oemar's betrayal in [[1896]].

## Account from *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.* (1954) — 1896

**[[1896]]** was the year of the [[Battle of Aneu Galong (1896)|Battle of Aneu Galong]] — the defence in which the eldest son and successor of *[[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri]]* was killed as commandant, and in which *[[Tgk. Mat Amin]]* — father of the future resistance leader *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* — also fell as a *geloofsgetuige* (a witness of the faith). The boy *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* was an infant at the breast when his father died; by [[1911]] he would be fifteen and the last leader of the Tiriō bende. [[Dominee Thenu]] — who had arrived in Aceh in [[1894]] — spoke the troops before battle for the first time in [[1896]]. The year marks the moment at which "het lijdelijk afwachten was afgelopen en de troepen zelf tot actie overgingen" (the passive waiting was over and the troops themselves moved to action).

## Account from *Oost-Indisch Journael* (1947) — 1896

**[[1896]]** was the year of the defection of the cunning adventurer [[Teukoe Oemar]] on [[29 March]] [[1896]] — the critical days that followed the Graafland period and preceded Van Heutsz's period of glory. After the defection of [[Teukoe Oemar]] on [[29 March]] [[1896]], critical days began. The defection is the marker of the crisis that opened the way to Van Heutsz's period of glory: the sequence of the Graafland period (the all-out offensive) — the defection of Teukoe Oemar (the critical days) — Van Heutsz's period of glory (the time of the great expeditions and excursions). About [[1896]] the pretender-sultan [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] was also compelled, owing to a quarrel with local chiefs, to break up his "court" at [[Keumala]] and lead a wandering life.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — 1896

**[[1896]]** was the year of the [[Toekoe Oemar's verraad (1896)|verraad (betrayal) of Toekoe Oemar]] — the dramatic turn that, according to the Encyclopaedisch Bureau, "inaugurated a new direction in our policy in Atjeh" which would lead to "the complete pacification of that territory." The Bureau's account frames the betrayal as a defining caesura in the history of the Dutch East Indies' outer possessions: together with the [[1894]] pacification of [[Lombok]] and the [[1902]] turn toward active policy in [[Djambi]] and [[Koerintji]], it is one of the three milestone moments by which the Bureau periodises the Dutch "ommekeer" (about-turn) in policy toward the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]] in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

The immediate strategic consequence of Toekoe Oemar's betrayal was the abandonment of the [[Postenlinie|postenlinie]] — the famous line of forts around [[Kota Radja]] that had defined the geography of the Dutch position in Atjeh since the [[1870s]]. With the postenlinie abandoned, the [[Ceintuur-trambaan|belt-tramway]] built along it in [[1885]] lost its rationale and was "gradually cleared" (*geleidelijk opgeruimd*) in the following years. Its replacement was the long tramway that, beginning in [[1897]], followed the Dutch extension of authority into the [[Atjeh's Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh Dependencies]] and bound successive military posts to the command centre at [[Kota Radja]]. The year [[1896]] thus marks both the end of the defensive *postenlinie* era and the beginning of the era of aggressive pacification.

The new direction in Atjeh policy inaugurated in [[1896]] is also identified by the Bureau as the deeper origin of the wider Buitenbezittingen turn that, after [[1902]], would extend to [[Djambi]], [[Koerintji]], and other parts of the archipelago.

## Account from Keuchenius's 1905 letter — the transfer of Van Heutsz and the Atjeh-opstellen

**[[1896]]** is also significant as the year in which [[Keuchenius|W. H. J. Keuchenius]] published his *[[Atjeh-opstellen]]* and Lieutenant-Colonel [[J. B. van Heutsz]] was transferred to Aceh to lead a decisive campaign for its [[Submission of Aceh|submission]]. In April 1896, Keuchenius was writing public commentary on the colonial situation in Aceh; at the same time, the transfer of Van Heutsz to Aceh occurred. Keuchenius interpreted this as the beginning of the end for the long-running conflict, viewing it as a "fresh awakening in a clear morning" and the only remaining path to victory through "[[Kracht van wapenen|kracht van wapenen]]" (force of arms). This year, therefore, marks a turning point in the Aceh War in Keuchenius's retrospective framing, setting the stage for Van Heutsz's successful pacification campaign.

## Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours

The confiscation of Baid Setia Maharadja's gold chain in [[1896]] deserves particular attention as a material and political event. The chain — a formal mark of colonial favour granted in [[1888]] — was a finely wrought piece of gold wirework terminating in the Dutch coat of arms. Its presentation had been intended to cement the loyalty of one of the coastal [[VII Moekims|district]]'s most important [[Uleebalang|uleebalangs]]. Its revocation eight years later, on grounds of *ontrouw* (disloyalty), represented not merely a personal disgrace but a public rupture in the relationship between the colonial state and one of its indigenous allies.

The Dutch word *ontrouw* (disloyalty, faithlessness) is unambiguous in its meaning: Baid Setia Maharadja was judged to have betrayed the trust placed in him. Whether he had actively joined the resistance, maintained secret contacts with guerrilla leaders, or simply failed to provide the degree of cooperation the Dutch demanded is not specified in the surviving sources. What is clear is that the episode reflected the broader instability of Dutch‑Acehnese alliances in this period. Uleebalangs who accepted Dutch honours and subsidies frequently found themselves caught between the demands of their colonial patrons and the expectations of their own communities, where collaboration with the Dutch could be seen as betrayal of the Islamic cause.

After confiscation, the chain entered the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]]'s collection, where it was displayed in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh) as a specimen of goldwork — albeit one with a distinctly colonial backstory. Its catalogue entry in the 1928 guide preserves both the date of the original grant ([[1888]]) and the date and reason for its revocation ([[1896]], *ontrouw*), making it a valuable historical document in its own right and a material reminder of the alternating pattern of co‑optation and resistance that characterised the later phases of the [[Aceh War]].

## Significance

[[1896]] is one of the principal turning points in the history of Dutch colonial rule in Atjeh. It fell in the interval between the failed strategies of the 1870s and 1880s — the coastal expeditions, the fort‑building, the unsuccessful attempts at negotiation — and the more aggressive and ultimately effective approach adopted under [[J.B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] from [[1898]]. The Bureau's periodisation — Lombok [[1894]], Toekoe Oemar [[1896]], Djambi/Koerintji [[1902]] — places the betrayal of Toekoe Oemar at the centre of the wider strategic reorientation that opened the era of Dutch "effective authority" across the Buitenbezittingen in the early twentieth century.

The revocation of [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja|Baid Setia Maharadja]]'s honours provides a concrete case study of the alliance instability that characterised this phase of the war. The granting of the chain in [[1888]] and its confiscation in [[1896]] encapsulated, in a single object's biography, the Dutch experience in Atjeh: initial optimism about co‑opting the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class, followed by disillusionment as alliance after alliance proved transient, and ultimately the material residue of these failed relationships accumulating in colonial museum collections.
