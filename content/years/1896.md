**1896** was a year in which several notable events, material objects, and personal incidents intersected within the context of the [[Aceh War]] and Dutch colonial administration in [[Aceh]]. The year is marked by the revocation of the Dutch‑honour gold chain from the coastal [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], the ongoing stalemate between Dutch forces and Acehnese resistance, and the brief appearance of a local figure, [[Toekoe Oemar]], in a Dutch marine chronicle. These episodes together illustrate the fragility of colonial alliances, the material culture of Dutch‑Acehnese interaction, and the limited archival record of certain military operations.

**1896** — the year of [[A. Kruisheer|Kruisheer]]'s *ATJEH '96* — was the year in which the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] entered its second great crisis, opened by the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]] from the [[Dutch]] colonial government in the last days of [[March]] [[1896]] and closed, on [[28 June]] [[1896]], by the [[Departure of Vetter and Restoration of Civil and Military Governor (28 June 1896)|departure of Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]] as [[Regeeringscommissaris]] and the restoration of the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur]] under [[J. W. Stemfoort|Kolonel Stemfoort]]. Between those two administrative transitions, the first semester of [[1896]] saw the collapse of the [[buitenlinie]] of posts around [[Koeta Radja]], the [[Relief of Biloel and Lamkoenjit (8 April 1896)|relief]] and [[Evacuation of Biloel and Lamkoenjit (12 April 1896)|evacuation]] of the besieged posts of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]], the [[Battle of Senelop / Lifting of Senelop and Lam Soet (17 April 1896)|bloody lifting of Senelop]] on [[17 April]] [[1896]], the dismissal of [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. Deykerhoff]] and the issue of three letters and a Government Resolution against Umar, the [[Bombardment of Lampisang (27 April – 23 May 1896)|twenty-seven-day bombardment of Lampisang]], the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive of 23–25 May 1896]] against Umar's stelling at [[Lam-Pisang]], the [[Tuchtiging of Lam-Krak Moekims (9–10 June 1896)|punitive expedition against Lam-Krak]] of [[9 June|9]]–[[10 June]] [[1896]], the [[Tuchtiging der V Moekims Montassik (16–17 June 1896)|two-day tuchtiging of the V Moekims Montassik]] of [[16 June|16]]–[[17 June]] [[1896]], and the sea-borne [[Ladoeng Landing and Glé Broeë Attack (22–23 June 1896)|Ladoeng landing and Glé Broeë attack]] of [[22 June|22]]–[[23 June]] [[1896]].

## Events

- **Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours (1896)** — The Netherlands Indies government confiscated the gold chain bearing the Dutch coat of arms that had been granted to [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] of the [[VII Mukims|VII Moekims]], in [[1888]]. The official Dutch description reads: *"hem in 1896 wegens ontrouw ontnomen"* — "taken from him in 1896 on account of disloyalty." The chain subsequently entered the collection of the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]], catalogued in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh), item 31.

## People

- [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]] — [[Uleebalang|Uleebalang]] of the [[VII Mukims|VII Moekims]]; had his colonial honour revoked and the gold chain confiscated in [[1896]] for alleged disloyalty.
- [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]] — Dutch military commander who would, from [[1898]], launch the decisive "short war" strategy in Atjeh; in [[1896]] the colonial position remained one of attrition and uncertain alliances with the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class.
- [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] — Dutch Arabist and advisor on Acehnese affairs; his analysis of Acehnese society—distinguishing the secular [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class from the religious leadership—was increasingly influential in shaping colonial policy by the mid‑1890s.
- [[Toekoe Oemar]] — Local Acehnese figure noted in a Dutch marine chronicle for a marine operation in [[1896]]; the source provides no further details, indicating a need for additional archival research.

## Places

- [[VII Mukims|VII Moekims]] — Territory whose [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] was declared disloyal; the confiscation of the chain signalled a rupture between the colonial state and this coastal district.
- [[Koeta Radja|Kutaraja]] — Dutch headquarters; the seat of the military and civil administration that ordered and executed the revocation.
- [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavia]] — Destination of the confiscated chain; the Batavian Society's treasure room became the repository for objects divested from their original Acehnese owners through military or political action.

## Concepts

- [[Uleebalang]] — The class of territorial aristocrats whose shifting allegiances defined the politics of the middle and late phases of the [[Aceh War]].

## Historical accounts

The year [[1896]] is documented in several Dutch colonial sources, each offering a different perspective on the state of the Aceh conflict at this juncture:

### Oom Ambon (1954)

The popular history *Oom Ambon* (1954) provides a narrative account of the Aceh War aimed at a general Dutch readership. Its treatment of [[1896]] emphasises the frustration and stagnation of the colonial military effort after more than two decades of inconclusive warfare. The revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours is presented as emblematic of the broader Dutch dilemma: alliances with Acehnese elites proved fragile, and the co‑optation strategy yielded only temporary compliance.

### Oost‑Indisch Journael (1947)

*Oost‑Indisch Journael* (1947), a retrospective collection of colonial‑era journalism and commentary, records the growing Dutch public debate about the conduct of the Aceh War in the mid‑1890s. The year [[1896]] fell in the period between the early ineffective coastal campaigns and the later Van Heutsz offensive, and the journal's contributors reflect on the sense of stalemate — the difficulty of securing lasting loyalty from the [[Uleebalang|uleebalangs]], the persistence of the religious resistance, and the mounting cost in Dutch lives and treasury. The Baid Setia Maharadja affair is noted as one instance among many of an alliance that had soured.

### De Buitenbezittingen (1917)

*De Buitenbezittingen* (1917), a colonial reference work published in the final years of Dutch colonial rule, provides an administrative perspective on [[1896]]. Its treatment of the Aceh conflict in this period focuses on the organisational and fiscal pressures generated by a war that showed no sign of resolution. The work notes the periodic revocations of honours and the shifting pattern of alliance and defection among the coastal uleebalangs as evidence that the Dutch had not yet found an effective political strategy for Atjeh.

### Keuchenius (1905)

The account by Keuchenius (1905) — written closer to the events and before the final Dutch victories under Van Heutsz — offers a contemporary political analysis. Keuchenius was critical of the conduct of the Aceh War and argued that the colonial administration had repeatedly squandered opportunities for a negotiated settlement. The revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours in [[1896]] is cited by Keuchenius as an example of the Dutch tendency to rely on punitive measures — confiscation, burning, and public humiliation of cooperative rulers — rather than on building durable political relationships. For Keuchenius, the episode illustrated the fundamental contradiction of Dutch policy: demanding loyalty while offering only the most precarious and conditional recognition of indigenous authority.

## Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours

The confiscation of Baid Setia Maharadja's gold chain in [[1896]] deserves particular attention as a material and political event. The chain — a formal mark of colonial favour granted in [[1888]] — was a finely wrought piece of gold wirework terminating in the Dutch coat of arms. Its presentation had been intended to cement the loyalty of one of the coastal [[VII Mukims|district]]'s most important [[Uleebalang|uleebalangs]]. Its revocation eight years later, on grounds of *ontrouw* (disloyalty), represented not merely a personal disgrace but a public rupture in the relationship between the colonial state and one of its indigenous allies.

The Dutch word *ontrouw* (disloyalty, faithlessness) is unambiguous in its meaning: Baid Setia Maharadja was judged to have betrayed the trust placed in him. Whether he had actively joined the resistance, maintained secret contacts with guerrilla leaders, or simply failed to provide the degree of cooperation the Dutch demanded is not specified in the surviving sources. What is clear is that the episode reflected the broader instability of Dutch‑Acehnese alliances in this period. Uleebalangs who accepted Dutch honours and subsidies frequently found themselves caught between the demands of their colonial patrons and the expectations of their own communities, where collaboration with the Dutch could be seen as betrayal of the Islamic cause.

After confiscation, the chain entered the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]]'s collection, where it was displayed in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh) as a specimen of goldwork — albeit one with a distinctly colonial backstory. Its catalogue entry in the 1928 guide preserves both the date of the original grant ([[1888]]) and the date and reason for its revocation ([[1896]], *ontrouw*), making it a valuable historical document in its own right and a material reminder of the alternating pattern of co‑optation and resistance that characterised the later phases of the [[Aceh War]].

## Significance

The year [[1896]] occupies a pivotal position in the historiography of the [[Aceh War]]. It fell in the interval between the failed strategies of the 1870s and 1880s — the coastal expeditions, the fort‑building, the unsuccessful attempts at negotiation — and the more aggressive and ultimately effective approach adopted under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] from [[1898]]. The four historical accounts cited above, spanning from 1905 to 1954, collectively illustrate how [[1896]] was understood by Dutch writers as a year of stalemate, frustration, and political failure.

The revocation of [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja|Baid Setia Maharadja]]'s honours provides a concrete case study of the alliance instability that characterised this phase of the war. The granting of the chain in [[1888]] and its confiscation in [[1896]] encapsulated, in a single object's biography, the Dutch experience in Atjeh: initial optimism about co‑opting the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class, followed by disillusionment as alliance after alliance proved transient, and ultimately the material residue of these failed relationships accumulating in colonial museum collections. The brief mention of [[Toekoe Oemar]] in a Dutch marine chronicle also hints at lesser‑documented military actions that took place in the same year, underscoring the fragmentary nature of the archival record for certain aspects of the conflict.


## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**1896** is the year in which [[Toekoe Oemar's defection ([[1896]])|Toekoe Oemar turned against the Dutch]], ending the [[Concentratie-stelsel ([[1884]]-[[1896]])|concentratie-stelsel]] of [[1884]]-[[1896]] and ushering in the final, decisive phase of the [[Atjeh-oorlog ([[1873]]-[[1904]])|Atjeh-oorlog]]. The year marks the failure of the policy of co-opting Atjehnese warlords as Dutch allies and the beginning of the [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]-[[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] era of systematic conquest.

### The defection of Toekoe Oemar

[[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar (Teuku Umar)]] had for some years been the principal Atjehnese ally of the Dutch in [[Groot-Atjeh]]. In [[1896]] he defected, turning his weapons and his influence against his former patrons. The *concentratie-stelling* around [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]] was thereby unhinged; even the small "Noordoostpolder"-sized area the Dutch had held since [[1884]] was "niet meer geheel zeker" — no longer wholly secure.

### The parliamentary crisis

In the [[Tweede Kamer]] the crisis of [[1896]] brought the long-simmering criticism of Atjeh policy to a head. [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] (u.l.) declared: "naar mijn oordeel zijn wij er niet op aangekleed zulk een land te veroveren" — in my judgement we are not equipped to conquer such a land — and recalled that the [[Netherlands]] had sat in Makassar for 150 years without being able to move beyond the city limits. He compared the Atjeh war unfavourably with the government's patient toleration of de facto self-rule on [[Celebes]].

[[Abraham Kuyper|Kuyper]] (a.r.) acknowledged that the [[Netherlands]] bore guilt for unleashing the war in [[1873]] but held that this injustice had now prescribed. He summed up the Atjeh policy as a "volledige staalkaart van alle denkbare methoden om Atjeh te bestrijden" — a complete sample-card of every conceivable method of combating Atjeh — and observed sardonically that, by [[1896]], the government had returned to "het reeds eenmaal beproefde stelsel van Van der Heyden" — the already-once-tried system of Van der Heyden — and that "een negende nieuw stelsel heeft men blijkbaar niet kunnen vinden" — a ninth new system apparently could not be found.

### Significance

The defection of [[Toekoe Oemar|Toekoe Oemar]] in [[1896]] is the immediate trigger of the [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]-[[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] conquest of Atjeh. The recovery of [[Groot-Atjeh]] after the defection — the pursuit of the resistance leaders to [[Pedirse|Pedir]] and then, in [[1904]], to the [[Gajoland]] and [[Alasland]] interior — is the military spine of the years [[1896]]-[[1904]].


## Account from *Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis* (1955)

The following account is drawn from the parliamentary history of the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal (colonial business)|Second Chamber]] as narrated by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] in the colonial section of the [[Schets eener parlementaire geschiedenis van Nederland van 1849 tot 1901 (Rengers-Vermeulen, 1955)|1955 fourth revised edition of Rengers's *Schets*]].


**1896** is the year in which [[Teungku Um|Teungku Um]] — who had been armed and subsidised by the Dutch — "wierp het masker af" (threw off the mask) and turned against his former patrons, and in which [[J. B. van Heutsz|overste Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]] was charged with the direction of military operations in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. The [[1896]] defection ended the policy of co-opting Atjehnese warlords as Dutch allies; the [[1896]] Van Heutsz appointment began the post-*concentratie* offensive that would, by [[1904]], bring the sultanate under Dutch control.

### The 1896 defection of Teungku Um

The source records that the governor of Atjeh, [[Deykerhoff|Deykerhoff]], and the governor-general [[Pijnacker Hordijk|Pijnacker Hordijk]] had, "tegen diens waarschuwing in" (against his warning), placed their trust in [[Teungku Um|Teungku Um]]. He was officially granted clemency, permitted to maintain a legion of 250 men at Dutch expense, and later received "nog 1000 geweren, veel ammunitie en geld" (a further 1,000 rifles, much ammunition and money). "Korte tijd daarna (1896) wierp Teungku Um het masker af en hadden onze soldaten te vechten tegen met modern geweren bewapende Atjehers" (A short time later (1896) Teungku Um threw off the mask, and our soldiers had to fight against Atjehnese armed with modern rifles).

### The Van Heutsz appointment

The governor-general [[Van der Wijck|Van der Wijck]] (1893-99) "begreep, dat Snouck Hurgronje's adviezen gevolgd moesten worden" (understood that Snouck Hurgronje's advice had to be followed). [[J. B. van Heutsz|Overste Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz]] "had dit begrepen" (had understood this) too. "In 1896 werd hij belast met de leiding der militaire operaties in Groot-Atjeh" (In 1896 he was charged with the direction of military operations in Groot-Atjeh).

### The mobile-column strategy

The [[1896]] Van Heutsz appointment brought with it a new strategy: "Met mobiele kolonnes werd dit gebied onafgebroken doorkruist en de vijand geen rust gelaten" (With mobile columns this area was traversed uninterruptedly and the enemy was given no rest). The strategy is the source's first mention of the *Van der Heyden-Van Heutsz system* of pursuit into the remotest hiding-places that would, after [[1896]], become the standard Dutch method.

### Significance

[[1896]] is the political-military hinge of the Atjehnese war. The [[Teungku Um|Teungku Um]] defection ended the policy of co-opting Atjehnese warlords as Dutch allies; the [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] appointment began the post-*concentratie* offensive that would conclude the war. The two events together — defection in the spring, appointment in the same year — define the [[1896]] turning-point.

## Account from *Atjeh* by H.C. Zentgraff (1938)

[[H.C. Zentgraff|Zentgraff]] gives [[1896]] its fullest treatment as the pivot-year of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. In [[February]] [[1896]] [[Teukoe Oemar]] defected — *den moreelen kaakslag* that broke the *geconcentreerde linie* policy. By [[8 May]] [[1896]] 5000 Dutch troops lay at [[Koetaradja]] and Oeleëlheuë *en men wist eigenlijk niet precies wat men er mee moest doen*. The [[Fall of Aneukgalong (1896)|overval of Aneukgalong]] on [[28 March]] [[1896]] — at half past midnight from Lambaroe, under *overste* [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] with [[G.C.E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] as *chef-staf* — left 110 dead including [[Tengkoe Mat Amin di Tiro|Tgk. Mat Amin di Tiro]], whose body was carried to kampong Moereuë. On [[2 May]] [[1896]] the *bentengs* Senelop and Lamsoet were retaken and immediately re-abandoned — *een bittere pil*. Van Daalen's pencil-notes to his wife (29 April, 3 May, 8 May 1896) record the confusion: *het Staf heeft de beschikking over meer dan 5000 man [...] liet [ze] eene maand zonder brood en vleesch*.
