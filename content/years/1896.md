Figures, places, and events in Acehnese history associated with the year [[1896]].

## Events

- **Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours (1896)** — The Netherlands Indies government confiscated the gold chain bearing the Dutch coat of arms that had been granted to [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] of the [[VII Moekims]], in [[1888]]. The stated reason was disloyalty (*ontrouw*). The original Dutch description reads: *"hem in 1896 wegens ontrouw ontnomen"* — "taken from him in 1896 on account of disloyalty." The chain subsequently entered the collection of the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]], where it was catalogued in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh), item 31. See [[Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours (1896)]].

## People

- [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]] — [[Uleebalang|Uleebalang]] of the [[VII Moekims]]; had his colonial honour revoked and the gold chain confiscated in this year for alleged disloyalty.
- [[J.B. van Heutsz|J.B. van Heutsz]] — Dutch military commander who would, from 1898, launch the decisive "short war" strategy in Atjeh; in 1896, the colonial position remained one of attrition and uncertain alliances with the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class.
- [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|C. Snouck Hurgronje]] — Dutch Arabist and advisor on Acehnese affairs; his analysis of Acehnese society — distinguishing the secular [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class from the religious leadership — was increasingly influential in shaping colonial policy by the mid-1890s.

## Places

- [[VII Moekims]] — Territory whose [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] was declared disloyal; the confiscation of the chain signalled a rupture between the colonial state and this coastal district.
- [[Koeta Radja|Kutaraja]] — Dutch headquarters; the seat of the military and civil administration that ordered and executed the revocation.
- [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavia]] — Destination of the confiscated chain; the Batavian Society's treasure room became the repository for objects divested from their original Acehnese owners through military or political action.

## Concepts

- [[Uleebalang]] — The class of territorial aristocrats whose shifting allegiances defined the politics of the middle and late phases of the [[Aceh War]].

## Historical accounts

The year 1896 is documented in several Dutch colonial sources, each offering a different perspective on the state of the Aceh conflict at this juncture:

### Oom Ambon (1954)

The popular history *Oom Ambon* (1954) provides a narrative account of the Aceh War aimed at a general Dutch readership. Its treatment of 1896 emphasises the frustration and stagnation of the colonial military effort after more than two decades of inconclusive warfare. The revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours is presented as emblematic of the broader Dutch dilemma: alliances with Acehnese elites proved fragile, and the co-optation strategy yielded only temporary compliance.

### Oost-Indisch Journael (1947)

*Oost-Indisch Journael* (1947), a retrospective collection of colonial-era journalism and commentary, records the growing Dutch public debate about the conduct of the Aceh War in the mid-1890s. The year 1896 fell in the period between the early ineffective coastal campaigns and the later Van Heutsz offensive, and the journal's contributors reflect on the sense of stalemate — the difficulty of securing lasting loyalty from the [[Uleebalang|uleebalangs]], the persistence of the religious resistance, and the mounting cost in Dutch lives and treasury. The Baid Setia Maharadja affair is noted as one instance among many of an alliance that had soured.

### De Buitenbezittingen (1917)

*De Buitenbezittingen* (1917), a colonial reference work published in the final years of Dutch colonial rule, provides an administrative perspective on 1896. Its treatment of the Aceh conflict in this period focuses on the organisational and fiscal pressures generated by a war that showed no sign of resolution. The work notes the periodic revocations of honours and the shifting pattern of alliance and defection among the coastal uleebalangs as evidence that the Dutch had not yet found an effective political strategy for Atjeh.

### Keuchenius (1905)

The account by Keuchenius (1905) — written closer to the events and before the final Dutch victories under Van Heutsz — offers a contemporary political analysis. Keuchenius was critical of the conduct of the Aceh War and argued that the colonial administration had repeatedly squandered opportunities for a negotiated settlement. The revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours in 1896 is cited by Keuchenius as an example of the Dutch tendency to rely on punitive measures — confiscation, burning, and the public humiliation of cooperative rulers — rather than on building durable political relationships. For Keuchenius, the episode illustrated the fundamental contradiction of Dutch policy: demanding loyalty while offering only the most precarious and conditional recognition of indigenous authority.

## Revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours

The confiscation of Baid Setia Maharadja's gold chain in 1896 deserves particular attention as a material and political event. The chain — a formal mark of colonial favour granted in [[1888]] — was a finely wrought piece of gold wirework terminating in the Dutch coat of arms. Its presentation had been intended to cement the loyalty of one of the coastal [[VII Moekims|district]]'s most important [[Uleebalang|uleebalangs]]. Its revocation just eight years later, on grounds of *ontrouw* (disloyalty), represented not merely a personal disgrace but a public rupture in the relationship between the colonial state and one of its indigenous allies.

The Dutch word *ontrouw* (disloyalty, faithlessness) is unambiguous in its meaning: Baid Setia Maharadja was judged to have betrayed the trust placed in him. Whether he had actively joined the resistance, maintained secret contacts with guerrilla leaders, or simply failed to provide the degree of cooperation the Dutch demanded is not specified in the surviving sources. What is clear is that the episode reflected the broader instability of Dutch-Acehnese alliances in this period. Uleebalangs who accepted Dutch honours and subsidies frequently found themselves caught between the demands of their colonial patrons and the expectations of their own communities, where collaboration with the Dutch could be seen as betrayal of the Islamic cause.

After confiscation, the chain entered the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society's]] collection, where it was displayed in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh) as a specimen of goldwork — albeit one with a distinctly colonial backstory. Its catalogue entry in the 1928 guide preserves both the date of the original grant (1888) and the date and reason for its revocation (1896, *ontrouw*), making it a valuable historical document in its own right and a material reminder of the alternating pattern of co-optation and resistance that characterised the later phases of the [[Aceh War]].

## Significance

The year 1896 occupies a pivotal position in the historiography of the [[Aceh War]]. It fell in the interval between the failed strategies of the 1870s and 1880s — the coastal expeditions, the fort-building, the unsuccessful attempts at negotiation — and the more aggressive and ultimately effective approach adopted under [[J.B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] from 1898. The four historical accounts cited above, spanning from 1905 to 1954, collectively illustrate how 1896 was understood by Dutch writers as a year of stalemate, frustration, and political failure.

The revocation of [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja|Baid Setia Maharadja]]'s honours provides a concrete case study of the alliance instability that characterised this phase of the war. The granting of the chain in [[1888]] and its confiscation in 1896 encapsulated, in a single object's biography, the Dutch experience in Atjeh: initial optimism about co-opting the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] class, followed by disillusionment as alliance after alliance proved transient, and ultimately the material residue of these failed relationships accumulating in the colonial museum collections. The fact that the chain's catalogue entry preserved the full story — the grant, the revocation, and the reason — suggests that even the museum curators recognised its significance as more than a piece of decorative metalwork.
