**The year 1602** was the year of the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding of the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie on 20 March 1602]], of the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]], of the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]] on [[3 October]] [[1602]], and of the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|first Atjeh embassy to the Dutch Republic]] — the embassy that placed Atjeh inside the Dutch war of independence from Spain. The year is the principal institutional and naval year of Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]] and the *terminus ad quem* of the pre-VOC period of the Dutch-Indies trade. In [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s account it is also the year of the founding of [[Fort Zeeland at Atjeh]] — the first Dutch office on Sumatra — and of the death of the senior Atjeh ambassador [[Abdoel Hamid]] at Middelburg.

## The founding of the VOC (20 March 1602)

The full account of the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding]] is given in the entry on the founding itself. In summary: the charter (*octrooi*) of [[20 March]] [[1602]] — printed by Van der Woude as **Bijlage I** — created the VOC as a company with a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan for twenty-one years. The Company's capital was divided among six **kamers** (chambers): Amsterdam (half), Zeeland (a quarter), the Maze, Hoorn and Enkhuizen. The supreme governing body was the **[[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]]** (Lords Seventeen). The charter's most consequential clauses granted the Company the right to build fortifications, to maintain armed forces, to conclude treaties with native rulers, and to wage war in the name of the States-General.

## The James Lancaster visit (June 1602)

The full account of the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|Lancaster visit]] is given in the entry on the visit itself. In summary: [[James Lancaster|Sir James Lancaster]] anchored off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]] with four English ships — the first English East India Company fleet. He was received with great ceremony by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]], praised Queen Elizabeth as "a princesse of great noblenesse, for fame speaketh so much of her", and obtained leave to trade. He found the pepper prices higher than his pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] had led him to expect and his capital insufficient for a full cargo. He conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese carrack from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]] and was joined by [[Joris van Spilbergen|Joris van Spilbergen]]'s Dutch fleet.

## The Joris Spilbergh visit and the joint capture of the *kraak* (September-October 1602)

[[Joris van Spilbergen|Spilbergh]]'s fleet arrived at Atjeh later in [[1602]]. There he found the *Lam* — a yacht from his own fleet that had strayed — already lying at Atjeh, hired by the English Company under [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]], with [[John Davis|John Davis]] under Lancaster's command. In cooperation with the English, Spilbergh took a richly-laden Portuguese *kraak* (merchantman); after unloading the goods, the *kraak* was returned to the Portuguese "met hun vrouwen en kinderen" (with their wives and children) to sail to [[Malakka|Malakka]]. This clemency was a deliberate signal of Dutch-English good faith in Atjeh waters.

At Atjeh the first [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] fleet — sent out by the newly-founded Company — also arrived during Spilbergh's stay. Spilbergh sold one of his ships to the new foundation and reached the fatherland with his well-laden ship.

## The Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave

The Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] sent three ambassadors on the returning Zeeland ships: [[Abdoel Hamid]] (the senior envoy, well over seventy), [[Sri Mohammed]] (the Admiral of Atjeh), and [[Mir Hassan]] (a nobleman). A Luxemburger, [[Leonard Werner]], served as their interpreter. They reached the Republic in [[1602]] and were received at [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]]'s siege camp at Grave — where the envoys toured the siege-works, discharged two cannon-shots at the city in memory of the occasion, and stayed for fifteen months as guests of the East India Company. The aged [[Abdoel Hamid]] died at [[Middelburg]] in August [[1602]] before the embassy could complete its journey to Grave. See [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)]].

## The founding of Fort Zeeland at Atjeh

At Atjeh the Zeelanders received their first Dutch office on Sumatra, baptised "[[Fort Zeeland at Atjeh|Zeeland]]" — where two merchants and several assistants remained behind. Two of them sailed on Atjeh vessels to the Gulf of Cambay, on the west coast of Voor-Indië, where — thanks to the letters of the Atjeh Sultan — they were excellently received in [[Goezeratte|Goezeratte]] and could begin their trade. The Sultan's letters thus opened the Gujarat trade to Dutch merchants.

## The Zeeland lodge fire (13 January 1602)

Earlier in the year — on [[13 January]] [[1602]] — a great fire broke out in Atjeh, destroying some 200 houses; the Zeeland lodge "Zeelant" that the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland Company's mission of August 1601]] had founded burned down with the rest, though with little loss of goods. [[Jan Grenier|Jan Grenier]], vice-admiraal of the [[Jacob van Neck|Van Neck]] fleet, who arrived off Atjeh on [[15 December]] [[1601]] and witnessed the fire, sailed on [[19 February]] [[1602]] to [[Tikoe|Tikoe]] — where he could buy no pepper because trade with foreigners in the west-coast ports was forbidden except at Atjeh — and then to [[Bantam|Bantam]].

## The Atjeh ambassadors' sea-battle off St. Helena

The Atjeh ambassadors had, on their voyage to Europe, the privilege of witnessing a sea-battle off [[St. Helena]]. Two Zeeland ships lay at the island when a sail was sighted, presumed to be a Dutch ship. The *Langebercque*, only 150 lasts in size, found itself opposite a heavy Spanish galleon from Goa bound for Lisbon. The Spaniard opened fire. When two Zeeland sailors had fallen, the *Langebercque* "met de volle laag aan" — gave her full broadside — and after three days' fighting the Spaniard surrendered. The rich prize was very welcome in the fatherland.

## Significance

The year [[1602]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative and in Godee Molsbergen's account, the principal institutional and naval year of the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship and the *terminus ad quem* of the pre-VOC period of the Dutch-Indies trade. The [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding of the VOC on 20 March 1602]] was the institutional turning-point; the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|joint Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese carrack of 3 October 1602]] were the principal instances of the early Anglo-Dutch commercial co-operation; and the [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|first Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits]] placed Atjeh literally inside the Dutch war of independence from Spain. The founding of [[Fort Zeeland at Atjeh]] opened the continuous Dutch presence in the sultanate that would persist, through the [[Dutch lodge at Atjeh|lodge]] and the *Opperhoofden*, until the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy of 1641]] and beyond.
