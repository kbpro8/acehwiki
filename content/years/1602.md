**The year 1602** was the year of the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding of the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie on 20 March 1602]] and of the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]] on [[3 October]] [[1602]]. The year is the principal institutional and naval year of Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]] and the *terminus ad quem* of the pre-VOC period of the Dutch-Indies trade.

## The founding of the VOC (20 March 1602)

The full account of the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding]] is given in the entry on the founding itself. In summary: the charter (*octrooi*) of [[20 March]] [[1602]] — printed by Van der Woude as **Bijlage I** — created the VOC as a company with a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan for twenty-one years. The Company's capital was divided among six **kamers** (chambers): Amsterdam (half), Zeeland (a quarter), the Maze, Hoorn and Enkhuizen. The supreme governing body was the **[[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]]** (Lords Seventeen). The charter's most consequential clauses granted the Company the right to build fortifications, to maintain armed forces, to conclude treaties with native rulers, and to wage war in the name of the States-General.

## The James Lancaster visit (June 1602)

The full account of the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|Lancaster visit]] is given in the entry on the visit itself. In summary: [[James Lancaster|Sir James Lancaster]] anchored off [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]] with four English ships — the first English East India Company fleet. He was received with great ceremony by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]], praised Queen Elizabeth as "a princesse of great noblenesse, for fame speaketh so much of her", and obtained leave to trade. He found the pepper prices higher than his pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] had led him to expect and his capital insufficient for a full cargo. He conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese carrack from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]] and was joined by [[Joris Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]]'s Dutch fleet.

## The Joris Spilbergh visit (September 1602)

The full account of the [[Joris Spilbergh's Atjeh visit (1602)|Spilbergh visit]] is given in the entry on the visit itself. In summary: [[Joris Spilbergh|Spilbergh]]'s fleet of three ships and the yacht *'t Lam, outfitted by [[Balthasar de Moucheron|Balthasar de Moucheron]], arrived off Atjeh in early September [[1602]] after a call at [[Ceylon|Ceylon]] on the way. The yacht *'t Lam under [[Cornelis Spex|Cornelis Spex]] had become separated from the others near the [[Comoro Islands|Comoros]] and had arrived at Atjeh in early September to place itself under Lancaster's command. Spilbergh himself arrived a few days later with the other two ships.

## The capture of the Portuguese carrack (3 October 1602)

The full account of the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|capture]] is given in the entry on the capture itself. In summary: the combined Anglo-Dutch squadron of four English ships and two Dutch, having sailed from Atjeh on [[21 September]] [[1602]] and spread themselves across the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]], intercepted, attacked and captured the annual Portuguese carrack from the Koromandel coast bound for Malaka on [[3 October]] [[1602]], taking 950 bales of calicoes and pintados plus rice and other merchandise. The 600 Portuguese crew were released unhindered to Malaka. The capture was the first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies and the closing episode of the first Dutch-Achehnese diplomatic sequence.

## The Zeeland lodge fire (13 January 1602)

Earlier in the year — on [[13 January]] [[1602]] — a great fire broke out in Atjeh, destroying some 200 houses; the Zeeland lodge "Zeelant" that the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland Company's mission of August 1601]] had founded burned down with the rest, though with little loss of goods. [[Jan Grenier|Jan Grenier]], vice-admiraal of the [[Jacob van Neck|Van Neck]] fleet, who arrived off Atjeh on [[15 December]] [[1601]] and witnessed the fire, sailed on [[19 February]] [[1602]] to [[Tikoe|Tikoe]] — where he could buy no pepper because trade with foreigners in the west-coast ports was forbidden except at Atjeh — and then to [[Bantam|Bantam]]. The first Dutch factory on Achehnese soil thus passed out of existence within six months of its founding.

## Significance

The year [[1602]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal institutional and naval year of the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship and the *terminus ad quem* of the pre-VOC period of the Dutch-Indies trade. The [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding of the VOC on 20 March 1602]] was the institutional turning-point of the early Dutch-Indies trade and the proximate cause of the consolidation of the Dutch position at Atjeh. The [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English East India Company visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|joint Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese carrack of 3 October 1602]] were the principal instances of the early Anglo-Dutch commercial co-operation against the Portuguese in the East Indies. The Zeeland lodge fire of [[13 January]] [[1602]] marked the end of the first Dutch factory on Achehnese soil and the beginning of the VOC's preference for operating at Atjeh through visiting fleets rather than a permanent factory. The year [[1602]] thus marks the close of the pre-VOC period and the beginning of the unified Dutch-Indies trade.
