**Koninklijke Vereeniging "Koloniaal Instituut"** — the Royal Association "Colonial Institute" — was the Amsterdam-based Dutch colonial-ethnographic institute that, on the title-page of Prof. [[T. J. Bezemer|T. J. Bezemer]]'s [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|1933 plate-atlas *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid*]], is named as the institute under whose *medewerking* (co-operation) the atlas was published. The institute was the principal Dutch centre for the scholarly and popular-scholarly study of the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]] in the late colonial period; its ethnographic division — the *Afd. Volkenkunde* (Ethnography Department) — supplied the largest single group of objects illustrated in the 1933 atlas, including the [[Atjeh weefgetouw (Bezemer plate 62)|Atjeh loom]] (plate 62), the [[Atjeh imported silk warp ikat (Bezemer plate 65)|Atjeh imported-silk warp-ikat]] (plate 65), and the [[Atjeh silk ikat warp strokes (Bezemer plate 66)|Atjeh silk ikat with warp strokes]] (plate 66), all of which are listed in the atlas's *Verzameling* (Collection) appendix as held by the *Kol. Instituut, Afd. Volkenkunde, Amsterdam*.

## Foundation and character

The Koloniaal Instituut was established in Amsterdam as a private association — *Vereeniging* — with royal patronage (*Koninklijke*), to serve as the metropolitan centre of Dutch colonial scholarship on the Indies. Its foundation belonged to the same early-twentieth-century movement that produced the Dutch "Ethical Policy" (*Ethische Politiek*) of state-led development for the Indies: the institute was intended to consolidate, in the metropolis, the scholarly and commercial knowledge of the colonies that the Dutch colonial state needed for the administration and economic exploitation of the archipelago. Its Amsterdam location — rather than The Hague, the seat of the colonial ministry — reflected its character as a semi-civilian, semi-scholarly institution rather than a government department; its royal patronage placed it in the same register as the other Dutch royal institutes (*Koninklijk Instituut*) of the period.

The institute housed several departments — *Afdelingen* — of which the *Afd. Volkenkunde* (Ethnography Department) was the most important for the documentation of Atjeh craft. The *Afd. Volkenkunde* built up, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, a substantial collection of Indonesian ethnographic objects — including Atjeh looms, Atjeh silk textiles, Atjeh sirih-ware, and Atjeh brassware — that formed the principal Dutch museum collection of Atjeh craft objects in the late colonial period.

## Role in the publication of the 1933 atlas

The Koloniaal Instituut's role in the publication of the [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|Bezemer atlas of 1933]] is recorded on the title-page as one of *medewerking* — co-operation. The phrase *UITGEGEVEN MET MEDEWERKING DER KONINKLIJKE VEREENIGING "KOLONIAAL INSTITUUT", AMSTERDAM* — "Published with the co-operation of the Royal Association 'Colonial Institute', Amsterdam" — indicates that the institute supplied the institutional and collections resources for the atlas, while the actual publication was handled by the [[N.V. tot Exploitatie van het Maandblad "Nederl-Indië, Oud en Nieuw"|N.V. tot Exploitatie van het Maandblad "Nederl-Indië, Oud en Nieuw"]]. The division of labour was typical of late-colonial Dutch scholarly publishing: a specialist institute (the Koloniaal Instituut) supplied the scholarly content and the collections, while a commercial publisher (the N.V. tot Exploitatie) handled the printing and distribution.

The Koloniaal Instituut's *Afd. Volkenkunde* supplied, from its own collections, the largest single group of objects illustrated in the atlas. The *Verzameling / Collection / Sammlung* appendix to the atlas (pp. 169–170) lists the lenders of the photographs and drawings, and the Koloniaal Instituut's *Afd. Volkenkunde, Amsterdam* appears more frequently than any other single collection: it is credited with plates 13, 17–18, 19, 20–21, 24–28, 29–35, 37–41, 42–45, 47–50, 51, 58, 60–61, 63–68, 69–71, 75–77, 80–86, 87, 113, 121, 124, 125, 127, 134–135, 137–139, 162, 167, 171, 175–177, 178–179, 180, 181, 182ed-e, 183, 187–189, 195–196, 200–207, 210–213, 215, 220–221, 222–223, 230–231, 234–237, 241. Among these, the [[Atjeh weefgetouw (Bezemer plate 62)|Atjeh loom (plate 62)]], the [[Atjeh imported silk warp ikat (Bezemer plate 65)|Atjeh imported-silk warp-ikat (plate 65)]], and the [[Atjeh silk ikat warp strokes (Bezemer plate 66)|Atjeh silk ikat with warp strokes (plate 66)]] are all listed under the Koloniaal Instituut's holdings — making the institute the single most important Dutch repository of Atjeh craft objects in 1933.

## The *Afd. Volkenkunde* and Atjeh craft

The *Afd. Volkenkunde* of the Koloniaal Instituut was, by 1933, the principal Dutch ethnographic repository of Atjeh craft objects. The Atjeh holdings of the institute — documented in the Bezemer atlas as plates 62, 65, and 66, and probably also contributing to the [[Noord-Sumatra standing brass lamps (Bezemer plate 174)|Noord-Sumatra standing brass lamps (plate 174)]] — represented the accumulated product of half a century of Dutch collecting in Atjeh, beginning with the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] period of [[1873]]–[[1904|1904]] and continuing through the pacification and civil administration of the residency. The Atjeh loom (plate 62), in particular, is the kind of large-scale craft object that colonial officials and military officers acquired as a result of the conquest and administration of the territory; the Atjeh silk textiles (plates 65, 66) are the kind of portable craft objects that were regularly collected by Dutch officials as specimens of indigenous industry.

The Koloniaal Instituut's Atjeh holdings were, in 1933, at their fullest: the institute had been accumulating objects since the [[1870s|1870s]]–[[1880s|1880s]], and the late colonial period saw both continued collecting and the systematic cataloguing and publication of the collection. The Bezemer atlas is, in this sense, a published snapshot of the institute's Atjeh holdings at the moment of their fullest late-colonial documentation. After [[1942|1942]] — the Japanese invasion of the Indies — and [[1945|1945]] — the Indonesian declaration of independence — the flow of new objects from Atjeh to Amsterdam ceased, and the institute's Atjeh holdings became, in effect, a closed historical collection: the Atjeh craft objects that Bezemer photographed in 1933 are, in many cases, the same objects that survive in the institute's successor collections today.

## Significance for Atjeh

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the Koloniaal Instituut's significance is principally as the institutional locus of the Dutch colonial documentation of Atjeh craft. The institute's *Afd. Volkenkunde* collected, catalogued, and published the largest single Dutch museum collection of Atjeh craft objects; its *medewerking* made the Bezemer atlas of [[1933]] possible; and its institutional continuity — through the Japanese occupation, the Indonesian revolution, and the post-[[1949|1949]] reorganisation of Dutch colonial-era institutions — preserved the Atjeh collection into the post-colonial period. Any AcehWiki entry on an Atjeh craft object that draws on the Bezemer atlas is, in effect, drawing on the Koloniaal Instituut's documentation of its own collection.
