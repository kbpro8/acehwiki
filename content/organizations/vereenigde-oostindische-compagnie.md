**The Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC)** — the **Dutch East India Company**, chartered by the [[Staten-Generaal|Staten-Generaal]] of the United Provinces on [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|20 March 1602]] for a period of twenty-one years (renewable in ten-year accounting periods), with a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan — was the principal Dutch commercial and political instrument in the East Indies for two centuries and the institutional framework within which the Dutch-Atjeh relationship unfolded from [[1602]] to the Company's dissolution in [[1799]]. The VOC is the central institution of Van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The charter of 20 March 1602

The full account of the founding is given in the entry on the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding itself]]. In summary: the charter (*octrooi*) of [[20 March]] [[1602]] — printed by Van der Woude as **Bijlage I** — created the VOC as a company with a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan for twenty-one years. The Company's capital was divided among six **kamers** (chambers): Amsterdam (half), Zeeland (a quarter), the Maze (Rotterdam and Delft), Hoorn and Enkhuizen (each an eighth). The supreme governing body was the **[[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]]** (Lords Seventeen): eight from Amsterdam, four from Zeeland, two from the Maze, two from the Noorderkwartier, and a seventeenth member chosen in rotation by the smaller chambers. The charter's most consequential clauses granted the Company the right to build fortifications, to maintain armed forces, to conclude treaties with native rulers, and to wage war in the name of the States-General.

## The early VOC at Atjeh

The early VOC inherited the diplomatic framework that the voorcompagnieën had established at [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. The [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|Atjeh embassy of 1601]] had produced a treaty of friendship and commerce between the sultan and the States-General; the VOC inherited this treaty and continued to operate under its terms. The Company's factors at Atjeh continued to buy pepper, though in declining quantities as the trade shifted to the west coast of Sumatra and to [[Bantam|Bantam]]. The [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s death in [[1604]] and the eventual succession of his son preserved the same balancing policy.

The first VOC fleet to arrive at Atjeh after the charter was that of [[Cornelis Matelieff|Cornelis Matelieff]] in [[1606]] on his way to [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Malaka]] — by which time the Atjeh trade had lost most of its significance for the Dutch. The Zeeland lodge "Zeelant" that the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland Company's mission of 1601]] had founded had burned down on [[13 January]] [[1602]] — three months before the charter — and was not rebuilt; the VOC preferred to operate at Atjeh through visiting fleets rather than a permanent factory.

## The monopoly-by-force policy (1608-1614)

The monopoly-by-force policy that would define the VOC's conduct at [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] and elsewhere was inaugurated by the secret instruction of [[11 April]] [[1608]] — "de eylanden van Banda op 't allerhoochste gerecommandeert" — and the further instruction of [[4 May]] [[1609]]. The instructions, dispatched after the [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet had already left Texel, committed the Company to the new policy: the [[Twelve Years' Truce|Twelve Years' Truce]] with Spain had just been signed; the Dutch were to use the truce to entrench the Company's position before Spain could regain its strength. The [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet's [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Malaka expedition of 1608]] and its [[Murder of Verhoeff at Banda (May 1609)|Banda expedition of 1609]] — in which Verhoeff was murdered with forty-six of his men on [[22 May]] [[1609]] — were the first operations under the new policy.

The [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] *Discours* of [[1 January]] [[1614]] completed the policy framework. Coen's argument — "Van Banda connen ons meester maken, dezelvige eylanden met macht aantasten; sulcx moeten nootelijcken doen ende de plaetse populeren, ofte het landt verlaten, want 't en sal anders met Banda nyet zijn" — was the working-out of the bewindhebbers' instructions of [[1608]]-[[1609]] and the foundation of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force that would define VOC policy for the next two centuries.

## The institutional framework

The VOC's institutional framework — the [[Heeren Zeventien|Heeren Zeventien]] in Amsterdam, the Governor-General in the Indies, the system of factories (factorijen) and forts, the dual bookkeeping (the *grootboek* and the *rekeningen-courant* of the Indies versus the *retouren-generaal* and *equipage-generaal* of patria) — was the institutional framework within which the Dutch-Atjeh relationship and the broader Dutch-Indies trade would unfold. Van der Woude treats the dual bookkeeping as the principal institutional failure of the VOC: "de gescheiden administratie symboliseert de tegenstelling tusschen de inzichten in Holland en de consequenties daarvan in Indië", and the failure to maintain a unified general-trade account would, in Van der Woude's analysis, contribute to the Company's "smadelijke ondergang" at the end of the eighteenth century.

## Significance

The VOC is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the central institution of the early Dutch-Indies trade and the institutional framework within which the Dutch-Atjeh relationship unfolded from [[1602]] to the Company's dissolution in [[1799]]. The charter of [[20 March]] [[1602]] — with its grant of quasi-sovereign powers (fortification, war, treaty-making) — was the legal foundation of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force that would define VOC policy from [[1609]] onward. The early VOC inherited the diplomatic framework of the voorcompagnieën at Atjeh — the [[Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits (1601)|treaty of 1601]], the lodge "Zeelant", the practice of buying pepper from visiting fleets — and continued to operate within it for the first decade of its existence, while the strategic centre of Dutch-Indies policy shifted south and east to [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] and [[Jacatra|Jacatra]]. The Company's monopoly-by-force policy, inaugurated by the secret instruction of [[11 April]] [[1608]] and completed by the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] *Discours* of [[1 January]] [[1614]], would define the Dutch-Indies trade for the next two centuries and would, in the long run, lead to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]-[[1903]].
