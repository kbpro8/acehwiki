**Darul Islam** ("House of Islam") was an Indonesian Islamist insurgent movement that sought to establish an Islamic state — the [[Negara Islam Indonesia]] (N.I.I.) — in place of the secular Republic of Indonesia, operating from approximately [[1948]] into the [[1960s]] across three principal theaters: [[West Java]] under its supreme leader [[Kartosuwirjo|S. M. Kartosuwirjo]], [[Aceh]] under [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]], and [[South Celebes]] under [[Kahar Muzakkar]]. Its armed wing was the *Tentara Islam Indonesia* (T.I.I., Islamic Army of Indonesia), and the combined movement was commonly known as **DI/TII**.

## Origins and structure

The movement grew out of the Islamist political currents that emerged during the Japanese occupation and the Indonesian national revolution. Kartosuwirjo, a Javanese Islamic leader, proclaimed the Negara Islam Indonesia on [[7 August]] [[1949]] in [[West Java]] as the Dutch–Indonesian transfer of sovereignty neared. The DI was organised as a parallel state, with Kartosuwirjo as Imam, claiming sovereignty over territory it controlled by force.

By [[1953]], the movement operated in three distinct regional theaters, each under a semi-autonomous commander who recognised Kartosuwirjo's nominal religious authority:

- **West Java** — the original theater, controlled by Kartosuwirjo himself, combining guerrilla warfare with a shadow administration in the mountainous interior.
- **Aceh** — proclaimed on [[21 September]] [[1953]] when [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Daud Beureueh]] declared Aceh's accession to the N.I.I.; Kartosuwirjo named Daud Beureueh his governor for Aceh. The rebellion was triggered by political exclusion of the [[Masjumi]] party from the cabinet of [[Ali Sastroamidjojo|Ali Sastroamidjojo]], installed [[1 August]] [[1953]].
- **South Celebes** — under [[Kahar Muzakkar]], who declared South Celebes incorporated into the DI state on [[6 August]] [[1953]], a fortnight before the Aceh outbreak.

## The Aceh branch and its costs

The Aceh theater of the DI was of particular strategic importance. Beynon's account of the affair (*[[Nederland staat terecht in Indonesië]]*, [[1957]]) reports parliamentary figures on the human and material cost of the Aceh rebellion: approximately **10,000 dead**, **27,000 people in need**, material damage of **100 million rupiah**, railway losses of **80 million rupiah**, and **70% of railway stations destroyed**.

The rebellion broke out on [[20 September]] [[1953]]. [[Garuda Indonesian Airways]] suspended flights to the Acehnese capital [[Kutaradja]] on [[21 September]]. Daud Beureueh was officially designated as rebel leader on [[25 September]] [[1953]]. The territorial commander, [[Kolonel Simbolon]], stated on [[3 October]] [[1953]]: *"De toestand in Atjeh is zeer ernstig; het is niet mogelijk deze kwestie met wapenen alleen op te lossen"* ("The situation in Aceh is very serious; it is not possible to solve this issue with weapons alone").

The reconquest of Aceh's main towns was achieved by Colonel Simbolon of Territorium I, Bukit Barisan, through October–November [[1953]], but the underlying insurgency continued for years. The same DI period is characterised by the *De staatkundige ontwikkeling van Indonesië* as the movement operating "in West-Java, Atjeh en Zuid-Celebes" — the three theaters.

## The NIGO connection

The DI/TII movement was invoked in the political trials against Dutch nationals in [[Djakarta]] during [[1953]]–[[1956]]. The indictment against Schmidt in the **NIGO** affair (*Nederlands Indische Guerilla Organisatie*) charged him with providing support — including weapons drops near Tangkuban Prahu — to the DI/TII in West Java, and with being the leader of the NIGO, which was alleged to coordinate with the DI and other armed underground organisations. A prosecution witness, Haris bin Suhaemi, who described himself as a former DI member, testified to the alleged collusion between the Dutch Hoge Commissaris and Kartosuwirjo — testimony the presiding judge refused to enter into the record, wishing to avoid international complications.

## Significance

The Darul Islam movement represents the most sustained challenge to the authority of the Indonesian Republic in the [[1950s]], and its Acehnese branch connects directly to the longer history of Acehnese resistance to outside authority — Dutch colonial, Japanese, and then republican — that runs through the entire twentieth century. The movement also illustrates the central role of [[Masjumi]] and Islamic politics in Acehnese political identity, since it was the exclusion of Masjumi from government, not a purely religious grievance, that triggered the Aceh theater of the DI.
