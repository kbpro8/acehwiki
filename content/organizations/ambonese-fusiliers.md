**Ambonese fusiliers** (Dutch: *Ambonsche fuseliers*) were the Christian infantry companies recruited from the island of [[Ambon]] and the surrounding [[Moluccas]] that served as light foot-soldiery (Dutch: *fuseliers*) of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]] during the [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1904]]) and the subsequent [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]] campaigns under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]]. Distinguished in the colonial record as *gehecht aan hun godsdienst* (Dutch: "attached to their religion") — a phrase used to mark the depth of their Protestant and Catholic attachment — the Ambonese fusiliers formed a substantial component of the Dutch forces operating around [[Koeta-Radja]] and the inland lines of [[Groot-Atjeh|Great Atjeh]], and their loyalty to the colonial crown was framed, in the contemporary idiom, as the inseparable corollary of their Christian faith. The companies were ministered to by the Ambonese *Inlands Godsdienstleraar* [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]] — the *Soldaten-Dominee* of Atjeh — and by the Catholic *Soldaten-Pastoor* [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor Verbraak S.J.]], and they appear in the *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* tradition as the type of the colonial soldier whose authority rested less on rank than on knowledge, experience, and personality (Dutch: *gezag dat berustte op kennis, ervaring en persoonlijkheid*).

## Recruitment and the Moluccan inheritance

The Ambonese fusiliers were drawn from the Christianised villages of [[Ambon]], [[Saparoea]], [[Haroekoe]] and the surrounding islands of the central Moluccas, whose Reformed and Catholic populations were the inheritance of the [[17th century|seventeenth-century]] mission of the [[Dutch East India Company|Oost-Indische Compagnie]] and, before it, of the [[Portuguese traders|Portuguese]] mission of the early [[16th century|sixteenth century]]. By the [[19th century|nineteenth century]] these villages had been supplying soldiers to the Dutch colonial army for generations, and the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]] had come to rely on them as a reliable counterweight to the Muslim Javanese and Sundanese soldiery whose loyalty was, in the Atjeh theatre, considered politically suspect. The Ambonese recruits were formed into *fuseliers* companies — light infantry armed with the rifle (Dutch: *fusil*) — and attached to the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Marechaussee]] and line battalions operating in Atjeh. Their recruitment reflected the broader Dutch policy of using Christian-Indonesian forces (Dutch: *Christen-Indonesische troepen*) to supplement European troops in the archipelago, and the source's description of the Ambonese as "the first people in Indië that became Christian" places them at the head of this confessional-military strategy.

## Service in the Atjeh theatre

In Atjeh the Ambonese fusiliers formed a substantial part of the garrison of [[Koeta-Radja]] and of the mobile columns that operated against the Acehnese resistance. They were present at the [[1873]] opening of the [[Aceh War]] and continued to serve through the [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]] of the early [[20th century|twentieth century]], participating in the campaigns of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]] and his successors, including the [[Colonne van Daalen 1904|colonne van Daalen of 1904]] through the [[Gajolanden|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]]. The companies were characterised by their discipline, their marksmanship, and their willingness to operate in small mobile units — qualities that made them the natural complement to the [[Indische marechaussees|Indische marechaussees]] in the counter-insurgency warfare of the post-[[1896]] phase. The *Oom Ambon* tradition — recorded by [[G. W. T. Dames|G. W. T. Dames]] in [[1954]] — names individual Ambonese fusiliers who distinguished themselves in this service: [[Nussy]] the dardanel and tracker of the [[Colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]], [[Thijs de Fretes|Thijs de Fretes]] the *woudloper* and hunter of [[Bakongan]], [[Tanamal]] the natural shot, [[Salawono]] the M.W.O. Ridder, and [[Tamaela]] the korporaal of the [[Souvereiniteitsoverdracht (27 December 1949)|transfer of sovereignty]].

## Spiritual care and the dual chaplaincy

The Ambonese fusiliers were the principal congregation of the dual chaplaincy of Atjeh. Their Protestant majority was ministered to by the Ambonese *Inlands Godsdienstleraar* [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]], who accompanied them on expeditions, visited every post in the gewest several times a year, comforted the wounded and dying in the [[Panteh Perak|military hospital]] at Koeta Radja, and composed the [[Marechausseelied|Marechaussee songs]] that became the musical expression of their loyalty. Their Catholic minority was ministered to by the Jesuit *Soldaten-Pastoor* [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor Verbraak]], whose thirty-four-year chaplaincy paralleled Thenu's. The two chaplains practised an informal economy of pastoral substitution, each giving his strengthening word to the other's flock without disputing *sacramenten en geloofsleer*, and the *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.* tradition holds this arrangement up as the model of how a colonial garrison's spiritual care was actually done. The *gezag* of the chaplains, in this account, ran ahead of and outside the rulebook — a recognition that a dying Ambonese fusilier, whether Protestant or Catholic, needed the same thing from the man who came to his bed.

## Loyalty, the Oranje-Nassau and the end of the KNIL

The Ambonese fusiliers' loyalty to the colonial crown — framed in the *Marechausseelied* as "*trouw aan Koningin, trouw aan het vaandel en trouw aan de vlag*" (loyalty to the Queen, to the colours, and to the flag) — was the political-moral fact that anchored the colonial army's confessional-military strategy. Their loyalty survived the [[1942]] Japanese invasion, the [[1945]] Indonesian revolution, and the [[1949|1949]] transfer of sovereignty; but it could not survive the dissolution of the [[K.N.I.L.]] itself. The disbandment of the colonial army after [[1949]] — recorded in *Oom Ambon* as the end of the *[[tangsi]]* culture that had made the Ambonese fusilier — left the Ambonese soldiers without the institution that had made them, and the resulting political marginalisation of the Ambonese community in the new Indonesian republic culminated in the [[1950|1950]] unilateral proclamation of the [[Republik Maluku Selatan|Republik Maluku Selatan (RMS)]] and the subsequent suppression of the Ambonese insurrection by the Indonesian armed forces. The Ambonese fusiliers of Atjeh, in this longer arc, were both the type of the colonial soldier and the type of the soldier whom the colonial state left behind.
