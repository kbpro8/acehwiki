**Portuguese traders** (Dutch: *Portugeezen*) were the European merchants, soldiers, and missionaries who arrived in the Indonesian archipelago in the early [[16th century|sixteenth century]] and established a commercial and military presence in the spice-rich regions of [[Sumatra]], the [[Moluccas]], and the [[Strait of Malacca|straits of Malacca]] that competed directly with the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]] and supplied the maritime economy that the later [[Dutch East India Company|Dutch East India Company]] would inherit and displace. Trading in [[black-pepper|pepper]], gold, gunpowder, weapons, and [[opium-aceh|opium]], and engaging in the [[Strait of Malacca|straits]] in piracy and slave-capture — including the procurement of [[Nias|Nias]] islanders for the slave markets of the archipelago — the Portuguese found in [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Acheh]] a "conglomerate of fiercely competing Mohammedan potencies" each conducting trade with the *Overwal* (Malacca). Their activities contributed to the early modern maritime economy that later attracted Dutch and English interest, and their presence in the straits supplied the long-term *casus belli* between [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acheh]] and [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese Malacca]] that structured the history of the region for more than a century.

## The Estado da Índia and the capture of Malacca

The Portuguese presence in the East Indies was the work of the *Estado da Índia*, the network of forts, *feitorias* (factories), and missions established by [[Afonso de Albuquerque|Afonso de Albuquerque]] after his [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|capture of Malacca]] in [[August]] [[1511]]. The fall of Malacca to Albuquerque's fleet broke the existing Malay-Muslim trading network that had centred on the [[Malacca|Malaccan sultanate]] and displaced the Muslim merchants who had dominated the straits trade; many of these merchants resettled at [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Acheh]], [[Pedir]], and [[Pase|Pasei]], and the Koetaradja Sultanate that coalesced in the early [[16th century|sixteenth century]] drew much of its commercial and military momentum from the displaced Malaccan Muslims. The Portuguese *feitoria* at Malacca became the principal European entrepôt of the straits, and its captains — operating under the *Estado da Índia* based at [[Goa]] — conducted an aggressive commerce in spices, Chinese porcelain, Indian textiles, and Sumatran pepper that brought them into continual competition with the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]] and the other Muslim polities of the region. The Portuguese traders who appear in the Atjeh record were, in this sense, the commercial and military arm of the *Estado da Índia* in the straits.

## Trade, piracy, and the slave traffic in Nias

The commercial activities of the Portuguese traders in the Atjeh region were varied and frequently violent. They dealt in [[black-pepper|pepper]] — purchased at the Acehnese ports of [[Pedir]], [[Pase|Pasei]], and [[Pidie]] and shipped to [[Malacca]] and onward to Europe — and in Sumatran gold, camphor, and benjamin, and they introduced into the trade the gunpowder, muskets, and cannon that the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultans]] eagerly purchased for their wars. They also dealt in [[opium-aceh|opium]], which became one of the principal European imports into the archipelago. The Portuguese of the straits were, in addition, active in piracy and slave-capture: the *Cristão* (Portuguese-speaking) renegades and adventurers of the straits preyed on Muslim shipping, and the Portuguese traders procured slaves — including the [[Nias|Nias]] islanders whose capture is recorded in the source — for the labour markets of the archipelago. The slave traffic in Nias islanders was a long-standing feature of the regional economy, and the Portuguese involvement in it antedated and shaped the later Dutch regulation of the same traffic. The combination of commerce and predation that characterised the Portuguese traders in the straits is the source's principal image of them.

## The Acheh–Portuguese wars and the displacement of the Portuguese

The commercial and confessional rivalry between the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]] and the Portuguese of Malacca produced a series of naval wars that lasted for more than a century. The [[Atjeh-Aroe war (1539)|Acehnese attack on Aru]] of [[1539]] — repulsed by a Portuguese relief fleet — and the great [[Atjeh's attack on Malakka (1629)|Acehnese attack on Malacca]] of [[1629]] were the principal military episodes of this rivalry, but the straits saw continual Portuguese-Acehnese naval action throughout the [[16th century|sixteenth]] and early [[17th century|seventeenth centuries]]. The Portuguese traders found in Aceh a "conglomerate of fiercely competing Mohammedan potencies" — that is, a fragmented set of coastal polities — and the consolidation of these polities into the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Koetaradja Sultanate]] was, in part, a response to the Portuguese threat. The Portuguese monopoly in the straits was broken in the early [[17th century|seventeenth century]] by the arrival of the [[Dutch East India Company|Dutch East India Company]] and the [[English East India Company|English East India Company]], and the [[Conquest of Portuguese Malakka (1641)|Dutch capture of Malacca]] in [[January]] [[1641]] — accomplished with Acehnese acquiescence — ended the Portuguese era in the straits. The Portuguese traders who remained in the archipelago were absorbed into the new Dutch-dominated commercial order or withdrew to the residual Portuguese possessions of [[Timor]], [[Solor]], and [[Macau|Macao]].

## Legacy and the long memory of the Portuguese

The Portuguese presence in the Atjeh region left a durable legacy. The Roman Catholic faith introduced by Portuguese missionaries in the [[Moluccas]] — at [[Ambon]], [[Saparoea]], and the surrounding islands — survived the Dutch Reformed mission that succeeded it and remained the confession of a substantial minority of the [[Ambonese soldiers|Ambonese soldiery]] of the colonial army. The Portuguese language, in its creolised form, remained a lingua franca of the archipelago's Christian communities well into the [[19th century|nineteenth century]], and Portuguese loanwords entered the Malay and Acehnese vocabularies. The Portuguese introduction of firearms, cannon, and gunpowder into the Sumatran trade had a longer-term strategic consequence: the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]] of the [[16th century|sixteenth]] and [[17th century|seventeenth centuries]] was one of the most heavily armed Muslim polities of the Indian Ocean, and the great Acehnese naval expeditions against [[Malacca]] would have been impossible without Portuguese arms. The Portuguese traders, in this sense, both competed with and armed the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]], and their displacement by the [[Dutch East India Company|Dutch]] in the [[17th century|seventeenth century]] closed the first European chapter of the Atjeh maritime economy.
