**The Seulimeum-colonne** was a Dutch column formed under [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Major Palmer van den Broek]] in late February [[1942]] — composed of ten brigades of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Maréchaussee]] drawn from the IVde divisie and the central-Atjeh reserve at [[Lawe Sigalagala|Lawe Sigalagala]] — for the suppression of the [[F-beweging|F. beweging]] sabotage wave in the *sagi der XXII moekims* around [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]]. In the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), the Seulimeum-colonne is the operational instrument by which Colonel [[G. F. V. Gosenson|Gosenson]] sought to suppress the pre-invasion Atjehnese rising — and the operational decision that hollowed out the central-Atjeh guerrilla on the eve of the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese landings of 12 March 1942]].

## Formation (23 February 1942)

On [[23 February]] [[23 February 1942|23 February 1942]] — the day after the [[murder of Tiggelman at Seulimeum|Tiggelman murder]] and the day before the [[murder of Von Sperling|Von Sperling murder]] — Colonel [[G. F. V. Gosenson|Gosenson]] returned from his command post at [[Takengon|Takengon]] to [[Kotaradja|Kotaradja]] and ordered:

- That several Maréchaussee brigades of the nearest IVde divisie be sent immediately to Seulimeum under an officer.
- That 10 Maréchaussee brigades under Major Palmer van den Broek be directed to the same area as soon as possible.

The latter ten brigades — drawn from the central-Atjeh reserve at Lawe Sigalagala and Kotatjane — formed the Seulimeum-colonne. Palmer van den Broek was given operational orders: to restore order and tranquillity in the XXII moekims and to prevent further sabotage in that area; to track down the chiefs who had fled since the sabotage began (including the eldest son of the late [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]]); and to assume the administration of the XXII moekims.

## Operations (24 February – 8 March 1942)

By patrolling day and night and laying ambushes, Major Palmer van den Broek succeeded within a few days in bringing about an improvement in the situation and in preventing further sabotage, whereupon the second phase — that is, a period of relative calm — began. The chiefs who had fled — including the eldest son of the late [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] — remained untraceable. The staff history observes drily that the restored calm was merely an apparent success: no resistance cells had been encountered anywhere, and none of the chiefs who had fled had been captured. It had, however, become clear that the F. beweging was planning a second wave of activity. The role of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] remained unclear. The involvement of the local leaders in Seulimeum was certain, as was the involvement of prominent indigenous persons who, like a number of chiefs, had "disappeared."

The Seulimeum-colonne's operations closed the first action period of the F-beweging and opened the period of relative calm that would be broken by the second sabotage wave of [[7 March|7]]–[[8 March]] [[8 March 1942|8 March 1942]]. On [[8 March]] [[8 March 1942|8 March 1942]] the colonne reported that the Lho'Nga school had been burned and the Chinese ward master of Seulimeum murdered; in the area between Kotaradja and Seulimeum, which was under strict military control, the sabotage could not be brought under control. The colonne's operations thus failed to suppress the F-beweging despite the day-and-night patrolling.

## Operational consequences

The Seulimeum-colonne's ten brigades — drawn from the central-Atjeh guerrilla reserve — were not available for the central-Atjeh guerrilla after the Japanese landings of [[12 March]] [[12 March 1942|12 March]]. The staff history's emphasis on the colonne's failure — no resistance cells had been encountered anywhere — frames the deployment as a strategic error: the Dutch command committed its central-Atjeh reserve to the suppression of a rising it could not suppress, and thereby deprived itself of the reserve it would need for the guerrilla it had planned.

## Significance

The Seulimeum-colonne is, in the staff history's Atjeh chapter, the operational instrument of the Dutch response to the F-beweging rising — and the operational decision that hollowed out the central-Atjeh guerrilla. The staff history's emphasis on the colonne's apparent success — outward calm without the actual suppression of the resistance cells — frames the deployment as the structural cause of the Dutch inability to mount a sustained central-Atjeh guerrilla after the Japanese landings. The colonne is also the staff history's principal instance of the operational paradox of the pre-invasion period: the more troops the Dutch committed to the suppression of the F-beweging, the fewer troops they had available for the guerrilla they had planned.
