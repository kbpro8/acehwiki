**The Koetaradja Sultanate** was the powerful Malay Muslim polity centred on the coastal settlement of [[Koeta-Radja|Koetaradja]] (modern [[Banda Acheh|Banda Aceh]]) on the northern tip of [[Sumatra]] that, by approximately [[1500]], had extended its suzerainty over the entire [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Acheh]] region and the neighbouring territories of [[Pidie]], [[Pase|Pasei]], and the east-coast polities of [[Tamiang]] and [[Langkat]]. Its sultans gradually subjugated the other local rulers of northern Sumatra, consolidating control over the [[black-pepper|pepper]] trade and the maritime routes of the [[Strait of Malacca|straits of Malacca]], and laying the institutional foundation that [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] would carry to its seventeenth-century apogee. The polity's dominance waned in the [[19th century|nineteenth century]] as [[Dutch East Indies Government|Dutch colonial pressure]] increased, culminating in the outbreak of the [[Aceh War]] in [[1873]] and the formal abolition of the sultanate after the [[Capture of the kraton (24 January 1874)|fall of the kraton]] in [[1874]].

## Origins and the consolidation of the northern Sumatran coast

The Koetaradja Sultanate emerged in the late [[15th century|fifteenth century]] out of the older patchwork of coastal polities — [[Lamuri]], [[Pedir]], [[Pase|Pasei]], [[Pidie]] and [[Daya]] — that had long controlled the northern Sumatran outlets of the [[Strait of Malacca|straits of Malacca]] trade. The foundation of the sultanate is traditionally associated with [[Ali Mughayat Syah|Sultan Ali Mughayat Syah]] (r. c. [[1514]]–[[1530]]), who unified the competing coastal ports under a single ruler seated at Koetaradja and substituted the new sultanate for the older, fragmented commercial network. The process of consolidation continued under his successors, who absorbed [[Daya]] (c. [[1520]]), [[Pidie]] and [[Pase|Pasei]] ([[1524]]) and extended the sultanate's reach southward along both coasts of Sumatra. By the close of the [[16th century|sixteenth century]] the Koetaradja Sultanate had become the dominant Muslim power of the eastern Indian Ocean, and its sultans had begun to challenge the [[Portuguese traders|Portuguese]] of [[Malacca]] for control of the straits. The[[koeta-radja|capital]] itself — the *kuta raja* or "royal fortress" — gave its name to the polity and became, under Iskandar Muda, one of the great commercial capitals of the Malay world.

## Expansion and the pepper-and-gold economy

The sultanate's expansion enabled it to dominate the so-called *Overwal* (literally "across the wall") trade network — the Malay-world traffic across the [[Strait of Malacca|straits]] that exchanged Sumatran [[black-pepper|pepper]], gold, camphor, and benjamin for Indian textiles, Chinese porcelain, and European firearms. The pepper gardens (Dutch: *pepertuinen*) of the [[Groot-Atjeh|Great Atjeh]] hinterland and the gold washings of the [[Gajolanden|Gayo highlands]] supplied the sultanate's principal exports, and the customs house (Dutch: *tolhuis*) at Koetaradja drew the revenue that financed both the court and the fleet. The [[Portuguese traders|Portuguese]] who arrived in the archipelago after their [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|capture of Malacca]] in [[1511]] found the Koetaradja Sultanate to be, in the phrase of the source, a "conglomerate of fiercely competing Mohammedan potencies" each conducting trade with the *Overwal* — but the consolidation of the sultanate under a single sultan progressively unified these competing outlets and gave the polity the military and commercial weight to mount the great naval expeditions against [[Malacca]] of [[1529]], [[1568]], [[1573]], [[1575]], and the disastrous [[Atjeh's attack on Malakka (1629)|attack of 1629]]. The court of Koetaradja became, in the early seventeenth century, one of the great Islamic courts of Southeast Asia.

## The Iskandar Muda apogee and the long decline

The Koetaradja Sultanate reached its political and cultural apogee under [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] (r. [[1607]]–[[1636]]), whose reign is treated in the wiki as the [[Atjeh bloeitijdperk (1607-1636)|bloeitijdperk (golden age) of Atjeh]]. Iskandar Muda centralised the administration of the sultanate at the [[Kraton of Aceh|kraton of Koetaradja]], extended the sultanate's suzerainty over the [[Gajolanden|Gayo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]], the east-coast dependencies of [[Deli]], [[Langkat]], and [[Asahan|Serang]], and the western Malay states of [[Perak]], [[Kedah]], and [[Pahang]], and turned the court into a major centre of Islamic learning under the influence of the Sufi scholar [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nuruddin ar-Raniri]]. After Iskandar Muda's death in [[1636]] the sultanate entered a long decline marked by the [[Female rule of Acheh|succession of four queens]] ([[1641]]–[[1699]]) and the gradual devolution of effective power from the sultan to the territorial *uleebalang* (chieftains). By the early [[19th century|nineteenth century]] the Koetaradja Sultanate had become a loose confederation of regional potentates nominally subject to a sultan whose actual authority extended little beyond the capital.

## Dutch pressure and the abolition of the sultanate

The decline of the Koetaradja Sultanate was sealed by the westward expansion of the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]] in the [[19th century|nineteenth century]]. The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of London]] of [[1824]] placed Sumatra within the Dutch sphere of influence, and the [[Sumatra Tractaat (1871)|Sumatra-tractaat]] of [[1871]] removed the British reservation that had protected the sultanate. The subsequent Acehnese negotiations with foreign consuls in [[Singapore]] provided the *casus belli* for the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|Dutch declaration of war]] of [[26 March]] [[1873]], and the [[Capture of the kraton (24 January 1874)|fall of the kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]] marked the formal end of the Koetaradja Sultanate as a sovereign polity. The fugitive sultans [[Sultan Mahmud Syah|Mahmud Syah]] (d. [[1874]]) and [[Sultan Muhammad Dawot Syah|Muhammad Dawot Syah]] continued a nominal resistance from the interior until the [[Surrender of the Atjeh pretender Sultan (1903)|surrender of the pretender Sultan]] in [[1903]], but the sultanate itself was not revived; the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] administered the former territory as the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] from its seat at [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta Radja]], the colonial town it built on the ruins of the sultanate's capital.
