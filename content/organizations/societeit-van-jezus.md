**The Sociëteit van Jezus** — the Society of Jesus (Dutch: *Sociëteit van Jezus*), the Catholic religious order of priest-members known colloquially as the Jesuits — was the metropolitan religious society in the [[Netherlands]] that [[Pastoor Verbraak|H. C. Verbraak]] joined before his [[1869]] ordination and that supplied the theological formation and institutional framework from which the Catholic mission of [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Atjeh]] was launched. As a Counter-Reformation order founded by [[Ignatius of Loyola]] in [[1540]] and restored in the [[Netherlands]] in [[1853]] after its late-eighteenth-century suppression, the Dutch province of the Society of Jesus was the principal vehicle of Dutch Catholic intellectual and missionary life in the second half of the [[19th century|nineteenth century]], and its members were dispatched to the colonies as priests, chaplains, and missionaries — playing a key role in the spiritual life of the [[Indische Leger|colonial forces]] and of the Catholic congregations of the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]]. In Atjeh, the Society's principal exponent was the *Soldaten-Pastoor* [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor Verbraak S.J.]], whose thirty-four-year chaplaincy paralleled that of the Protestant *Soldaten-Dominee* [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]] and whose ministry gave the Atjeh garrison its dual confessional structure.

## Origins and the restored Dutch province

The Society of Jesus was founded in [[1540]] by the Basque soldier-saint [[Ignatius of Loyola|Ignatius of Loyola]] and was, from its foundation, organised as a centrally directed order of priest-members bound by a special vow of obedience to the Pope for mission service wherever he might send them. The Society's early missions in Asia — those of [[Francis Xavier|Francis Xavier]] to [[India]], the [[Moluccas]], and [[Japan]] in the [[1540s]] — established the Jesuits as the principal Catholic missionaries of the early modern East, and the [[Portuguese traders|Portuguese]] *padroado* system gave them institutional control over the Asian mission until the suppression of the Society in [[1773]]. The Society was restored in [[1814]], and its Dutch province was reconstituted in [[1853]] — a date that coincided with the restoration of the Catholic hierarchy in the [[Netherlands]] after the constitutional reforms of the [[1848]] constitution. The restored Dutch province was small, intellectually vigorous, and missionary in orientation, and it supplied priests both to the Dutch mission in the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] and to the chaplaincy of the Dutch army. The Society's novitiate and scholasticate in the [[Netherlands]] provided the theological formation that prepared missionaries for service in the colonies, and [[Pastoor Verbraak|Verbraak]] was among the Dutch Jesuits who passed through this formation in the [[1860s]].

## The Verbraak chaplaincy and the Atjeh mission

The Society's most visible institutional presence in Atjeh was the chaplaincy of [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor H. C. Verbraak S.J.]], who joined the Sociëteit van Jezus in the [[Netherlands]] before his [[1869]] ordination and who arrived in [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Atjeh]] in [[1874]] as a *R.K. geestelijke van den tweeden rang* (Roman Catholic clergyman of the second rank) attached to the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]]. Verbraak's thirty-four-year chaplaincy — which took him onto the field with the troops, into the wards of the [[Pante Pirak|military hospital]] at [[Koeta-Radja]], and eventually to the extension of the mission to [[Pedir]] in [[1898]] — is the central institutional fact of Catholicism in Atjeh, and his presence made the Jesuit mission the more visibly institutionalised of the two Christian confessions of the garrison. Verbraak consecrated the first Catholic church of the colonial capital in [[1885]] (a wooden church built from his own savings and the gifts of believers at [[Padang]] and [[Batavia]]), built the chapel-of-ease at [[Oeleë-Lheue|Olehleh]] in [[1896]], and produced the *Kerkelijke beschouwingen over Groot-Atjeh en onderhoorigheden* (ecclesiastical observations on Great Atjeh and Dependencies) that ran in the magazine [[St. Claverbond|St. Claverbond]] from [[1889]] to [[1907]]. The Catholic mission he founded passed, on his [[1908]] departure, into the care of the [[Zusters van Schijndel|Zusters van Schijndel]], and by the [[1934]] jubilee of the Indian mission counted over a thousand Catholic souls in Koeta Radja-Atjeh alone.

## The Dutch Jesuits and the colonial chaplaincy

The Dutch Jesuits who served in the Netherlands Indies did so within an institutional framework shaped by the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]]'s regulation of religion and by the order's own missionary priorities. The government posted and paid Catholic *veldpredikers* (field chaplains, Dutch: *veldpredikers*) to the garrisons where Catholic soldiery were concentrated, and the Jesuit province supplied the priests who filled these posts; the order's own missionary work, by contrast, was directed through the apostolic vicariates of the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] and the network of Jesuit schools, seminaries, and mission stations that the order maintained across the archipelago. In Atjeh, where the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] had been rigorously Muslim since the [[16th century|sixteenth century]] and where the colonial state forbade direct proselytism among Muslims, the Jesuit presence was confined to the European and Ambonese soldiery of the garrison. The chaplaincy of [[Pastoor Verbraak|Verbraak]] was thus the principal form in which the Sociëteit van Jezus was actually present in Atjeh, and the chaplaincy's irenic cooperation with the Protestant [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]] — each giving his strengthening word to the other's flock without disputing *sacramenten en geloofsleer* — was the lived expression of the dual confessional structure of the Atjeh garrison.

## Symbolic role and the Dutch royal house

The Sociëteit van Jezus was bound, through its chaplaincy and mission, to the symbolic apparatus of the [[Dutch royal house|Dutch royal house (Dutch: *Koninklijk Vorstenhuis*)]] and the broader Dutch Catholic revival of the late [[19th century|nineteenth]] and early [[20th century|twentieth centuries]]. The Jesuit mission was, in its metropolitan orientation, a vehicle of Dutch Catholic identity, and its representatives were received at court and decorated by the crown. The audience granted to [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]] by [[Queen Wilhelmina|Koningin Wilhelmina (Dutch: *Koningin*)]] in [[1934]] after his forty Atjeh-years — though a Protestant rather than a Catholic occasion — illustrates the wider pattern by which the colonial chaplaincy, Catholic and Protestant alike, drew its authority from the endorsement of the monarchy. The Catholic mission of Koeta Radja-Atjeh stood, in this sense, at the intersection of three authorities — the Sociëteit van Jezus that supplied its priests, the [[Dutch East Indies Government|N.I. Gouvernement]] that posted and paid them, and the [[Dutch royal house|Koninklijk Vorstenhuis]] that decorated them — and the Society's role in Atjeh is best understood as the working-out of this triple commission at the level of the garrison and the bedside.
