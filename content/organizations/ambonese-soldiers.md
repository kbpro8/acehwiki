**Ambonese soldiers** (Dutch: *Ambonezen*) were the Christian Indonesian troops recruited from the island of [[Ambon]] and the surrounding [[Moluccas]] who formed, from the early [[19th century|nineteenth century]] to the [[1949]] dissolution of the [[K.N.I.L.]], a core component of the Dutch colonial army in the [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands Indies]] and a principal instrument of the campaigns to bring [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Aceh]] under Western rule. Described in the source as "the first people in Indië that became Christian" — a phrase placing the Ambonese at the head of the colonial state's confessional-military strategy — they served alongside Dutch officers throughout the early phases of the [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1904]]), providing manpower, local knowledge, and the disciplined Christian soldiery on which the *concentratie* (concentration) system and the post-[[1898]] campaigns of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]] depended. Their presence in the Atjeh garrison reflected the broader Dutch policy of using Christian-Indonesian forces (Dutch: *Christen-Indonesische troepen*) to supplement European troops in the archipelago, and their association with the colonial army outlasted the colonial state itself.

## Origins and the Moluccan Christian inheritance

The Ambonese soldiers of the colonial army were the military expression of a much older Christian presence in the [[Moluccas]]. The [[Portuguese traders|Portuguese]] mission of the early [[16th century|sixteenth century]] and the [[Dutch East India Company|Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC)]] Reformed mission of the [[17th century|seventeenth century]] had between them produced a deeply Christianised population on the islands of [[Ambon]], [[Saparoea]], [[Haroekoe]] and the surrounding Lease and Uliaser groups, and these villages had supplied soldiers to the Dutch colonial state since the company period. By the [[19th century|nineteenth century]] the recruitment of Ambonese soldiers was institutionalised through the [[Koninklijke Militaire Academie|RMA]]-trained officer corps and a network of local recruitment posts on the islands, and the Ambonese companies had acquired a reputation as the most reliable non-European soldiery of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]]. The Reformed and Catholic villages of the Moluccas were, in the colonial idiom, *kerkelijke dorpen* (churched villages) whose men could be counted on to remain loyal in the face of the Muslim resistance that the Atjeh campaigns confronted, and the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]] accordingly built its Atjeh strategy around them.

## Service in the Aceh War

The Ambonese soldiers served in every phase of the [[Aceh War]], from the [[5 April|5 April]] [[1873]] amphibious assault that opened the conflict to the [[1903|1903]] surrender of the [[Surrender of the Atjeh pretender Sultan (1903)|pretender Sultan]] and the [[1904|1904]]-[[1908|1908]] extension of Dutch authority into the [[Gajolanden|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]]. They served in the line battalions of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]], in the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Korps Marechaussee]] counter-insurgency columns, and in the [[Ambonese fusiliers|Ambonese fusilier]] companies that operated as light infantry around [[Koeta-Radja]] and the inland posts of [[Groot-Atjeh|Great Atjeh]]. Their service is documented in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* by [[G. W. T. Dames|G. W. T. Dames]] ([[1954]]), which records the named figures who embodied the type: [[Nussy]] the dardanel and tracker of the [[Colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt of 1910-1911]], [[Thijs de Fretes|Thijs de Fretes]] the *woudloper* and hunter of [[Bakongan]], [[Tanamal]] the natural shot, [[Salawono]] the M.W.O. Ridder, and [[Tamaela]] the korporaal of the [[Souvereiniteitsoverdracht (27 December 1949)|transfer of sovereignty]] in [[1949]]. The tradition recorded by Dames frames the Ambonese soldier as the moral centre of the colonial army — the *Oom* (uncle) whose authority rested on knowledge, experience and personality (Dutch: *gezag dat berustte op kennis, ervaring en persoonlijkheid*) rather than on rank — and presents this authority as the patient daily work that held the colonial army together when the rulebook failed.

## The chaplaincy and the *Soldaten-Dominee*

The Ambonese soldiery of Atjeh were the principal congregation of the Ambonese *Inlands Godsdienstleraar* [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]], the universally honoured *Soldaten-Dominee* who served the garrison from [[1894]] to his death on [[11 May|11 May]] [[1937]]. Thenu — himself an Ambonese — accompanied the troops on expeditions, visited every post from [[Koeta-Radja|the capital]] to the deeply inland [[Blang-Kedjeren]] and [[Lokop]] several times a year, comforted the wounded and dying in the [[Panteh Perak|military hospital]], and composed the [[Marechausseelied|Marechaussee songs]] whose refrain of "*trouw aan Koningin, trouw aan het vaandel en trouw aan de vlag*" became the musical expression of the Ambonese soldier's loyalty. He was made *Ridder in de Orde van Oranje Nassau* in [[1901]] — exceptional because the *Inlandsche godsdienstleraar* ranked only for a *medaille* — and after his [[1934]] retirement was received in audience by [[Queen Wilhelmina|Koningin Wilhelmina (Dutch: *Koningin*)]] in the [[Netherlands]]. The chaplaincy of Thenu is the principal institutional expression of the bond between the Ambonese soldier and the colonial state, and his pairing with the Catholic *Soldaten-Pastoor* [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor Verbraak S.J.]] — the two together forming the dual chaplaincy of Atjeh — gave the Ambonese soldiery a spiritual care that the *Oom Ambon* tradition holds up as exemplary.

## The end of the KNIL and the political aftermath

The Ambonese soldiers' loyalty to the colonial crown survived the [[1942]] Japanese invasion and the [[1945]] Indonesian revolution, but it could not survive the dissolution of the [[K.N.I.L.]] itself after the [[Souvereiniteitsoverdracht (27 December 1949)|transfer of sovereignty]] of [[27 December]] [[1949]]. The disbandment of the colonial army — recorded in *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.* as the end of the *[[tangsi]]* culture that had made the Ambonese soldier — left the Ambonese community without the institution that had anchored its place in the colonial state, and the resulting political marginalisation culminated in the [[1950|1950]] unilateral proclamation of the [[Republik Maluku Selatan|Republik Maluku Selatan (RMS)]] at [[Ambon]] and the subsequent suppression of the Ambonese insurrection by the Indonesian armed forces. The *Oom Ambon* tradition records this end with the figure of [[Nussy]]: "*was altijd soldaat gebleven, soldaat sadja*" — always a soldier, nothing but a soldier — even after his transfer to the *mantri-politie* on Ambon. The Ambonese soldiers of Atjeh were, in this longer arc, both the type of the colonial soldier and the type of the soldier whom the colonial state left behind.
