**The Djawa** — the colony at [[Mekka|Mekka] of Muslims originating from [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Nederlandsch-Indië]] — was the principal East Indian Muslim institution at Mekka in the late 19th century, and the principal object of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s attention during his [[Snouck Hurgronje's Mekka journey (1884-1885)|Mekka journey]] of [[1884]]–[[1885]]. The colony — treated in the fourth chapter of Snouck Hurgronje's [[Mekka (Snouck Hurgronje)|Mekka]] (1888–1889) — comprised pilgrims from all parts of Nederlandsch-Indië, including the Atjèhse pilgrims who would be the immediate occasion of Snouck Hurgronje's Atjèh interest.

## The Composition

The Djawa colony at Mekka was composed of Muslims originating from Nederlandsch-Indië — pilgrims who had come to Mekka for the *hajj* and had stayed, or who came and went annually with the pilgrimage season. The colony was the principal East Indian Muslim institution at Mekka, and was — in Snouck Hurgronje's account — the principal means by which the religious life of Nederlandsch-Indië was influenced by the spiritual centre of Islam.

## Snouck Hurgronje's Attention

The Djawa had Abd al-Gaffār's (Snouck Hurgronje's) particular attention, witness the fourth chapter of his [[Mekka (Snouck Hurgronje)|Mekka]]. Among them were also the Atjèhers. They attracted him, because they presented for the Netherlands a problem whose solution had not yet been found. Over there he had spoken much with them; and the exiled [[Habib Abdul Rachman El-Zahir|Habib Abdoerrachman ez-Zahir]] — Atjèh's "unkingsed king" from the days of the governor [[General van der Heijden|Van der Heijden]], who had submitted against the payment of a handsome annual salary and had since resided at Mekka — had already on [[8 Moharram]] [[1302]] (i.e. in [[October 1884|October]] [[1884]], when Snouck Hurgronje had been but a few months at Djidda) given him a *recept* — a recipe — for how Atjèh, in the Habib's view, was to be pacified.

## Significance

The Djawa colony at Mekka is the principal East Indian Muslim institution at Mekka in the late 19th century, and the principal object of Snouck Hurgronje's attention during his Mekka journey of [[1884]]–[[1885]]. The colony — treated in the fourth chapter of his *Mekka* — was the immediate context of Snouck Hurgronje's Atjèh interest; it was among the Atjèhse pilgrims of the Djawa colony that he first encountered the people whose resistance to the Dutch he would later, as advisor to the Netherlands Indies government, diagnose and prescribe the means to suppress.
