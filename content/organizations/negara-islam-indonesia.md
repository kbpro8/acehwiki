**Negara Islam Indonesia** (N.I.I., "Islamic State of Indonesia") was the Islamic-state movement proclaimed by [[Kartosuwirjo|Kartosuwirjo]] in [[West Java|West Java]] in [[1949]], whose armed wing, the *Tentara Islam Indonesia* (T.I.I.), fought a protracted insurgency against the Republic that spread, by [[1953]], to South Celebes under [[Kahar Muzakkar|Kahar Muzakkar]] and — most consequentially for its strategic weight — to [[Atjèh|Atjeh]] under [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]].

## Atjeh's accession (September 1953)

Henri J. H. Alers's *Om een rode of groene Merdeka* treats [[Atjèh's accession to the Negara Islam Indonesia (1953)|Atjeh's accession to the N.I.I.]], proclaimed on [[21 September]] [[1953]], as by far the most significant expansion of the movement in its history to that point — transforming what outside observers could until then dismiss as mere "bandit affairs" into, in Alers's words, "an armed Islamic uprising of national proportions" at a single stroke. Kartosuwirjo appointed Daoed Beureuh governor for Atjeh, and the rebel forces there were organised as **Divisie Tje' Dit'Do** of the T.I.I.; Atjeh became the N.I.I.'s Vth military territory, as against North Sumatra's status as the Republic's Ist.

## Coordination across fronts

Alers notes signs of at least loose coordination between the N.I.I.'s fronts: increased T.I.I. activity in the [[Preanger|Preanger]] coincided with the outbreak of the Atjeh rising, and Kahar Muzakkar's South Celebes forces intensified their own operations — attacking [[Madjene|Madjene]] on [[20 October]] [[1953]] and fighting around [[Tjamba|Tjamba]] on [[1 November]] — around the same time. Contact between the Celebes and Java branches, by contrast, Alers judges to have been "so slight" that even minor doctrinal differences between them went unnoticed for a time.

## International repercussions

The N.I.I.'s Atjeh accession, in Alers's analysis, carried genuine international risk in a way its other fronts did not, given Atjeh's strategic position. He connects Kartosuwirjo's propaganda pamphlet to foreign embassies — invoking the World Muslim Conference's declaration of [[27 September]] [[1953]] against "red" causes — and President [[Soekarno|Sukarno]]'s own charge, made at [[Bone|Bone]] on [[14 October]] [[1953]], that Kartosuwirjo had solicited American assistance, as evidence that the Republic itself recognised Atjeh, rather than South Celebes, as the front that could plausibly internationalise the conflict. The government's subsequent strategy — of first securing Atjeh's coasts, main roads, and principal towns under Colonel [[Simbolon|Simbolon]] — was designed specifically to prevent that internationalisation, reducing the Atjeh front, by the end of November 1953, to scattered interior bands even as the parallel insurgencies in West Java and South Celebes likewise settled into prolonged stalemate.
