**Tirò Teungkoes** was the fanatical house of *[[oelama]]s* of [[Pidië]], "the cradle of the irreconcilable religious leaders", whose destruction by [[H. J. Schmidt]] between [[1908]] and [[3 December]] [[1911]] was, in [[Maurice Henri du Croo|du Croo]]'s phrase, "the great epic of those stirring days".

The line ran from [[Teungkoe di Tirò]], the fiercest religious resistance leader, who died in [[1891]], through his eldest son [[Teungkoe Mat Amin]], killed at [[Aneu' Galong]] in June [[1896]], to [[Tgk. Tjhi' Majét]] and [[Tgk. di Boekét]], killed by Schmidt in May [[1910]], and finally to the sixteen-year-old [[Tgk. Ma'at]], who fell in December [[1911]] after refusing a last offer of submission "because a Tirò Teungkoe preferred [[sjahid|martyrdom]] to surrender." At the end of [[1907]] the Tirò band fled from [[Geumpang]] to Pidië before Schmidt's patrols; on [[8 February]] [[1908]] Schmidt surprised its hiding-place on the *kr.* [[Meu']], where five of the principal Teungkoes fell. After the deaths of the two great Tirò men in [[1910]] the band split: the *Pedireezen* attached themselves to [[T. Dagang]] of [[Tangsé]], while the strangers, mainly *oelama's* from [[Groot Atjeh]], followed Tgk. Ma'at. The struggle was waged by Schmidt with his two famous *[[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|marechaussée]]* brigades under [[Molier]] and [[Von Gilsa]], "who for years devoted themselves exclusively to this commission," and is described as a classic example of the colonial *kleine oorlog* in *[[Marechaussee in Atjeh (1943)|Marechaussee in Atjeh]]*. A last remnant under [[Tgk. Ma' Aréh di Lam Goet]] was taken in January [[1913]], "and Central Pidië was cleared."
