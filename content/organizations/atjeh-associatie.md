**The Atjeh-Associatie** — also referred to as the *Aceh Association* — was a Dutch civil/military association in [[Koeta Radja]] in [[Aceh War|Atjeh]] during the spring of [[1896]], mentioned in the early pages of Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* in connection with its cattle market near the road to [[Ketapan Doea]]. The association's grounds formed a topographical reference point for the defensive works thrown up around [[Koeta Radja]] on [[30 March]] [[1896]] after [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]]: one of the four raised guardhouses — each for 15 men, surrounded by a 10 m wide wire fence — was erected near the road to Ketapan Doewa about 100 m from the veekraal of the Atjeh-Associatie.

## Background and Formation

The Atjeh-Associatie's character — whether a civil association of Dutch residents and traders, a military officers' mess association, or a combined civil/military social club — is not fully described in Kruisheer's source. The presence of a cattle market near the road to [[Ketapan Doea]] suggests an economic function: the supply of cattle to the colonial garrison and the European population of [[Koeta Radja]]. The name "*Associatie*" itself echoes the Dutch East Indies tradition of military and civil *associaties* — formal associations of officers or officials for mutual support, mess, and recreation — that flourished in the nineteenth-century colonial army and administration. The association's grounds lay in the eastern fringe of the Dutch *geconcentreerde Linie (Concentrated Line)|geconcentreerde linie*, near the Ooster Ringdijk.

## Role in the Atjeh War

The Atjeh-Associatie's role in the spring [[1896]] campaign is recorded indirectly, through the topographical significance of its cattle market for the defensive works thrown up around [[Koeta Radja]] after [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]]. On [[30 March]] [[1896]] — as part of Gen.-Maj. C. Deykerhoff's counter-measures — strong work was done to improve the defence of Koeta Radja, including barbed wire standing fence around [[Nesoch]]; two gun stands on the south side of the campement; ~14 covered infantry positions; *banketten* and covered infantry positions along the enceinte of kampong Baroe and kampong Gedah; raised guardhouses made more storm-proof; 3 cm armor plates placed on different *banketten*; and a *palissadeering* placed behind the fence between the laboratory and the watch on the road to [[Oleh-leh]]. Four raised guardhouses — each for 15 men, surrounded by a 10 m wide wire fence — were erected: (1) on the road to Oleh-leh at the bridge over the Kroeng Doi; (2) behind the eastern *kogelvanger* at [[Petjoet]]; (3) near the road to Ketapan Doewa about 100 m from the *veekraal* of the Atjeh-Associatie; and (4) at the Ooster Ringdijk. The Atjeh-Associatie's veekraal thus served as a landmark for the third of these raised guardhouses, anchoring the eastern sector of the Koeta Radja defensive line.

## Composition and Strength

The Atjeh-Associatie's membership, leadership, and corporate structure are not described in Kruisheer's source. The institution is referenced only by its cattle market near the road to [[Ketapan Doea]], as a topographical landmark for the defensive works of [[30 March]] [[1896]]. The Governor-General [[Jhr. C. H. A. van der Wijck]] is mentioned in the frontispiece portrait of the balso and may be presumed to have patronized such an association, though no direct link is recorded in the source.

## Significance

The Atjeh-Associatie's significance in the spring [[1896]] campaign is essentially topographical: its *veekraal* served as the landmark for one of the four raised guardhouses erected on [[30 March]] [[1896]] to defend [[Koeta Radja]] against an enemy assault that, in the event, did not materialize. The association's existence also reflects the colonial society of Atjeh — a small European community of officials, officers, and traders served by formal associations that provided mess, recreation, and economic functions — that underpinned the Dutch military presence. The naming of one of Koeta Radja's streets as the Deijkerhofflaan, after the dismissed Gen.-Maj. C. Deykerhoff, illustrates the same colonial-society culture.
