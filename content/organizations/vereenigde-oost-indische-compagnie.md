The **Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie** ("United East India Company") — the VOC, the Dutch East India Company — was the chartered trading company of the United Provinces of the Netherlands, founded on [[20 March]] [[1602]] by the amalgamation of the several pre-existing *voorcompagnieën* (pre-Company trading companies) into a single national monopoly. In [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history the VOC is the principal European institutional interlocutor of the sultanate from [[1602]] onwards: the founder of the [[Dutch lodge at Atjeh|first Dutch lodge on Sumatra]] (baptised "[[Fort Zeeland at Atjeh|Zeeland]]"), the employer of the *Opperhoofden* and *Opperkooplieden* who conducted the Atjeh trade, and the patron of the formal embassies — above all [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury's embassy of 1641]] — through which the Dutch-Atjeh relationship was conducted.

## Foundation, 20 March 1602

The VOC was founded on [[20 March]] [[1602]], in the midst of the "wilde vaart" period of [[1595]]–[[1602]] — the years of independent Dutch trading voyages to the East Indies that the new Company was created to monopolise. The amalgamation of the several *voorcompagnieën* into the "Nederlandsche Generale Geoctroyeerde Oost-Indische Compagnie" — better known by its monogram, "de groote V, in den linkerarm de O, in den rechter de C" — was not a smooth financial process, especially because several fleets were still at sea during the foundation. The Company's charter gave it a national monopoly of the East India trade, with the powers of treaty, war, and coinage in the East.

## The first VOC fleet at Atjeh, 1602

The first VOC fleet sent out after the Company's foundation reached [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in [[1602]], where [[Joris van Spilbergh|Spilbergh]] — still sailing as a pre-VOC admiral — was already present. Spilbergh sold one of his ships to the new Company at Atjeh, and reached the fatherland with his well-laden ship. The episode is one of the practical moments at which the pre-VOC "wilde vaart" was absorbed into the chartered Company's structure. From this point forward, Dutch-Atjeh relations would be conducted under the Company's flag.

## The Dutch lodge at Atjeh

By [[1641]] the Company possessed "in Atjeh een loge, waar een Opperhoofd met acht helpers verblijf hield" — a lodge in Atjeh, where an *Opperhoofd* (chief) with eight *helpers* (assistants) resided. The Sultan had granted the Company "het uitsluitend recht tot handel op Sumatra's Westkust" — the exclusive right to trade on Sumatra's Westkust — where pepper was the principal product. The lodge was the Company's principal station on the northern coast of Sumatra, and the institutional base from which the Westkust pepper trade was organised. See [[Dutch lodge at Atjeh]].

## The Heeren Zeventien and the Bewindhebbers

The Company was governed by two bodies: the *Bewindhebbers* (Directors), drawn from the chambers of the several port-cities (Amsterdam, Zeeland, Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn, Enkhuizen); and the *Heeren Zeventien* (the Lords Seventeen), the central standing committee that directed the Company's affairs between meetings of the full directorate. In [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s account the *Bewindhebbers* are the body that "voerden de orders uit" of the Atjeh sultans — "gaarne voerden de Bewindhebbers de orders uit wegens de groote winsten, die er op te maken waren" — gladly executed the orders, because of the great profits to be made — and the body that, in later years, tried in vain to sell further jewels in Atjeh.

The *Heeren Zeventien* are the body to whom the High Government at Batavia reported — and whose long-winded missives in "de tale Kanaäns" (the language of Canaan, the formal official style) the Batavian officials sometimes found ways to circumvent. In the Atjeh record, the Heeren Zeventien sent copies of sworn declarations to "den Grooten Heere tot Constantinopelen" (the Great Lord at Constantinople) so that the Ottoman Sultan might know who the guilty parties were, should complaints about the Dutch reach him — a striking instance of the Company's awareness of the wider Islamic diplomatic context of its Atjeh trade.

## The Soury embassy of 1641

The most fully documented Company embassy to Atjeh is [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury's mission of 1641]] — the *Commissaris* [[Pieter Soury|Pieter Soury]]'s legation to congratulate the [[Sultana of Atjeh (1641)|Sultana]] on her accession after the death of Sultan [[Iskander Tsani]], and to deliver the late Sultan's outstanding order of jewels cut to [[Atjehsche mode|Atjeh-style]]. The embassy was sent by the *Hooge Regeering* (High Government) at Batavia, seen off by the *extra-ordinaris Raad van Indië* [[Joan Maetsuycker|Joan Maetsuycker]] and by [[Justus Schouten|Justus Schouten]], and was the most elaborate VOC diplomatic mission to Atjeh in the seventeenth century.

## The Company's doctrine of ceremonial

The Company's diplomatic doctrine, as Godee Molsbergen describes it, was to give native princes the full honours they claimed while simultaneously exacting the respect indispensable to the Company's own prestige: "De Oost-Indische Compagnie was gewoon aan de Inlandsche vorsten en vorstinnen ten volle de eer te geven waarop ze aanspraak maakten, maar ze wist tegelijk voor zichzelf den eerbied af te dwingen, die onontbeerlijk was voor haar aanzien en succes." A letter of the Gouverneur-Generaal and the *Raden* was to be considered something of very great value; such a token of friendship from the powerful body — which could trade benevolently but also punish severely where it judged necessary — was to be delivered and received with reverent seriousness. The full ceremonial of [[Atjehsche ambassade ceremony (1 July 1641)|1 July 1641]] is the practical application of this doctrine.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the VOC is the principal European institutional interlocutor of the sultanate from [[1602]] until the Company's dissolution in [[1799]]. Its institutional presence in Atjeh — through the [[Dutch lodge at Atjeh|lodge]], the *Opperhoofden*, and the periodic embassies — defined the structure of Dutch-Atjeh relations through the long era of female rule that began in [[1641]], through the subsequent sultanates, and into the eighteenth century. The Company's ceremonial doctrine, its commercial ambitions (pepper, jewels, textiles), and its strategic concern with the wider Islamic diplomatic context all shaped the relationship that Soury's embassy so richly documents.
