**Batakkerk** — Dutch for "Batak Church", the indigenous Protestant church of the Batak lands of northern Sumatra — is named in Dr. [[K. J. Brouwer|Brouwer]]'s chapter *De invloed der protestantsche zending* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as one of the indigenous (*inheemsche*) churches of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Dutch East Indies]] that had achieved self-governing status by the late-colonial period. The church, founded in [[1930]], had by [[1946]] some 430,000 members — making it, with the Minahassakerk (300,000) and the Moluksche kerk (180,000), one of the three largest indigenous Protestant churches of the Indies.

## Batakkerk's outreach to Atjeh

Brouwer's chapter records one specific Atjeh-relevant fact about the Batakkerk: its missionary outreach extended "through its swarming members across the border of Atjeh" (*de Batakkerk werft door zijn uitzwermende leden tot over de grens van Atjeh*). That is, Batak Christians — lay members of the Batakkerk — migrated as evangelists into the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] borderlands, carrying their Protestant faith with them into a region that was otherwise, in the volume's account, almost uniformly Muslim. The mention is brief but significant: it is one of the few references in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* to a Christian presence (however small) in or adjacent to Atjeh.

## Place among the indigenous churches

Brouwer's list of indigenous churches that had achieved self-governing status by 1940 — with their founding dates and approximate memberships — runs:

- Batakkerk (1930): 430,000 members
- Oost Java kerk (1931): 35,000
- Midden Java kerk (1933): 20,000
- Minahassakerk (1934): 300,000
- West Java kerk (1935): 5,000
- Dajakkerk (1935): 10,000
- Moluksche kerk (1936): 180,000
- Niaskerk (1938): 130,000

The Batakkerk is by far the largest of these indigenous churches, and the only one whose missionary outreach extended across the border into Atjeh.

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the Batakkerk's significance is the documentation of a small but real Christian presence in the Atjeh borderlands, established not by foreign missionaries but by lay Batak Christians migrating into the region. The Batak–Atjeh religious frontier — between the Christian (and part-animist) Batak lands to the south-east and the Muslim Atjeh sultanate to the north-west — is a recurring feature of the volume's account of northern Sumatra; the Batakkerk's "swarming" lay evangelists are the clearest twentieth-century institutional expression of that frontier.
