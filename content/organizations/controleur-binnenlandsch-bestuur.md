**The Controleur 2e klasse bij het Binnenlandsch Bestuur** — also referred to as *Controleur der XXVI Moekims (wd.)*, *Controleur der XXII Moekims (wd.)*, or *Controleur te Oleh-leh* — was the Dutch colonial civil administrative officer of the inland administration (*Binnenlandsch Bestuur*) charged with the day-to-day oversight of one of the Atjeh mukim-clusters, the maintenance of relations with the *adathoofden* and *oeloebalang*, and the gathering of political and military intelligence. In the spring of [[1896]] the office existed in several territorial variants — the wd. (*waarnemend*, acting) controleur der XXVI Moekims held by 1e Lt. M. Neelmeijer, the wd. controleur der XXII Moekims held by 1e Lt. K. A. Vosmaer, and the controleur te Oleh-leh (mentioned in [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[First Letter of Teuku Umar (Bijlage II)|first letter]] as the alleged source of insults). The Controleur 2e kl. Gisolf of the Binnenlandsch Bestuur was the officer who, before [[29 March]] [[1896]], warned the general about Umar's "more than suspicious attitude" — a warning that was ignored, with consequences that defined the spring [[1896]] campaign.

## Background and Formation

The Inland Administration of the Netherlands East Indies was the civil-administrative branch that governed the indigenous territories through a hierarchy of European officials — *Resident*, *assistent-resident*, *controleur*, and (in Atjeh) the *controleur* of a specific mukim-cluster — working alongside the indigenous *adathoofden* and *oeloebalang*. The literally "controller" was the lowest-ranking European official with territorial responsibility, typically a young official of the second class (*2e klasse*) or an acting (*waarnemend*, wd.) officer seconded from the military. In [[Aceh War|Atjeh]], where the Dutch territorial control was still partial and contested, the *controleur* of a mukim-cluster was both administrator and intelligence officer, responsible for reporting on the disposition and intentions of the local *adathoofden* and for maintaining the political relationships on which Dutch influence depended. The offices of *wd. controleur der XXVI Moekims* and *wd. controleur der XXII Moekims* were territorial variants for the [[Sagi der XXVI Moekims|XXVI]] and [[Sagi der XXII Moekims|XXII Moekims]] respectively; the *controleur te Oleh-leh* served the port and civil station of [[Oleh-leh]].

## Role in the Atjeh War

The Controleur 2e kl. Gisolf of the Binnenlandsch Bestuur was the officer who, before [[29 March]] [[1896]], warned Gen.-Maj. C. Deykerhoff about [[Teuku Umar]]'s "more than suspicious attitude". Despite Gisolf's warning, Umar had "*the audacity*" (the brass) to attend a conference at the Governor's office on [[28 March]] [[1896]]; the Governor, "*in any case little inclined to give credence to the various reports*" (anyway little inclined to give credence to the various reports), was thereby so convinced of Umar's good faith that he did not even consider the advice of his subordinates to arrest the Panglima Prang besar. The *wd. controleur der XXVI Moekims* — held by 1e Lt. M. Neelmeijer — was the officer ordered, on the night of [[28 April]]–[[29 April]] [[1896]], to march from [[Boekit Karang]] by night and surprise the *benting Batoe* in the XIII Moekims Toengkoeb (the [[Night Surprise of Benting Batoe (28–29 April 1896)|Night Surprise of Benting Batoe]]), to punish Toekoe Tjoet Toengkoeb for his defection. Two officers and 6 brigades [[Korps Marechaussée|marechaussée]] (103 *minderen* + 24 *dwangarbeiders* + 6 *tandoes*) marched at 11 PM; Sgt. Panman (No. 21620) opened the first gate from inside; Sgt. Vogel (No. 22210) opened the second, killing Leh-Oe; houses were burned; seven Atjehers were killed trying to break out; the patrol returned with two lightly wounded, capturing one repeteerkarabijn, one voorlaadgeweer, and *blanke wapens*. The *wd. controleur der XXII Moekims* — held by 1e Lt. K. A. Vosmaer — was the recipient of intelligence reports on enemy losses and was stationed with the XXII Moekims at this period. The *controleur te Oleh-leh* was mentioned in [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[First Letter of Teuku Umar (Bijlage II)|first letter of 30 March 1896]] as the alleged source of insults that Umar cited among his (fabricated) reasons for defection. The Controleur 2e kl. Gisolf is also recorded as having discussed Umar's operation with him and as having warned the governor about Umar (along with the waarnemend assistent-resident).

## Composition and Strength

The Controleur 2e klasse bij het Binnenlandsch Bestuur in Atjeh in the spring of [[1896]] included: Controleur 2e kl. Gisolf (whose specific territorial assignment is not given in the source); wd. controleur der XXVI Moekims held by 1e Lt. M. Neelmeijer; wd. controleur der XXII Moekims held by 1e Lt. K. A. Vosmaer; the controleur te Oleh-leh (unnamed); and a also unnamed who, alongside Gisolf, warned the governor about Umar. Other controleurs are named in connection with the political attendant Toekoe Gih (sent to the VI Moekims on [[14 April]] [[1896]]), the Imam of Daroe (summoned by the assistent-resident), and the various intelligence reports received by Vosmaer. The indigenous political attendant Mohamad Arief is also recorded in connection with the office's intelligence-gathering work.

## Significance

The Controleur 2e klasse bij het Binnenlandsch Bestuur was, in the spring of [[1896]], the lowest-level European official with territorial responsibility in Atjeh, and as such the officer most directly exposed to the realities of Dutch-Atjeh political relations. Gisolf's ignored warning to Gen.-Maj. C. Deykerhoff about [[Teuku Umar]]'s "more than suspicious attitude" on the eve of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|29 March 1896 defection]] is one of the most poignant *what-ifs* of the Atjeh campaign: had the Governor heeded his controleur's warning and arrested Umar at the [[28 March]] [[1896]] conference, the spring [[1896]] crisis — and the dismissal of Deykerhoff himself — might have been averted. The *wd. controleur der XXVI Moekims* Neelmeijer's command of the [[Night Surprise of Benting Batoe (28–29 April 1896)|Night Surprise of Benting Batoe]] illustrates the operational role that controleurs were called upon to play in the counter-insurgency campaign, beyond their formal civil-administrative duties.
