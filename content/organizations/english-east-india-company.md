**The English East India Company (EIC)** — chartered by Queen Elizabeth I on [[31 December]] [[31 December]] [[1600]] as the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies" — was the principal English commercial instrument in the East Indies for two and a half centuries and the chief European rival of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] in the early-modern Malay-Indonesian archipelago. The EIC's first voyage under [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] produced the [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first English visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval operation in the East Indies]] on [[3 October]] [[1602]]. The EIC is the principal English institution of Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The charter of 31 December 1600

The EIC was chartered by Queen Elizabeth I on [[31 December]] [[1600]] — fifteen months before the [[Founding of the VOC (1602)|founding of the VOC on 20 March 1602]]. The charter granted the company a monopoly of English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan for fifteen years, with the right to make treaties, build fortifications, and maintain armed forces. The charter's terms were broadly similar to those of the later VOC charter, and reflected the same early-modern European practice of state-backed commercial monopoly.

## The first voyage and the Atjeh visit (1601-1603)

The EIC's first voyage, under [[James Lancaster|Sir James Lancaster]], left [[Woolwich|Woolwich]] on [[13 February]] [[1601]] with four ships — *Hector*, *Susan*, *Ascension* and *Red Dragon*. The fleet reached the [[Cape of Good Hope|Cape]] in April [[1601]], the [[Nicobaren|Nicobaren]] later that year, and [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[5 June]] [[1602]]. The [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|Atjeh visit]] is the principal English episode of Van der Woude's Atjeh chapter: Lancaster was received with great ceremony by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]], praised Queen Elizabeth, exchanged gifts transported by caparisoned elephants, and obtained leave to trade; he found the pepper prices higher than expected and his capital insufficient for a full cargo; he conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese carrack from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]]; he was joined by [[Joris van Spilbergen|Joris van Spilbergen]]'s Dutch fleet; and on [[3 October]] [[1602]] the combined Anglo-Dutch squadron [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|captured the Portuguese carrack]] in the Strait of Malaka.

## The Anglo-Dutch partnership and its dissolution

The [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|capture of the Portuguese carrack on 3 October 1602]] was the high-water mark of the early Anglo-Dutch commercial partnership in the East Indies — the brief moment in which the EIC and the VOC co-operated against the Portuguese rather than competing with each other. The partnership would dissolve within a few years as the commercial rivalry between the two companies intensified: by [[1609]] [[William Keeling|William Keeling]] would be writing from [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] that the Bandanese "do much marvel that in so long a time there have come no English shipping there", and the Anglo-Dutch rivalry would harden into the conflicts of the [[1610s]] and the [[Ambonese murder|Ambonese murder of 1623]]. The EIC's role in the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship was thus both co-operative (at Atjeh in [[1602]]) and competitive (at Banda after [[1609]]) — a duality that would define the Anglo-Dutch East India relationship for the next century.

## Significance

The English East India Company is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal English institution of the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship and the chief European rival of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] in the early-modern Malay-Indonesian archipelago. The [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|first EIC visit to Atjeh]] in June [[1602]] and the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|joint Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese carrack of 3 October 1602]] were the principal instances of the early Anglo-Dutch commercial partnership in the East Indies — the brief moment of co-operation before the rivalry of the [[1610s]] and [[1620s]]. The EIC's role in the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship frames the Anglo-Dutch East India relationship as both co-operative and competitive, and anticipates the conflicts that would define the Anglo-Dutch East India relationship for the next century. The EIC's later role at [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — through the [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh|Captain Canning mission of 1814]] and the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission|Raffles-Coombs commission of 1819]] — would also frame the British diplomatic interest in Atjeh that would continue through the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog of 1873-1903]].
