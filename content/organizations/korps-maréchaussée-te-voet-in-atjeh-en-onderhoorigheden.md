The **Korps Maréchaussée te voet in Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden** ("Corps of Maréchaussée on foot in Atjeh and Dependencies") — known in the jubilee literature as *De Atjehsche Garde*, the Atjehnese Guard — was the elite dismounted constabulary and light-infantry corps of the [[Oost-Indisch Leger|Netherlands-Indies army]] established by *Gouvernementsbesluit* of April [[1890]] and celebrated at its fortieth anniversary on [[2 April]] [[1930]] with the grant of a *vaandel* (colour) by [[Wilhelmina of the Netherlands|Queen Wilhelmina]]. This article records the account of the corps given in the [[1930]] jubilee number of *[[Het Nieuws van den Dag voor Nederlandsch-Indië]]*, *[[Het Korps Maréchaussée in Atjeh (Jubileum-nummer 1930)|Het Korps Maréchaussée in Atjeh]]*.

## Foundation and original purpose

The corps was instituted by *Gouvernementsbesluit* of April [[1890]], under the further approval of the King, with the intention of exercising police work within the *[[Geconcentreerde linie|geconcentreerde stelling]]* (concentrated position) of [[Groot-Atjeh]], and was originally destined to hand that task over to the civil administration as soon as conditions should permit. The immediate provocation was the state of affairs after the *[[Concentratiestelsel|concentratie]]* decision of [[19 August]] [[1884]]: Dutch troops had been thrown back into a defensive attitude, "kleine benden slopen de linie binnen, vielen onze convooien aan en vernielden kunstwerken" — small bands crept inside the line, attacked the convoys and destroyed engineering works. *Overvallen* (surprise attacks) occurred "met schier verbijsterende regelmaat" — with almost bewildering regularity. To end them, the carrying of weapons by the Atjehnese population was made a punishable offence and a *[[Passenstelsel|passenstelsel]]* (pass system) introduced; but the evil was not thereby overcome, and a separate corps was raised.

The jubilee editorial observed that the corps had grown far beyond that original design, its indispensable existence now fixed in the right to carry a colour.

## Recruitment and the mark of distinction

The corps had to be recruited from officers, non-commissioned officers and "de beste niet-Europeesche manschappen van het Leger" — the best non-European rank and file of the army. Placement with the Maréchaussée "is een onderscheiding geweest en is dat gebleven" — had been and remained a distinction. A European soldier had first to earn the rank of non-commissioned officer, and even then his admission to the ranks of the Guard depended on his military record.

Mosselman recorded that entry was entirely voluntary: every supplementary requisition on the infantry corps carried the words "geschikt en… genegen" — fit and willing. In that word *genegen*, he wrote, lay not only the freedom to join or not — since the regular soldier had otherwise only to go where he was sent — but also the recognition by the command that more would be asked of a *maréchaussée* than could be expected of the mass, and that the greatest exertion and the greatest sacrifices might be demanded at any moment. Whoever could not or would not give that "deed beter weg te blijven" — did better to stay away.

## The first establishment

Only one *divisie* was at first designated. It consisted of a captain-commandant, one first lieutenant, a European sergeant-major, a European *fourier* (quartermaster-sergeant), twelve European sergeants, twelve non-European sergeants, twelve non-European corporals, and 180 non-European *maréchaussées*. Of these it was required that they should make themselves acquainted with the language and the *[[Adat|adat]]* (customary law) of the country, in order to win confidence and obtain reliable intelligence — so far as that was possible in a region where the population that had submitted was in league with the enemy.

## From police work to field operations

From [[1890]] to [[1896]] the corps worked within the concentrated line, performing police duties and also administrative work: it laid out registers of the kind kept by the *[[Binnenlandsch Bestuur|B.B.]]*, recording possessions and trades carried on, so that the administration might extend its paternal care over the population. From [[1896]] the *maréchaussées* also entered the purely military field and sought out the enemy in his hiding places. The jubilee number credited this decisive extension to the then captain of Maréchaussée, Jhr. [[G. J. W. C. Graafland]], who "had het initiatief daartoe genomen en voerde het met ontembare energie uit" — had taken the initiative and carried it out with untameable energy; his name accordingly stood at the head of the list of knighted *maréchaussées*.

The [[Defection of Teukoe Oemar (1896)|defection of Teukoe Djohan, alias Oemar]] in [[1896]] compelled the government of Atjeh to abandon the system of *concentratie* adopted in [[1884]]: the Dutch now faced the man whom they had themselves supplied with weapons and ammunition, and who forced them to resume the war they had broken off in 1884.

## Duties

An extensive task was assigned to the corps. Its sphere of work "strekte zich uit over alles, dat de orde en rust zou kunnen verstoren" — extended over everything that might disturb order and peace — and included the prevention of smuggling in foodstuffs and pepper, which had arisen because the coasts of enemy territory were closed by warships. It took part in all military operations of any importance and waged a guerrilla against the Atjehnese, who sought to maintain the war against the Dutch in the same manner.

The *maréchaussées* received the most dangerous and difficult assignments: "het afbijten van de spits" (biting off the point, that is taking the lead), the carrying out of *omtrekkingen* (turning movements), nocturnal surprise attacks and the like. The field battalions found in the corps a particular support that could not be valued highly enough.

## Growth of the establishment

The corps found ever more recognition and was repeatedly enlarged, until by *Gouvernementsbesluit* of [[3 March]] [[1899]] its strength was brought to 20 officers and 1,212 men, divided over five *divisies* of three *afdeelingen*, subdivided into four *[[Brigade (Maréchaussée)|brigades]]* each. Each division stood under the orders of a captain-division commander; so far as military administration was concerned, the corps was placed under the command of a field officer, for which post the *plaatselijk commandant* at [[Koeta Radja]] was soon designated.

An annual sum was placed at the disposal of the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur|Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh and Dependencies]] with which to reward *maréchaussées* who had excelled, in order to foster initiative. Officers attached to the corps were carried *à la suite* of their own arm, and the commanders of divisions and *afdeelingen*, where circumstances required it, were charged with the administration as *civiel-gezaghebber* (civil authority).

## Stations

In [[1898]] the corps was localised as follows: Staff at [[Koeta Radja]]; 1st Division at [[Seulimeum|Selimoen]]; 2nd Division at [[Indrapoeri]]; 3rd Division at [[Tjot Mantjang]] and [[Glé Broet]]; 4th Division at [[Tjot Goet]]; and 5th Division at [[Lam Baroe]] and [[Lho Nga|Lho'-Nga]].

In [[1910]] the following stations were assigned: 1st Division at [[Blang Kedjeren]]; 2nd Division at [[Meulaboh]]; 3rd Division at [[Takengeun|Takèngan]]; 4th Division at [[Lam Meulo]]; and 5th Division at [[Lho Soekon]], where it was still quartered in [[1930]].

## Pay, exemptions and uniform

Members of the corps were paid extra. Captains received the same salary as their equals in rank on the General Staff, lieutenants as their equals in the arm of the Engineers, and *onderluitenants* the same pay as second lieutenants in that arm, while a separate scale of pay was created for the non-commissioned officers and men.

Exempt from garrison duties, the *maréchaussées* ran their own *menage* (mess). In dress they were distinguished from other soldiers by a facing consisting of a trimming on both corners of the collar — the so-called *bloedvingers* ("blood-fingers") — and a flat curl on the sleeves, of gold for the officers and of orange cloth for the lower ranks.

## Armament

They were armed with a *[[Karabijn (M. 95)|karabijn]]* — at first the *[[Beaumont-karabijn|Beaumont]]*, later the *[[Mannlicher-karabijn|Mannlicher]]* — and with the *[[Klewang|klewang]]*, the specific weapon of the Atjehnese, with which the Atjehnese was now himself fought.

## Corps spirit

The chief factor in the corps's success was, in the editorial's judgement, "de uitstekende korpsgeest" — the outstanding corps spirit which distinguished the Maréchaussée from the other arms. Continual companionship during the most perilous moments had created a fraternisation, which the officers cultivated and promoted in their leadership. The corps's own motto, recorded by Mosselman, was "Eén voor allen, allen voor één" — one for all, all for one — and only two words were allowed to have value: *Doel* (objective) and *Bereiken* (to reach it). *Onmogelijk* — impossible — did not exist: "je deed, of bezweek bij de poging" — you did it, or you perished in the attempt.

## Later history

In [[1914]] the Maréchaussée became a part of the arm of the Infantry, and to that circumstance the jubilee editorial attributed the fact that its banner would correspond to that of "de Koningin van het Slagveld" — the Queen of the Battlefield. There followed a period of unbroken pursuit of the remaining resistance fighters who had chosen hiding places in the wastelands, and alongside it a consolidating task: what had been won had to be retained at any price.

The quiet that set in after [[1914]] led the government to reduce the corps in [[1925]] to 18 officers and 1,072 men — a reduction in which, the editorial remarked, the policy of *versobering* (austerity) must undoubtedly have had a hearty word to say. After the [[Westkust Resistance (1925-1927)|Westkust action]] had again demonstrated its indispensability, the corps was towards the end of [[1927]] once more raised to 23 officers and 1,214 men, and a new division was formed with [[Bakongan]] as its station.

## Casualties

In the period [[1890]]–[[1930]], the jubilee number recorded, approximately 950 members of the corps fell on the field of battle and about 250 were wounded.

## Significance

The jubilee number claimed for the corps a significance exceeding its purely military value. By continual patrolling it had, in the editors' words, kept the *djahats* (evil-doers) back from intended misdeeds; and precisely because so little was known of that part of its work — "de massa spitst zich op wapenfeiten" (the crowd fastens on feats of arms) — the fulfilment of it had given the corps its inner meaning for the pacification. Colonel [[L. A. Snell]] pointed in the same issue to the corps's work of peace: the numerous roads that traversed Atjeh and the many engineering works it had constructed, and the order and quiet created in "de vroeger door onderlinge veeten van elkander gescheiden staatjes" — the little states formerly divided from one another by mutual feuds.
