The **Militaire Willemsorde** (English: *Military Order of William*) — abbreviated **M.W.O.** — was the highest Dutch chivalric order for military merit, instituted by King [[William I of the Netherlands]] in [[1815]] for "*Moed, Beleid en Trouw*" (Courage, Judgment and Loyalty) in the field. The order was awarded to members of the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]] and the [[Royal Netherlands East Indies Army|K.N.I.L.]] for distinguished conduct in campaigns across the [[Netherlands Indies|Dutch East Indies]], including the battles for [[Palembang]] on [[20 June]] [[1821]] and [[24 June]] [[1821]], the [[Padri War]], the [[Aceh War]], and the related colonial pacifications of the early 20th century. It was the decoration most prized by the soldiers of the K.N.I.L. and most proudly worn in the *negorij* (village) or *kampong* (hamlet) of the [[Ambon|Ambonese]] and [[Minahasa|Menadonese]] soldiers.

## The four classes and the *riddersoldij*

The M.W.O. had four classes:

- **Fourth Class** (*Ridder 4e klas*) — awarded for notable bravery in the field, such as the Palembang engagements; the decoration of the rank-and-file *soldaat* (private) and *onderofficier* (non-commissioned officer).
- **Third Class** (*Ridder 3e klas*) — also a *Ridder* (Knight) rank; reserved for distinguished junior leadership.
- **Commandeur** (Commander) — for senior officers.
- **Grootkruis** (Grand Cross) — the highest class, for the most extraordinary leadership.

The Ridder M.W.O. also received a *riddersoldij* — an additional pay — and in [[1869]] it was *bepaald* (determined) that he would wear the decoration at the same *lint* (ribbon) as the higher classes. The order's citations often referenced specific dates and locations, linking individual valour to broader colonial objectives.

## The earliest indigenous *ridders*

The M.W.O. was opened to indigenous soldiers in the mid-19th century. In [[1865]] the M.W.O. 4e klas was *verleend* (bestowed) on the Ambonese *1e luitenant* (first lieutenant) [[Joseph Rakarias]] "*ter vervanging van de hem in 1850 verleende*" *Medaille voor Moed en Trouw* (Medal for Courage and Loyalty) — that is, as a replacement for the medal he had been given earlier, when the M.W.O. was extended to indigenous soldiers. The replacement was a recognition that the earlier medal had been inadequate to the act it recognised. After [[1865]] the M.W.O. became the standard decoration for indigenous soldiers who had shown *Moed, Beleid en Trouw* in the field. The book lists a sequence of 19th-century indigenous *ridders*, including the Bantam-M.W.O. and the [[1828]] and [[1839]] decorations.

## Marine Corps recipients in the East Indies

Recipients listed in the marine corps history include marines who earned the fourth-class distinction for actions in [[Palembang]] (see [[1822]]) and later for engagements in the [[Padri War]] and other Sumatra-wide operations. The order's citations often referenced specific dates and locations, linking individual valour to broader colonial objectives. The Fourth Class was awarded for notable bravery in the field; the higher classes were reserved for officers and extraordinary leadership.

## The Aceh War *ridders* and KNIL officers

During the [[Aceh War]], several officers were appointed to the 4th class for distinguished conduct in riverine operations, including:

- [[P. J. graaf van Hogendorp]]
- [[N. A. van Blom]] (variant: *vau Blom*)
- [[D. van Peppel]]

These were decorated for actions such as the **Tamiang** expeditions of [[1893]].

The bulk of the *Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.* account's M.W.O. *ridders* earned their decorations in the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and the related colonial pacifications of the early 20th century:

- [[Salawono]] earned his 4e klas at the [[Storming of Limboer (22 June 1903)|storming of Limboer]] in [[Djambi]] on [[22 June]] [[1903]] and his 3e klas on [[Celebes]] for the *Verovering van de Alla-stellingen (Celebes, 1905–1908)* (Conquest of the Alla positions).
- [[Nussy]] earned his 3e klas for the *colonne Schmidt* campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes of [[1910]]–[[1911]].
- [[Schmidt]] received the M.W.O. 3e klas for [[1911]] (and the *[[Eresabel]]* (Honorary Sabre) for [[1910]]).

## The *slippendragers* at Thenu's funeral

When [[Dominee Thenu]] died on [[11 May]] [[1937]], the four *slippendragers* (train-bearers) at his funeral were "*vier oude, gepensioneerde Marechaussees, alle vier ridders M.W.O.*" (four old, retired Marechaussees, all four Knights M.W.O.): [[Salawono]] (3e kl.), [[Pattiasina]], [[Kazen]] and [[Pattipuluhu]]. The choice of four M.W.O. *ridders* to carry the dominee's bier is the book's most resonant image of the moral economy of the [[Korps Marechaussee van Atjeh en Onderhorigen|Marechaussee]]: the men who had been decorated for courage under fire laid their decorations, in effect, at the feet of the man who had blessed them.

## The *Ridders M.W.O.* in the *negorij*

"*Ridders M.W.O.*, dragers van de [[Medaille voor Moed en Trouw]] en van het [[Kroontje (Eervolle Vermelding)|Kroontje]] (de Eervolle Vermelding), waren de trots van hun *kampong* of *negorij*" (Knights M.W.O., bearers of the Medal for Courage and Loyalty and of the Crown (Honourable Mention), were the pride of their hamlet or village). The M.W.O. Ridder was the pride of his home community: the *negorij* tracked his *wedervaren* (fortunes) through the entire Archipelago, and his decoration was worn at the *Koninginnedag* (Queen's Day) *rondgang* (procession). The [[1938]] incident at [[Ambarawa]] — in which the author congratulated a retired Javanese fusilier on 30 years as Ridder M.W.O. on behalf of the *Legercommandant* (Army Commander) — is the book's example of the decoration's lifelong prestige.

## Connection to Aceh

While the order was a Dutch imperial honour, its conferral on actions taken in [[Sumatra]] reflects the intertwined histories of Dutch colonial expansion and the resistance of the [[Sultanate of Aceh]]. The recognition of marine and K.N.I.L. personnel underscores the importance the Dutch placed on securing the island's northern and southern regions.

## Significance

The Militaire Willemsorde served both as a morale booster for Dutch forces and as a diplomatic signal of the Crown's commitment to its overseas territories, including Aceh. In *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* the M.W.O. is the central decoration of the book's moral economy: the *Ridders M.W.O.* are the *trots* (pride) of their *negorij*, the *slippendragers* at the dominee's funeral, and the figures through whom Dames makes his argument about the colonial army's recognition of courage. The book's quiet point is that the M.W.O. was the colonial army's most reliable instrument for recognising the actual military value of its Ambonese soldiers — a value the formal classification system could not measure. Its recipients' deeds are documented in regimental histories such as those examined here.
