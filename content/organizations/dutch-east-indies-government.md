**Dutch East Indies Government** (Dutch: *Gouvernement van Nederlandsch-Indië*; abbreviated *N.I. Gouvernement*) was the colonial administration that ruled the Indonesian archipelago from the early [[19th century|nineteenth century]] until the mid-[[20th century|twentieth century]], exercising sovereignty over [[Sumatra]], [[Java]], [[Borneo]], the [[Moluccas]] and the other islands of the East Indian archipelago from its seat at [[Batavia]] and acting, in the context of [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Aceh]], as the metropolitan authority that invoked the obligations of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of 1824]] to guarantee safe navigation in Acehnese waters and to suppress piracy. The government's dual strategy of regulating Western trade and attempting to "civilise" the Acehnese while maintaining a hard line against "piracy" and "raids" by local chieftains ended in the [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1904]]) and the protracted military and administrative consolidation that followed under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]]. The N.I. Gouvernement's legal foundation was the [[Regeringsreglement van Nederlands-Indië|Government Regulation for the Netherlands Indies (Dutch: *Regeringsreglement van Nederlands-Indië*)]], successively revised in [[1818|1818]], [[1825|1825]], [[1836|1836]] and [[1854|1854]], and its constitutional successor after [[1903|1903]] was the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies state]] of the [[Ethische Politiek|Ethical Policy]] period.

## Origins and the post-VOC inheritance

The Dutch East Indies Government was the institutional successor of the [[Dutch East India Company|Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC)]] whose charter had expired in [[1799|1799]] and whose territories had passed, after the Napoleonic interregnum and the [[1817|1817]] convention of London, to the restored Kingdom of the [[Netherlands]]. The government was constituted by the [[Regeringsreglement van Nederlands-Indië|Regeringsreglement]] of [[1818|1818]] — later revised in [[1825|1825]], [[1836|1836]] and [[1854|1854]] (the last as the *Regeeringsreglement van 1854* that would remain in force until the [[20th century|twentieth century]]) — and was headed by a [[Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies|Governor-General (Dutch: *Gouverneur-Generaal*)]] resident at [[Batavia]] who exercised both civil and military authority over the archipelago. Under the Governor-General served a hierarchy of *residenten*, *assistent-residenten* and *controleurs* who administered the *gewesten* (residencies) into which the colony was divided, and an advisory [[Raad van Indië|Council of the Indies (Dutch: *Raad van Indië*)]] whose consent was required for major legislation. The Atjeh territories, after their incorporation following the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], were administered as the *gewest* of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] under a [[Civiel- en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh|Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh]], a special arrangement that reflected the still-military character of the administration in the northern tip of Sumatra.

## The Treaty of 1824 and the road to the Aceh War

The Anglo-Dutch settlement of [[17 March|17 March]] [[1824|1824]] — the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of London]] — placed the whole of [[Sumatra]] within the Dutch sphere of influence in exchange for the Dutch cession of [[Benkoelen|Bencoolen]] and the British abandonment of the Indian factories, but left the status of the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acehnese sultanate]] ambiguous under a clause that the Dutch plenipotentiaries [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] and [[Captain Elout|Elout]] had treated as a mere bagatelle. For half a century the N.I. Gouvernement treated Atjeh as an independent sovereignty with which it maintained diplomatic relations, but the [[1871|1871]] [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-tractaat]] — by which the Netherlands ceded the Dutch Gold Coast in West Africa and recognised British rights in northern Borneo in exchange for the British release of the Acheh reservation — dissolved this ambiguity and made Atjeh a Dutch responsibility. The subsequent Acehnese negotiations with the Italian and American consuls in [[Singapore]] provided the *casus belli*, and on [[26 March|26 March]] [[1873|1873]] the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|Dutch declaration of war]] invoked the obligations of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of 1824]] to guarantee safe navigation in Acehnese waters and to suppress piracy. The failure of the dual strategy of regulation and "civilising" — combined with the indecisiveness of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[1884]]–[[1896]] — produced the protracted conflict that ended only after the decisive campaigns of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]] in the early twentieth century.

## The Van Heutsz era and the administrative consolidation

The appointment of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] as [[Civiel- en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh|Civil and Military Governor of Atjeh]] in [[1898|1898]] — and his subsequent promotion to [[Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies|Governor-General]] in [[1904|1904]] — marked the strategic and administrative turn that ended the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Under Van Heutsz the N.I. Gouvernement abandoned the *concentratie* of the [[1884|1884]]–[[1896|1896]] period in favour of the vigorous offensive recommended by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] and broke the organised, general Acehnese resistance through the systematic extension of Dutch authority into the [[Gajolanden|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]] and the [[Colonne van Daalen 1904|colonne van Daalen of 1904]]. The administrative instruments of the consolidation — the [[korte verklaring|korte Atjèhverklaring]] (short declaration) by which the *uleebalang* acknowledged Dutch suzerainty, the *Instructie* of [[7 May|7 May]] [[1902|1902]] on *heerendiensten* (corvée labour), the *muntzuivering* (currency reform) of [[1908|1908]], and the [[Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen|Atjèh-tram]] connection with the Deli-spoorweg completed on [[29 December|29 December]] [[1919|1919]] — were the work of the N.I. Gouvernement under Van Heutsz and his successors, and the *gewest* of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] became the model for the wider post-[[1902|1902]] extension of Dutch "effective authority" across the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]].

## Infrastructure and economic policy

Following the formal cessation of hostilities in [[1903|1903]], the Dutch East Indies Government oversaw the construction of strategic infrastructure intended to consolidate colonial control and open Aceh to plantation agriculture. The [[Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen|Atjèh railway]] and the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajo Road]] (Dutch: *Gajoweg*) were built to link the coastal capital of [[Koeta-Radja]] with the interior highlands of [[Gajoland|Gajoland]], while the [[Agrarische Wet 1870|Agrarian Law of 1870 (Dutch: *Agrarische Wet 1870*)]] limited land ownership to non-indigenous Europeans and placed indigenous lands under leasehold (Dutch: *erfpacht*). Together these measures reshaped the social and economic fabric of the region, opening the highlands to European plantation capital and the [[Deli-planters Vereeniging|Deli planters]]' rubber, oil-palm and sisal enterprises that would dominate the colonial economy of northern Sumatra until the [[1930s|1930s]] depression. The N.I. Gouvernement's regulation of Western trade — of books, newspapers, and radio — and its attempts to "civilise" the Acehnese through education and infrastructure were the civil counterpart of this economic policy, and the [[Volkslectuur|Balai Pustaka (Dutch: *Volkslectuur*)]] and the government school system were the principal instruments by which the Ethical Policy was implemented in Atjeh.
