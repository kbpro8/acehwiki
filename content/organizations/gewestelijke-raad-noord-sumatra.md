**The Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra** (Regional Council of North Sumatra) was the proposed advisory council for the new [[Noord-Sumatra (proposed gewest)]], discussed during the [[Bestuursreorganisatie Sumatra (1913-1914)]]. The council was envisioned as a key institution in the new governance structure, providing a mechanism for both official and non-official input into regional administration. Its proposed composition and powers were debated at several meetings, with particular attention to how the unique conditions of Atjeh would be accommodated within the council's structure.

## Proposed Composition

The council was to have 19 members, composed of both *ambtelijke* (official) and *niet-ambtelijke* (non-official) *leden* (members). The official members would be drawn from the colonial administrative service, while the non-official members were intended to represent the broader interests of the population and the business community. The precise balance between these two categories was a matter of significant discussion, particularly at the [[Koeta-Radja bestuursvergaderingen (19-20 January 1914)]], where Gouverneur [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. N. A. Swart]] asked whether the *ambtelijk element* (official element) in the council would be overly dominant. This concern reflected a broader anxiety in the colonial service that the proposed new governance structures, while nominally granting greater autonomy, might in practice simply concentrate more power in the hands of the official class, undermining the principles of decentralisatie and zelfbestuur that the reform was supposed to advance.

## The Question of Indigenous Participation

[[G. J. van Dongen]], Controleur of the XXV Moekims en de Zuidelijke Nederzettingen, raised the question of whether Inlandsche ambtenaren (Native officials) could take seats in the Gewestelijke Raad. This was a significant intervention that touched on the fundamental tension in colonial governance between the aspiration to incorporate indigenous voices into the administrative structure and the colonial reluctance to grant indigenous officials full participatory rights in governance. In the context of Atjeh, where the [[Inlandsch Bestuur]] was organized through the [[sagi]]-[[peleebalangschap]]-[[moekimschap]] hierarchy and the training of Inlandsche bestuurskrachten was still in its early stages, the question of whether indigenous officials would be qualified or permitted to serve on the regional council was particularly acute. The minutes do not record a definitive answer to Van Dongen's question, suggesting that the issue remained unresolved at the time of the Koeta-Radja meetings.

## Context in the Broader Reform

The Gewestelijke Raad was part of the broader framework of *bestuursdecentralisatie* (administrative decentralization) that Regeeringscommissaris [[S. de Graaff]] was implementing across the Buitenbezittingen. The concept of regional advisory councils was modeled on similar institutions that had been or were being established in other parts of the Netherlands East Indies, particularly on Java, where the Decentralisatie-Wet (Decentralization Law) of 1903 had created a framework for local and regional councils. However, the application of this model to the Buitenbezittingen—and especially to Atjeh—required significant adaptation, given the absence of the same level of organized local governance that existed on Java. The earlier meetings at Bandoeng (Bandung) had discussed the question of whether an institution with *uitvoerend gezag* (executive authority) should be created alongside the bestuurshoofd (administrative head) of the new gewesten, with some arguing that non-official persons of sufficient standing might not be available or willing to serve.

## Significance

The proposed Gewestelijke Raad Noord-Sumatra represented an important step in the colonial government's attempt to create more participatory governance structures in the outer territories. The debates about its composition—particularly the concerns about official dominance and indigenous participation—revealed the fundamental tensions inherent in the decentralisatie project: the colonial government wanted to be seen as granting autonomy and representation, but it was reluctant to cede real power to non-official or indigenous actors. For Atjeh specifically, the Gewestelijke Raad would have been the first institution through which the territory's population and officials could participate in regional governance beyond the autocratic authority of the Civiel en Militair Gouverneur. Whether this would have represented a genuine expansion of participation or merely a new mechanism for colonial control remained an open question.
