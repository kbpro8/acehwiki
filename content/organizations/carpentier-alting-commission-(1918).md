**Carpentier Alting commission (1918)** — Dutch *Commissie tot herziening van de bestuursgrondslagen in Nederlandsch-Indië* (Commission for the revision of the foundations of government in the Netherlands Indies) — was the commission appointed in [[1918]] under the chairmanship of Prof. Mr. [[J. H. Carpentier Alting]] to draft a revision of the constitutional foundations of the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Dutch East Indies]]. The commission's appointment is recorded in Mr. Dr. [[L. J. J. Caron|Caron]]'s chapter *De bestuursvormen* in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]).

## Background and appointment

According to Caron's account, the Governor-General of the day — in the difficult days at the end of the First World War — found occasion "in November 1918 to address the *Volksraad* (People's Council) and to set out a programme for the development of the central representative body, which would bring fundamental changes in the Indies' forms of government." To give effect to this promise, "in 1918 a commission was appointed under the chairmanship of Prof. Mr. J. H. Carpentier Alting for the revision of the foundations of government in the Netherlands Indies. This commission submitted, with its report, a draft law on the state ordering (*ontwerp-wet op de staatsordening*)."

## Work and afterlife

The Carpentier Alting commission's draft law was to exercise considerable influence on the constitutional revision of [[1922]] in the Netherlands, which in turn shaped the eventual Wet op de Indische Staatsregeling (Law on the Indies State Regulation) of [[1925]]. The 1922 constitutional revision — described by Caron as having introduced, in articles 1 and 63–64, "a very important change in colonial policy" by removing "all obstacles to the transfer of government and legislation to the Indies' representative bodies in the Indies itself" — opened the way for the commission's draft to be realised. After the 1922 revision, "a commission of professors in Leiden formed, which compiled a *proeve van een staatsregeling voor Nederlandsch-Indië*" — the [[Proeve-Oppenheim]], to which [[J. H. Carpentier Alting|Carpentier Alting]] himself also contributed, alongside Oppenheim, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], Van Vollenhoven and Kleintjes, with the Indonesian Mr. [[Oerip Kartodirdjo]] as secretary.

The eventual Wet op de Indische Staatsregeling of [[1925]] did not, in Caron's judgement, live up to the promise of the 1922 constitutional revision. The Dutch parliament, he writes, "changed it in a further reactionary direction. No autonomous administrative policy was given to the Governor-General, but the Governor-General was bound, in the exercise of general government, to the King's instructions." The assignment of the regulation of internal affairs to organs established in the Indies "had not taken place"; in principle, the regulation of internal affairs remained reserved to the mother-country legislator — that is, to the Crown and the States-General. The result was, in Caron's words, "great discontent in the Netherlands Indies," with "a strong echo in the *Volksraad*."

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the Carpentier Alting commission's significance is indirect but real. The constitutional questions the commission was appointed to address — how to revise "the foundations of government" in light of the promise of greater popular representation — were inseparable from the question of how the Atjeh-derived [[Korte Verklaring|*korte verklaring*]] policy of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] should be constitutionalised. The 261 *zelfbesturende landschappen* (self-governing territories) that the *korte verklaring* regulated by [[1946]] — including the small self-governing territories of Atjeh and Timor — were the constitutional object on which the Carpentier Alting commission's work bore.
