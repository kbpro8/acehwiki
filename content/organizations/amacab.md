**Amacab** — the Allied Military Administration Civil Affairs Branch, also written *amamacab* — was the Allied civil-affairs organisation that entered [[Atjèh|Atjèh]] after the [[Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945|Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945]] and was charged, under the leadership of the Chief Co Amacab (Chief Commanding Officer, Allied Military Administration Civil Affairs Branch) and the newly-designated Governor of Sumatra [[A. I. Spits|A. I. Spits]], with the restoration of Dutch civil authority in the former residency. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Amacab as the instrument through which the plans for Allied re-occupation of Sumatra were prepared — and as the apparatus whose failure to materialise beyond [[Sabang|Sabang]], [[Medan|Medan]], [[Padang|Padang]] and [[Palembang|Palembang]] opened the door to the [[1945|1945]]–[[1946|1946]] "social revolution" that destroyed *oelèëbalang* power in Atjèh.

## Origins and structure

Amacab was the civil-affairs arm of the Allied Military Administration established under the South East Asia Command (Sacsea — Supreme Allied Commander South East Asia). In Atjèh, civil affairs were vested in the Dutch administration, "the latter as part of the Amacab (Allied military administration civil affairs branch)," which entered the residency alongside the Dutch navy after the [[Japanese capitulation of 15 August 1945|Japanese capitulation]] in August [[1945]]. The Dutch administration was represented in the person of [[A. I. Spits|A. I. Spits]], who had been designated Governor of Sumatra. "The plans, prepared under the leadership of the Chief Co Amacab (Chief commanding officer allied military administration civil affairs branch), the Governor of Sumatra [[A. I. Spits|A. I. Spits]], had to remain unused" — the *casus belli* of Atjèh's post-war tragedy.

## The first landings at Sabang

The first Allied entry into Atjèhnese territory came at [[Sabang|Sabang]]. "Still in August the English fleet appeared before Sabang and a temporary naval base was established on Sabang. Also the Dutch navy and the Dutch administration, the latter as part of the Amacab, made their entry. The thousands of enterprise workers from Aceh, East Sumatra and Tapanoeli, who on the island had performed slave labor for the Japanese army, were transferred to the mainland, to be sent back to their place of origin through the care of the Japanese authorities." Sabang, which the Japanese had held as a naval base since [[1942]] and which had been liberated by Hr. Ms. *Tromp* in July [[1944]], was the only part of Atjèh where Amacab exercised full civil authority; on the mainland, "the Allied — English — occupation was limited to the places [[Medan|Medan]], [[Padang|Padang]] and [[Palembang|Palembang]]."

## The 1.3.6. force and Major Knottenbelt

For the mainland of Atjèh, Amacab's instrument was the "1.3.6. force," whose reconnaissance party under B. W. Lefrandt — "former receiver at the customs and excise service in [[Sigli|Sigli]]" — was already in the mountainous region of [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] preparing for the airborne phase of the planned invasion when the capitulation came. On [[5 October]] [[1945]] a "party of the 'A.D.C.S. 1.3.6. force'," under Major M. J. Knottenbelt of the Dutch commando troops, arrived in [[Koetaradja|Koetaradja]] by order of Sacsea. Knottenbelt's preliminary final report, reproduced by Piekaar, warned that "the absence of an Allied representative in Aceh can lead to a deterioration of the situation," since "Aceh can be compared to a powder keg, only a spark is needed to make it explode." "Major Knottenbelt was not replaced and an Allied occupation did not materialize."

## The non-occupation and its consequences

The non-materialisation of Amacab beyond Sabang was, in Piekaar's reading, the immediate cause of the [[1945|1945]]–[[1946|1946]] catastrophe. The *oelèëbalangs*, "freed from Japanese pressure, immediately set about to restore their old position of power," and rallied around [[Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah|Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah]] of [[Peusangan|Peusangan]]. But with no Allied force to interpose itself, the Poesa-inspired cadres of the dissolved [[PETA|PETA]] and the survivors of the [[Masubuchi-kikan|Masubuchi-kikan]] seized the opportunity. By December [[1945]] the civil war between the *oelama* party and the *oelèëbalang* class was raging from [[Lam Meuló|Lam Meuló]] to [[Sigli|Sigli]] and [[Bireuën|Bireuën]], ending in the destruction of *oelèëbalang* authority. "A timely Allied occupation," Piekaar concludes, "on which the *oelèëbalangs* had pinned their hopes to the last, could have prevented worse here."
