**Goedjarat** — modern Gujarat, the western coastal region of India opposite the Arabian peninsula — is named in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as the proximate source of the Islamisation of the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] region of northern Sumatra. In the chapter *De invloed van den Islam*, Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]] records that "the Islam, not directly from the holy city of Mekka in Arabia, but via Goedjarat in India, gained its first following in the Indies along the roads of trade." In the chapter *De oudste cultuurstroomen*, Dr. [[F. H. van Naerssen|Van Naerssen]] further specifies that the first Muslim ruler of [[Samudra]] on the [[Pasei|Pase-rivier]] — whose grave dates from [[1297]] — was buried under a tombstone imported "ready-made from [[Cambay]], the chief city of Goedjarat, which itself had only been Islamised since around 1250 and was developing more and more into a Muslim emporium."

## Role in the Islamisation of northern Sumatra

Both Van Naerssen and Kernkamp emphasise the role of Goedjarati Muslim merchants in the early Islamisation of the north Sumatran coast. Van Naerssen's account of the early fourteenth-century Samudra polity notes that "the Muslim founder of the little realm is buried there in 1297. His gravestone is preserved and proves to have been imported ready-made from Cambay, the chief city of Goedjarat, which itself had only been Islamised since around 1250 and was developing more and more into a Muslim emporium. The Islam will have, as everywhere, stimulated the trade expansion." By [[1415]], he adds, Chinese reports confirm that Islam "has already spread over the whole north coast of Sumatra and is also established on the east coast as far as the little realm of [[Aru]] with its own Sultan. As the preserved tombs show, Goedjarat remained here the source of culture."

The same Goedjarati mercantile network that brought the first Muslim tombstones to Samudra and Aru also supplied the early Malay courts with Muslim scholars and merchants. Van Naerssen further notes that the sultan of Samudra, as described by the Arab traveller Ibn Battuta some fifty years after the founder's death (i.e. around the 1340s), "is very interested in Islam and surrounded by jurists, among whom disputes take place. The court goes on Indian model; among the high officials there are also Persians." The Persian presence at the Samudra court — alongside the Goedjarati commercial network — gives the north Sumatran Islam of the fourteenth century a hybrid Gujerati–Persian complexion.

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Goedjarat's significance is foundational: the Atjeh sultanate that succeeded [[Samudra]] as the dominant Muslim power on the north Sumatran coast inherited its Islamic culture from the same Goedjarati–Persian mercantile network that had Islamised Samudra in the late thirteenth century. The Atjeh sultanate's later sixteenth-century conversion of the [[Minangkabau]] — Van Naerssen dates this to "about 1550" — was, on this account, a continuation of the same Goedjarati-mediated Islamisation that had begun at Samudra in the 1290s.
