A gampong from which the learned teacher [[Teungku di Lam Gut]] (proper name Jalaluddin) took his title. He was a pupil of the Malay [[Abduggamad]] and became not only a popular teacher but also kali of the XXVI Mukims. His son inherited his title and dignity but transferred his duties to his son-in-law [[Cheh Marahaban]]. The grandson of the old [[Teungku di Lam Gut]] survives as kali in name only but is consulted by none. In a footnote, Snouck mentions that [[Mohammad Zain bin Jalaluddin]] from [[Acheh]] wrote a Malay treatise on Mohammedan marriage law lithographed at [[Constantinople]] in A.H. 1304, and likely connects with the [[Teungku di Lam Gut]] who in A.H. 1242 wrote the Tambih rapilin (a book of admonition). The cloth pattern 'ija Lam Gugob' also mentions nearby Lam Gugob.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Lam Gut

**Lam Gut** is a gampong in the [[XXVI Mukims]] mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the birthplace and hereditary seat of the chief kali **Teungku Lam Gut**, who held the office of chief religious judge of the XXVI Mukims at the time of the first Dutch invasion of Acheh in [[1873]].

### The Kali Teungku Lam Gut

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Lam Gut in his survey of the religious-judicial hierarchy of the three sagis. He writes: "When the Dutch first came to Acheh, the chief kali of the XXVI Mukims was a man named **Teungku Lam Gut**, the grandfather on the mother's side of the present hatib (preacher) of the principal mosque at Kuta Raja. Born in Lam Gut and appointed as the successor of his father, who had some reputation for learning, he subsequently changed his abode, in consequence of his marriage, to [[Lam Bhu]] close to the Dalam." This narrative illustrates the hereditary character of the office of kali in the three sagis; the son succeeded the father as a matter of course, regardless of his actual learning.

### The Marriage Alliance with Cheh Marahaban

In Lam Bhu, the kali "married his daughter to a scholar of [[Pidie]] named [[Cheh Marahaban]], long a resident in Mecca, who was ulama and kali to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the Sultanate. Teungku Lam Gut was thus able to rely in his official work on the superior knowledge of his son-in-law, for he himself though a man of sound intelligence was devoid of learning." The story of Lam Gut thus illustrates Snouck's recurring theme: the hereditary kali offices of the sultanate had by its closing decades decayed in learning, and their holders were often obliged to lean on more learned sons-in-law or relatives.

### Significance

Although Lam Gut itself appears in Vol. I only as the native place of this one kali, the mention is significant for the religious geography of the [[XXVI Mukims]]. The hereditary office of the chief kali of that sagi was, at the time of the Dutch invasion, attached to the family of Lam Gut, and the family's marital alliance with the scholarly Cheh Marahaban of Pidie linked the lowland XXVI Mukims to the more learned tradition of the east-coast region.
