**Lam Gut** (also spelled **Lam Guha**, **Lam Gutë**) was a *gampong* (village) in the [[XXVI Mukims]] sagi of [[Great Acheh]] in [[Acheh]] Proper, renowned as the hereditary seat of the chief *kali* (religious judge) of the sagi and as the place whose name was borne by the learned teacher [[Teungku di Lam Gut]] (proper name Jalaluddin). The variant spelling **Lam Guha** — possibly reflecting the Sanskrit-derived *guha* (cave) — also attaches to a tradition concerning a Javanese religious teacher from [[Demak]] who secluded himself in a cave for many years and whose tomb behind the [[Ulee Lheue]] mosque became a place of pilgrimage.

## The Hereditary Kali Teungku Lam Gut

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Lam Gut in his survey of the religious-judicial hierarchy of the three sagis in *The Achehnese* Vol. I. He writes: "When the Dutch first came to Acheh, the chief *kali* of the XXVI Mukims was a man named **Teungku Lam Gut**, the grandfather on the mother's side of the present *hatib* (preacher) of the principal mosque at Kuta Raja. Born in Lam Gut and appointed as the successor of his father, who had some reputation for learning, he subsequently changed his abode, in consequence of his marriage, to [[Lam Bhu]] close to the Dalam."

The proper name of [[Teungku di Lam Gut]] was Jalaluddin. He was a pupil of the Malay scholar [[Abduggamad]] and became not only a popular teacher but also the *kali* of the XXVI Mukims. His son inherited his title and dignity but transferred his duties to his son-in-law [[Cheh Marahaban]] of [[Pidie]], a scholar long resident in [[Mecca]] who served as *ulama* and *kali* to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the Sultanate. The grandson of the old Teungku di Lam Gut survived as *kali* in name only and was consulted by none — an illustration of the decay of the hereditary religious offices in the closing decades of the sultanate.

## The Marriage Alliance with Cheh Marahaban

In [[Lam Bhu]], the *kali* "married his daughter to a scholar of Pidie named Cheh Marahaban, long a resident in Mecca, who was *ulama* and *kali* to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the Sultanate. Teungku Lam Gut was thus able to rely in his official work on the superior knowledge of his son-in-law, for he himself though a man of sound intelligence was devoid of learning." The marital alliance linked the lowland XXVI Mukims to the more learned east-coast tradition of Pidie and represents the recurring Achehnese pattern by which hereditary *kalis* of little learning were obliged to lean on more scholarly sons-in-law.

## The Javanese Cave-Dwelling Teacher

A separate Achehnese tradition, recorded by Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* Vol. II, attaches to the variant form **Lam Guha** — a name possibly connected with the Sanskrit *guha* (cave). From this *gampong* the teacher [[Teungku di Lam Guha]] took his name. He was a Javanese from [[Demak]] who secluded himself in a cave for many years. His tomb, which lies behind the mosque at [[Ulee Lheue]], is now visited by many pilgrims.

The two traditions concerning Lam Gut / Lam Guha — the hereditary *kali* line of the XXVI Mukims on the one hand, and the Javanese cave-dwelling teacher of Demak on the other — may either reflect two distinct religious figures associated with the same place across generations, or two slightly variant local traditions. Snouck's text uses the two forms without explicitly equating or distinguishing them.

## The Malay Treatise of Mohammad Zain bin Jalaluddin

In a footnote, Snouck Hurgronje mentions that [[Mohammad Zain bin Jalaluddin]] from [[Acheh]] wrote a Malay treatise on Mohammedan marriage law lithographed at [[Constantinople]] in A.H. 1304, and likely connects with the Teungku di Lam Gut who in A.H. 1242 wrote the *Tambih rapilin* (a book of admonition). The cloth pattern *ija Lam Gugob* also mentions the nearby hamlet of [[Lam Gut|Lam Gugob]].

## Significance

Although Lam Gut itself appears in *The Achehnese* principally as the native place of the chief *kali* of the XXVI Mukims, the mention is significant for the religious geography of the sagi. The hereditary office of the chief *kali* of that sagi was, at the time of the [[Aceh expedition 1873-1876|first Dutch invasion]] in [[1873]], attached to the family of Lam Gut, and the family's marital alliance with the scholarly [[Cheh Marahaban]] of Pidie linked the lowland XXVI Mukims to the more learned tradition of the east-coast region.
