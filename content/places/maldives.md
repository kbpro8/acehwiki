The **Maldives** (Maldivian *Dhivehi Raajje*) is an archipelagic sultanate in the Indian Ocean, south-west of the Indian subcontinent. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, the Maldives enter Snouck Hurgronje's account only as the source of one article of Achehnese diet — the dried stockfish called **keumamaih** — but that article was so indispensable to the Achehnese kitchen that its provenance is recorded more than once.

## Keumamaih: The Stockfish of the Maldives

The staple form of Achehnese food, eaten twice a day, is rice cooked in water. With the rice is taken *gulé* (the Malay *sayur*), of which there are three kinds in common use. The basis of the *gulé* is either dried fish (*eungkot tin*), or *karéng* (small fish of the kinds *bilch* or *aivo*, also dried), or "the stockfish imported from the Maldives (*keumamaih*)," or sliced plantains or brinjals. This stockfish from the Maldives was imported "in large quantities" and prepared in two different ways: *keumamaih cheunichah* (cut up into small pieces and mixed with blimbing, chillis, onions, and serai), and *keumamaih reundang* or *teumeh* (similar ingredients, but fried in oil rather than eaten raw).

## Kanduris, Weddings, and the Comforts of the Sick

At *kanduris* (religious feasts) and at weddings, funeral feasts, receptions of distinguished guests, and other ceremonious occasions, long strips of stockfish boiled in cocoanut milk — called **keumamaih teunaguën** — are added to the glutinous rice and other accompaniments. At a child-birth, the strict dietary observed in Acheh (as in [[Java]]) forbids vegetables and most fish; "no fish except the *eungkot chu'et* and the stockfish (*keumamaih*) from the Maldives," with dry rice and the like. Thus the same article that graced the wedding tray was also the one protein permitted to the lying-in woman, a striking index of its place in Achehnese life.
