## Geographic Position and Border Significance


The *onderafdeeling* Tamiang occupied the southeastern corner of the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*, where the territory of Atjeh abutted the Langkat sultanate and the broader *residentie* of Sumatra's Oostkust. This was a landscape of river valleys and lowlands that sloped gently toward the Strait of Malacca, quite different in character from the mountainous interior of Groot-Atjeh or the rugged western coastline. The Tamiang River valley provided a natural corridor of communication between the Atjeh highlands and the east coast lowlands, and the communities of the Tamiang region had historically maintained close economic and social ties with the Malay polities to their south—ties that in many cases predated the establishment of the colonial border between Atjeh and Sumatra's Oostkust.

The border between Atjeh and Sumatra's Oostkust was, in many respects, an artificial line drawn by the colonial state to delimit the jurisdiction of two fundamentally different administrative systems. On the Atjeh side, the military-dominated *Gouvernement* exercised authority through a combination of direct rule and indirect governance; on the Sumatra side, the civilian *Resident* of Sumatra's Oostkust administered a territory whose economy was dominated by European plantation interests and whose governance was oriented toward facilitating the plantation economy. The communities of Tamiang, situated at this administrative frontier, inevitably felt the pull of both systems, and their economic activities—trade in forest products, small-scale agriculture, and commercial exchanges with the ports of the east coast—connected them more closely to the world of Sumatra's Oostkust than to the Atjeh highlands.


## The Merger Proposal: From Atjeh to Langkat


At the Medan meeting of [[14 January]] [[1914]], Resident [[Van der Plas]] proposed that Tamiang be added to the Langkat *afdeeling* along with the [[Oostkust van Atjeh]]. This proposal was part of a broader scheme to realign the administrative boundaries of the border region, bringing Atjeh's eastern territories under the same administrative umbrella as the economically related Langkat and Deli sultanates. The logic of the proposal was straightforward: the communities of Tamiang had stronger economic and social connections to Langkat than to [[Koeta-Radja]], and their governance under a *Resident* based in the east coast region would be more efficient and responsive to local needs than continued administration from the distant Atjeh capital.

At the Koeta-Radja meeting ([[19 January]]–[[20 January]] [[1914]]), the proposal was modified but the principle of Tamiang's transfer was confirmed. The revised plan created the combined *afdeeling* "Noord- en Oostkust van Atjeh behalve Tamiang" (North and East Coast of Atjeh except Tamiang), explicitly excluding Tamiang from the new Atjeh-based administrative unit and confirming its proposed transfer out of the Atjeh administrative structure and into the Langkat-based division. The very name of the new *afdeeling*—"behaleve Tamiang" (except Tamiang)—testified to the significance of this boundary adjustment, which was the only instance in the Atjeh reorganization where a specific *onderafdeeling* was carved out of a combined unit for transfer to another *afdeeling*.


## Administrative Discretion and the Gouverneur's Role


[[Van der Plas]] stated at the Koeta-Radja meeting that the addition of Atjeh areas including Tamiang and [[Langsa]] to the *afdeelingen* Deli and Langkat should be left to the judgment of the Gouverneur van Atjeh. This deference to the Gouverneur's local knowledge reflected a recognition that border adjustments of this nature required an understanding of conditions on the ground that could not be fully captured in the minutes of a *bestuursvergadering*. The Gouverneur, with his firsthand experience of Atjeh's administrative realities and his relationships with local officials and indigenous leaders, was best positioned to determine whether the transfer of Tamiang would be administratively feasible and politically acceptable. This provision also acknowledged the sensitivity of transferring territory out of the *Gouvernement*—a move that could be perceived as a diminution of Atjeh's territorial integrity—and the importance of ensuring that such transfers were implemented in a manner that maintained the confidence of both the colonial officials and the indigenous communities affected by the change.


## Account from *The Geology of Indonesia* ([[1949]]) — Tamiang oil block

### Production figures

For the full North-Sumatran production table see the entry on the [[East Atjeh oil block]]. The Tamiang block alone accounted for 709,625 of the 920,755 total tons of North-Sumatran production in [[1940]] (77.1 %), and 527,590 of the 728,682 total tons in [[1941]] (72.4 %). The drop in Tamiang production between [[1940]] and [[1941]] — a fall of 182,035 tons, or 25.7 % — is the principal cause of the overall North-Sumatran production decline between those years, and is consistent with the general contraction of East Indian oil production in [[1941]] as the colonial economy braced for the Pacific War.

The operator of the Tamiang block is the [[B.P.M.]] (Bataafsche Petroleum Maatschappij), the Royal Dutch subsidiary that operated the great majority of the East Indian oil fields in the late colonial period.

### Geological context

The Tamiang block lies at the southern edge of the Atjeh residency, on the border with the residency of the East Coast of Sumatra. The Tamiang region — the basin of the [[Tamiang River]] — is geologically continuous with the oil-bearing neogene anticlines of the East-Coast-of-Sumatra oil province, and the Tamiang block's high productivity reflects its position within this productive neogene petroleum system rather than within the more difficult "steep folds" of the Atjeh interior proper. Van Bemmelen's classification of Atjeh as one of the "steep folds (Atjeh, Borneo)" that "were repeatedly investigated by drilling but the results have not been encouraging" applies to the interior Atjeh fields (the [[East Atjeh oil block|East Atjeh block]] and the smaller [[Atjeh oil complex]] fields), not to the Tamiang block, which belongs to the more gently-domed East-Coast-of-Sumatra structural style.

### Significance

The Tamiang oil block is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because it was, by a wide margin, the most productive oil field of the colonial Atjeh residency, accounting for more than 70 % of North-Sumatran oil production in both [[1940]] and [[1941]]. The Tamiang block accordingly represents the high-point of Atjeh's contribution to the colonial East Indian petroleum economy, and its production figures are the principal quantitative record of that contribution. The block's position on the southern edge of the residency also marks the geographic transition from the productive East-Coast-of-Sumatra oil province to the more difficult Atjeh-interior petroleum geology — a transition that determined the overall shape of Atjeh's colonial oil economy.
