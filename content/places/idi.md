**Idi** (also spelled *Edi*) was a district on the East Coast of [[Great Acheh|Acheh]], mentioned by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] in the context of regional saints and the *kramat* tradition. The recently deceased raja of Idi — the first pioneer of that district — was identified as the father of a Habib of the famous ʿAidarus Sayyid family, described as a half-crazy young man, the son of Teungku di Bukét.

The father had also enjoyed a reputation for sanctity, in conjunction with the lately deceased raja of Idi. His daughter was held to be so holy that her husband — after co-habiting with her once — became very ill and had not since ventured to renew his marital rights.

Idi represents the type of small coastal district that fell under nominal [[Aceh Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]] suzerainty while maintaining its own local chief (*raja*) and distinct identity.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Idi


**Idi** (Achehnese *Idi*, vulgarly *Edi*) is a district on the [[East Coast|East Coast]] of [[Acheh|Acheh]] mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje in connection with the settlement of Hadrami sayyids on that coast, and especially with the pioneering activities of the late raja of Idi in conjunction with the saint **Teungku di Buket**.
**Idi** (vulgarly *Edi*) is a district on the [[East Coast|East Coast]] of [[Acheh|Acheh]], mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the scene of the pioneering activity of a deceased raja who, together with the father of a celebrated living *Habib kramat*, first opened the district to settlement and influence. The raja, whose recent death is recorded by Snouck Hurgronje at the time of his writing, is referred to as "the lately deceased raja of Idi"; his profile is preserved among the people of the present wiki as [[Raja of Idi|Raja of Idi]].

### The Raja of Idi and the Saint of Buket


In the chapter on the sayyids of Acheh — the descendants of the Prophet, whose sacred descent gave them an "impregnable basis of action" in the religious and political life of the country — Snouck mentions among the living a **Habib kramat** of the famous family of **ʿAidarus**, "a half-crazy young man, the son of Teungku di Buket." The father, who also enjoyed a reputation for sanctity, "was in conjunction with the lately deceased raja of Idi (vulg. Edi) the first pioneer of that district." The mention is brief, but it places Idi in the same category of newly opened or reclaimed districts of the [[East Coast|East Coast]] whose settlement was, in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the work of coastal chiefs acting in conjunction with holy men.

### The East Coast of Acheh


The [[East Coast|East Coast]] of Acheh — the long stretch of low-lying shore between [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] in the south-east and the mouth of the [[Aceh River|Aceh River]] in the north — was, in Snouck's account, one of the chief theatres of Achehnese commercial and political expansion. Its districts, of which Idi was one, lay along the route by which the pepper and the betel-nut of the interior were carried down to the port at [[Banda Acheh|Banda-Acheh]] and to the roadstead at [[Krueng Raya|Krueng Raya]]. The settlement of such districts, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, was often the work of an enterprising chief who, in conjunction with a holy man, opened the country to cultivation and to Islam.

### The Hadrami Sayyids and the Pioneering of the Coast


The conjunction of the raja of Idi with the saint **Teungku di Buket** is a typical instance of the partnership between secular and religious authority in the pioneering of the East Coast. The Hadrami sayyids — immigrants or descendants of immigrants from the [[Hadramaut|Hadramaut]] in southern Arabia — were especially active in the religious life of the Achehnese coast in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The ʿAidarus family, named in Snouck's account, was one of the most famous of these; members of it had settled in [[Pidie|Pidie]] and other parts of Acheh, and the saint of the Idi connection — Teungku di Buket — appears to have been one of the local representatives of the family.

### The Idi District in Later Times


The raja of Idi whose death is recorded by Snouck was the late nineteenth-century chief of the district, and his pioneering activity, in conjunction with Teungku di Buket, must be referred to the earlier part of the century. The district was, at the time of Snouck's writing, one of the recognized chiefdoms of the [[East Coast|East Coast]] dependencies of the [[Sultanate of Acheh|Sultanate]]; after the abolition of the royal authority and the death of the last sultan, the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands India]] Government claimed the allegiance of its chief as the successor of the sultan. The Idi chief was one of those who formally submitted in order to protect their commerce, and his district passed, in the late nineteenth century, under the colonial administration.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Idi


**Idi** is a region and former harbour town on the East Coast of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]], situated between [[Langsa|Langsa]] to the south and the *afdeeling Noordkust* to the north, whose pre-eminence as a pepper port in the late nineteenth century was gradually eclipsed, in the colonial period, by the rise of [[Langsa|Langsa]] and [[Lhôseumawè|Lhôseumawè]], but whose territory became, with the discovery of petroleum, one of the principal oil-producing districts of Atjeh. *De Buitenbezittingen* treats Idi as a place whose economic history encapsulates the larger shift from native pepper cultivation to European plantation agriculture and extractive industry.

## The onderafdeeling


The *onderafdeeling Idi* was composed of eight *landschappen*:

1. Tandjong Seumatang and Meureubôh
2. Simpang Olém
3. Djoelèq Tjoet
4. Djoelèq Rajeu
5. Boegèng and Bagië
6. Idi Tjoet
7. Idi Rajeu
8. Peudawa Rajeu

These *landschappen* originated, like those of the surrounding East Coast, as colonies of pepper planters whose *tuinhoofden* — the *peutoeha seuneubô* — gradually acquired territorial authority over the lands they had cleared. The institutional structure was the same as that of [[Peureulak|Peureulak]]: the *toeha' peuët* (four elders) and *toeha' lapan* (eight-heads) stood between the *landschapshoofd* and the village heads, with the *peutoeha rajeu* and *peutoeha tjoet* administering the *meunasa*'s below.

## The decline of the pepper port


In the pre-colonial and early colonial period, Idi was "een der belangrijke havens der Oostkust" — one of the important harbours of the East Coast. With the construction of the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh-tram]] (which reached Koeala Simpang in [[1910]]–[[1912]] and was extended to Besitang in [[1913]]) and the rise of [[Langsa|Langsa]] as the principal East Coast harbour, Idi "bijna alle beteekenis als zoodanig verloren" — lost almost all significance as such — and its trade was diverted, partly to Langsa and partly to [[Lhôseumawè|Lhôseumawè]], the next importante harbour to the north. By the time of the handbook, Idi was "nog slechts beteekenis heeft als centrum voor den kleinhandel" — still only of significance as a centre for the small trade.

## Petroleum


The territory of Idi became, in the colonial period, one of the principal petroleum-producing districts of Atjeh. Of the five petroleum concession terrains that had been brought into exploitation by the time of the handbook, two were in Idi: *Peudawa* (split into the inactive Peudawa proper and the active *Oost-Peudawa*, exploited by the [[Bataafsche Petroleum Maatschappij|Bataafsche Petroleum Maatschappij]]) and *Paja-Bilik* (no longer exploited). The crude oil from these fields was piped, through a 5-inch pipeline of more than 120 kilometres, to the [[Bataafsche Petroleum Maatschappij|BPM]] refinery at [[Pangkalan Brandan|Pangkalan Brandan]] on the Oostkust van Sumatra. The declining production of the Idi fields — from 1,609 K.G.-tons in [[1913]] to 220 K.G.-tons in [[1915]] at Oost-Peudawa — was a concern of the handbook's compilers.

## Account from *Verkenningen in Atjeh* (1923)


**Idi** — also written *Edi* — is the chief place (*hoofdplaats*) of the *onderafdeeling* Idi in the *afdeeling* [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust van Atjeh]], situated on the great road and on the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh-tram]] line that runs along the eastern coast. The *onderafdeeling* with its seven *landschappen* has an attractive interior wholly Atjehnese, even though here too the population originated from pepper-colonies of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and [[Pasé|Pasé]].

### The Hoofdplaats


Although Idi declined — perhaps in connection with the general diminution of pepper-cultivation and certainly through the closing of the garrison — the place has a pleasant air when one stands before the house of the *controleur* or the *pasanggrahan* next door, looking across the mown open grass-plain, bordered on three sides by high avenue-trees. They say it is drier there than at Langsa. Everywhere there are signs of upkeep and neatness; fine roads in the shade of stout trees near the settlement of Idi make up for what one may miss in trade. A road along the cemetery — every burial-place in Atjeh has historical significance — leads to the beach, where one has a walk over the grey-yellow sand along the living surf of the sea.

The *keude* (market-street) is wholly taken up by the little shops of Chinese, and beside them stands a Chinese temple which, judged by the sculpture on its forecourt and front, is of somewhat more importance than one usually sees of such houses of worship on Sumatra. More than half a century ago many Chinese from Malakka sought settlement in Idi, and a *kongsie* then farmed the import-duties of the *radja* for fifty thousand dollars. The trade and importance of Idi have doubtless shrunk while those of other harbours grew. The ships of the [[Kon. Paketvaart|Kon. Paketvaart]], which maintain the service between Penang and North-Sumatra, call at Idi. The export was, in 1918 and 1921: bark 622.914 and 413.894 kg; pepper 613.121 and 340.725 kg; copra 18.228 and 150.622 kg; rattan 2.077 and 2.969 kg; pinang 1.073.416 and 843.675 kg.

### The Gemeentefonds


Idi has a *gemeentefonds* — a municipal fund — like several chief places. These are useful institutions and they work easily. The head of the local government, here the *controleur*, is chairman of the committee of four members which manages the fund. It looks after the interests of local traffic, health, and street-lighting. The proceeds are payments for street-lighting, the yield of rent on the market-sheds. It maintains a slaughterhouse, a tennis-court, a cinema. Some municipal funds maintain a bathing-place at sea. There are people who would rather live under the management of an Atjehnese municipal fund than under the care of a town-council on Java.

### The Landschappen


The road to [[Blang Sekotji|Blang Sekotji]] in the *landschap* [[Idi rajeu|Idi rajeu]] runs between the rice-fields and brings one into surroundings of cheerful garden-land round a great rice-field. Coming from the south, one can see the characteristic Atjehnese settlement here for the first time: the great rice-field, and round it the *kampongs* which have possessions in it. That is a useful and tasteful way of settling, testifying to good humour, which we would not attribute to the Atjehnese were we too used to seeing them in a moody disposition of resistance.

There are *blangs* of 100 to 200 *bouw* by estimation; here and there in the *blang* stands a tree with a heavy crown, as a tall guardian over many fields, and under that tree one brings the cut *padi* of the day. Sometimes one treads out the grains from the ears beside that tree, and part of the harvest is temporarily stacked there. The *blang* of an Atjehnese *landschap* knows many lovely days. The *kampongs* round the *blang* look like those of Middle Java: dwellings with a yard, *kampong*-paths with enclosure, and enclosure of the whole *kampong*. One speaks of *kampong* (the truly Atjehnese word is *gampong*), but the name *desa* would, given the arrangement and the nearness of the rice-fields, better indicate the character for one who knows the *desa* of Java. More than the Javanese *desa*, the Atjehnese *gampong* has the yards laid out as gardens, where coco- and pinang-palms, sometimes rubber-trees, grow beside fruit-trees.

### Significance


Idi is the type of the quiet, dried *hoofdplaats* of the eastern coast — a place once important for its pepper-trade and its Chinese *kongsie*, now reduced by the closing of the garrison and the general shrinkage of pepper, but still attractive by its trees, its roads and its *gemeentefonds*. The principal *oeléebalangs* of the *onderafdeeling* are [[Teukoe moeda Oesoch|Teukoe moeda Oesoch]] of [[Simpang Olim|Simpang Olim]] and [[Toeankoe Machmoed|Toeankoe Machmoed]] of [[Idi rajeu|Idi rajeu]].

## Account from *Verkenningen in Atjeh* (1923)

**Idi rajeu** — *Greater Idi*, *rajeu* meaning *great* — is the upper *landschap* of the *onderafdeeling* [[Idi|Idi]] in the *afdeeling* [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust van Atjeh]], stretching inland from the chief place. The higher part of the *landschap* borders inland on [[Gajo-land|Gajo-land]] and is less populated than the lowland. The northern border is the [[Kroeeng Djamboe Aje|Kroeeng Djamboe Aje]], a river whose flooding causes enough trouble to require a firm dyke along it.

### The Oeléebalang


The principal chief of the *landschap* at the time of Broersma's visit was [[Toeankoe Machmoed|Toeankoe Machmoed]], the *'nog jonge toeankoe'* — *still young toeankoe* — whose fine dwelling lies near the chief place of Idi and is connected with it by a fine avenue. Formerly the building must have stood as a fortress amid fortified terrain, since transformed into a garden. The old fortress, now a peaceful house, holds a great treasure of gold-work, partly of Atjehnese make, and precious stones, which are the delight of the eyes of the *toeankoe's* wife, the *tjoet nja*, sister of the previous *oeléebalang* and keeper of many histories of Greater Idi.

### The Northern Border and the Djamboe Aje


The northern border of the *landschap* is the [[Kroeeng Djamboe Aje|Kroeeng Djamboe Aje]], a river whose overflowing causes enough trouble to require a firm dyke along it. From the dyke the beautiful *kampong* [[Lho Nibong|Lho Nibong]] profits with its many dwellings and gardens full of palms. The prevention of swamp-formation by river-water and the effective use of it for agriculture may also in the *onderafdeeling* Idi be worked upon. Thus there was once begun in the *landschap* Idi rajeu an irrigation-work, which the river itself unfortunately destroyed. They say that there are well a thousand hectares irrigable in the *landschap*.
