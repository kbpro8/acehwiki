**West-Indies (Dutch)** was the designation used by the [[Dutch Republic|Netherlands]] in the seventeenth‑century to refer to its overseas possessions in the Caribbean and, by extension, to the broader Dutch colonial empire that later encompassed the [[Dutch East Indies|Oost-Indië]]—the archipelago that included [[Aceh|Acheh]]—and therefore formed a geopolitical backdrop for Dutch maritime activity in the Indian Ocean. The term appears in contemporary Dutch Marine Corps commemorations (see page 55 of the *Geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers*), where the flag of the corps bore the inscription “West‑Indie” to honour the corps’ contributions to the defence of Dutch overseas territories.

## Historical usage

In the aftermath of the **Treaty of Nijmegen** ([[1678]]), the Dutch Republic reduced the standing size of its marine forces, but the memory of earlier campaigns in the West‑Indies—particularly the defence of colonies such as **Tobago**, **Berbice**, and **Suriname**—remained a rallying point for the corps. The 1650‑1700 period saw the Dutch navy repeatedly draw on marine detachments for operations in the Caribbean, a practice that informed later deployments to the Indian Ocean, including the eventual Dutch presence in the Aceh Sultanate.

## Connection to Aceh

Although the phrase “West‑Indie” originally denoted Caribbean holdings, the Dutch colonial administration later applied the broader concept of “Indies” to its Asian possessions. The **Dutch East Indies** (Oost‑Indië) incorporated the northern Sumatra region of **Aceh**, which became a focal point of Dutch‑Acehnese conflict from the early eighteenth century onward (the **Aceh War** of [[1873]]‑[[1904]]). The earlier Caribbean experience, symbolised by the “West‑Indie” inscription, thus prefigured the strategic mindset that later guided Dutch naval and marine operations against the Aceh Sultanate.

## Significance

The dual use of “Indies” illustrates how Dutch imperial terminology evolved from a Caribbean focus to an Asian one, linking disparate colonial theatres. For Aceh, the legacy of Dutch marine organisation—shaped in part by Caribbean experience—proved decisive in the later military confrontations that reshaped the sultanate’s autonomy.
