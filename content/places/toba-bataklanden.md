**Toba-Bataklanden** — the "Toba-Batak Lands" — was, in the Dutch colonial administrative geography of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], the region of the Toba-Batak people on [[Noord-Sumatra|Northern Sumatra]], around [[Lake Toba|Lake Toba]] in the uplands south of [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. The region appears in Prof. [[T. J. Bezemer|T. J. Bezemer]]'s [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|1933 plate-atlas *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid*]] as the place of origin of the *[[cire perdue|à cire perdue]]* gold-casting technique documented by J. E. [[J. E. Jasper|Jasper]] en Mas [[Mas Pirngadie|Pirngadie]] in *[[De Inlandsche Kunstnijverheid in Nederlandsch-Indië|De Inlandsche Kunstnijverheid in Nederlandsch-Indië]]*, Vol. IV, pp. 25–26, and as the place of origin of the [[granuleeren|granulation]] technique documented in the same volume, p. 65. The region is, in this sense, the immediate Sumatran goldsmithing context against which the [[Atjeh goldsmithing (soeasa technique)|Atjeh *soeasa* wrapping technique]] is defined in the Bezemer atlas's § H.

## Geography and people

The Toba-Bataklanden occupy the volcanic uplands of [[Noord-Sumatra|Northern Sumatra]] around [[Lake Toba|Lake Toba]] — the large volcanic crater-lake that is the geographic centre of the Batak homeland. The region lies to the south-east of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], across the mountains of the [[Barisan|Barisan]] range, and to the north of the [[Padangsche Bovenlanden|Padangsche Bovenlanden]]. The principal Toba-Batak towns — [[Balige|Balige]], [[Tarutung|Tarutung]], [[Porsea|Porsea]], [[Sibolga|Sibolga]] (on the coast) — lie around the lake and along the rivers that drain from it to the east and west coasts. The Toba-Batak are the largest of the six Batak peoples (Toba, Karo, Pakpak, Simalungun, Angkola, Mandailing), and their homeland is the geographic and demographic centre of the Batak region as a whole.

The Toba-Batak are, in the late colonial period, predominantly Christian (a consequence of more than half a century of German Rhenish Missionary Society activity from [[1861|1861]] onward), but retain many pre-Christian religious and social institutions — including the *marga* (clan) system, the *adat* (customary law), and the ancestor-veneration practices documented in the colonial-ethnographic literature. The Toba-Batak were, in the late colonial period, one of the most economically dynamic of the Sumatran peoples; their involvement in the plantation economy of East-Sumatra's east coast, and in the colonial commercial economy more broadly, gave them a substantial middle class by the [[1930s|1930s]].

## Goldsmithing tradition

The Toba-Batak goldsmithing tradition is documented in the Jasper-Pirngadie passage quoted by Bezemer (p. 13). The passage describes, in unusually detailed technical terms, the *[[cire perdue|à cire perdue]]* casting process as practised by a Toba-Batak goldsmith (*een Toba-Batakscher goudsmid*) on [[Noord-Sumatra|Noord-Sumatra]]. The process involves the building of a clay-and-burnt-rice-bran core (*de globale voorvorm, de kern*), the laying-on of [[rimbaga|*rimbaga*]] (a wax-resin-fat compound) to form the wax model, the modelling of the relief in further *rimbaga*, the attachment of wax pouring-channels, the coating of the wax model with dry clay powder and then with a clay-and-rice-bran paste, the melting-out of the wax, and the pouring-in of the molten metal. The detail of the description — particularly the use of the indigenous technical term *rimbaga* — is the kind of craft-internal knowledge that, in the colonial-craft-scholarship convention, would have been supplied by Mas [[Mas Pirngadie|Pirngadie]] from his own craft knowledge or from his network of indigenous craft contacts.

The Toba-Batak *cire perdue* casting process is the immediate technical foil against which the [[Atjeh goldsmithing (soeasa technique)|Atjeh *soeasa* wrapping technique]] is defined in the Bezemer atlas. The contrast is documented by Bezemer precisely because Jasper and Pirngadie had already documented the Toba-Batak casting process: the Atjeh goldsmiths chose the wrapping technique — wrapping a copper core with [[spinsbek|*soeasa*]] sheet — as an alternative to the *cire perdue* casting used by their Toba-Batak neighbours. The juxtaposition of the two techniques in the same section of the Bezemer atlas's § H is, in this sense, a comparative juxtaposition of two regional Sumatran goldsmithing traditions, within which the Atjeh technique is defined as the regional variant.

## Granulation tradition

The Toba-Bataklanden are also documented, in the same Jasper-Pirngadie volume (Vol. IV, p. 65, quoted by Bezemer p. 13), as one of the regional centres of [[granuleeren|*granuleeren*]] (granulation) — the technique of decorating a metal surface with tiny soldered balls of precious metal. Bezemer notes that *granuleeren* is the technique of the Bataks, Dyaks and Timorese, and the Batak application is therefore the Sumatran variant of the technique. The Toba-Batak granulation and the Toba-Batak *cire perdue* casting are, in this sense, complementary techniques of the same regional Sumatran metalwork tradition, both documented in the same Jasper-Pirngadie volume.

## Toba-Bataklanden in the Bezemer atlas

The Toba-Bataklanden are documented in the Bezemer atlas principally by the *Ragidoep* — a cotton ikat textile — of plate 64 (p. 48), captioned in four languages as *Ragidoep. katoenen ikat. Toba-Bataklanden, Sumatra* / *Ragidup. Toba-Batak Lands, Sumatra* / *Ragidup. Pays des Toba Batak, Sumatra* / *Ragidup. Toba-Batak-Länder, Sumatra*. The *Ragidoep* is a Toba-Batak cotton ikat textile — a parallel, in cotton, to the Atjeh silk ikats of plates 65 and 66. The juxtaposition of the Toba-Batak cotton ikat of plate 64 with the Atjeh silk ikats of plates 65 and 66 on the same page-spread of the Bezemer atlas is, in this sense, a comparative juxtaposition of two Sumatran ikat traditions — one in cotton, one in silk — within which the Atjeh silk ikats are placed.

The Toba-Batak are also documented in the brasswork chapter (§ F) of the Bezemer atlas by the brass armlet of plate 169 (p. 123), captioned *Koperen armring der Toba Bataks. Sumatra* / *Brass Armlet of the Toba Batak. Sumatra* / *Bracelet de cuivre des Toba batak. Sumatra* / *Messing-Armspange der Toba-Batakker. Sumatra*, and by the Batak man with brass armlet of plate 168 (p. 122). The Toba-Batak brass armlet is, in this sense, another Toba-Batak metalwork object documented alongside the Atjeh brassware of the same chapter — placing the Toba-Batak and Atjeh metalwork traditions in the same comparative frame.

## Significance for Atjeh

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the Toba-Bataklanden are significant principally as the Sumatran goldsmithing region against which Atjeh goldsmithing is defined in the late colonial Dutch scholarship. The Toba-Batak *cire perdue* casting process is the immediate technical alternative to the Atjeh *soeasa* wrapping technique; the Toba-Batak granulation is the decorative technique applied to the cast objects; and the Toba-Batak cotton ikat of plate 64 is the textile parallel to the Atjeh silk ikats of plates 65 and 66. The region is, in this sense, the principal Sumatran craft-geographical reference point for the Atjeh metalwork and textile traditions, alongside the [[Padangsche Bovenlanden|Padangsche Bovenlanden]] to the south.

The Toba-Bataklanden are also significant as the immediate southern neighbours of Atjeh across the [[Barisan|Barisan]] range, and as the principal non-Atjehnese region of [[Noord-Sumatra|Noord-Sumatra]] in the Dutch colonial administrative geography. The two regions — Atjeh and the Toba-Bataklanden — together constituted the highland-and-coastal complex of northern Sumatra, and their metalwork and textile traditions were, in the late colonial period, in continuing contact across the mountainous interior.
