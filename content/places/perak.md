**Perak** was a Malay [[sultanate]] on the western coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]] that became a major tin‑rich polity and, from the late [[16th century]] onward, fell under the suzerainty of [[Atjeh|Aceh]] and later entered the Dutch‑Acehnese contest over the peninsula’s tin trade. The first Acehnese capture occurred in [[1579]], a control was reinforced by [[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] in the early [[17th century]], and a series of treaties in the 1650s formalised joint Acehnese‑Dutch exploitation of Perak’s tin until the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] withdrew its factory in [[1663]]. Linguistic evidence from a 16th‑century Malay translation of the [[Burda]] shows Arabic loanwords and other Acehnese influences on Perak‑Malay, underscoring the cultural as well as economic impact of Acehnese domination.

## Early history and pre‑Acehnese period
Before any external intervention, Perak functioned as an independent Malay polity renowned for its abundant tin deposits and its strategic position along the maritime routes of the [[Strait of Malacca]]. Its export‑driven economy linked it to regional trading centres such as [[Malacca]] and [[Kedah]].

## Acehnese conquest (1579) and consolidation
In [[1579]] the forces of [[Atjeh]] launched a successful invasion, bringing Perak under Acehnese control. Contemporary Acehnese records describe the event as “Perak … in 1579 door Atjēh veroverd.” The conquest was part of Aceh’s broader campaign to secure the western Malay coast, control the tin trade, and spread Islamic authority.

### Re‑assertion under Sultan Iskandar Muda
During the early [[17th century]] the expansionist [[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] of Aceh renewed and deepened Acehnese authority over Perak, as noted in the same source: “in de 17e eeuw door Sultan Iskandar Muda nogmaals onder Atjéhse heerschappij gebracht.” This second phase of domination ensured that Perak remained a tributary within the Acehnese sphere throughout the first half of the century.

## Linguistic and cultural interactions
The period of Acehnese dominance left detectable traces in the local language. A 16th‑century Acehnese‑Malay manuscript of the [[Burda]] (the *Malay translation of the Burda*) exhibits Perak‑Malay lexical and phonological features, including numerous Arabic loanwords that entered both Acehnese and Perak dialects. The *Maleise tekst van de Burda* records several such terms, highlighting the diffusion of Islamic vocabulary across the region.

## Dutch‑Acehnese contest over Perak (17th century)

### Atjeh suzerainty and the Tin Quartiers
VOC and Acehnese officials agreed that the tin of the [[Tin Quartiers|Tin Quartiers]] flowed “mostly via Perak, which stood under Atjeh.” Acehnese suzerainty thus became the legal basis for the sultane of Aceh to participate in the peninsular tin trade, while the VOC’s attempts to dislodge her contributed to the [[Blockade of Atjeh (1656)|1656 blockade]] crisis.

### The 1650 Treaty of Atjeh‑Perak
The [[Treaty of Atjeh-Perak (15 August 1650)|treaty of 15 August 1650]] marked the high point of VOC‑Atjeh commercial cooperation. The treaty stipulated that “the queen of Atjeh bound herself on 15 August 1650 to open the tin trade in Perak only to Atjehers and Hollanders.” This extended the earlier [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (November 1649)|Truytman treaties]] of [[1649]] to the peninsula’s richest tin source.

### Outrage of 1651 and peace of 1655
Violations erupted quickly. In the beginning of [[1651]] the Dutch office in Perak was overrun, resulting in the death of all officials. On [[1 August]] [[1651]] the VOC’s Hooge Regeering dispatched a fleet demanding satisfaction, but achieved none. Hostilities persisted until [[1655]], when a peace treaty, negotiated in concert with Atjeh, restored a tenuous calm. Under the 1655 agreement Perak was obliged to pay 50,000 reals in tin as damages and to permit the rebuilding of a VOC factory and warehouse. VOC records describe the local population in stark terms: “the nation in itself is very foul and murderous.”

### The 1659 Perak settlement
The [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (5 October 1659)|peace of 5 October 1659]] allocated the tin revenue equally: “half of the tin won in Perak should be for Atjeh, half for the Compagnie.” The VOC was to receive a provisional price of 30 reals per baar, to be raised to 32½ reals once arrears were cleared.

### VOC withdrawal in 1663
Perak’s merchants repeatedly evaded the treaty’s terms, prompting the VOC to withdraw its factory in [[1663]]. The withdrawal was part of a larger West Coast reorganisation that also saw the closure of the VOC’s Atjeh factory and the signing of the [[Painansch Contract]] on [[6 July]] [[1663]]. After 1663 the VOC limited its presence to ship‑board trade, marking the limits of its peninsular authority.

## Significance
Perak served as the principal prize in the Dutch‑Acehnese rivalry for control of the Malay Peninsula’s tin wealth. The 1650 treaty remains the only direct Dutch‑Acehnese‑Malay treaty concerning a peninsular territory, and the 1659 settlement is the sole documented joint sovereignty arrangement between the VOC and Aceh. The repeated outrages of [[1651]] and [[1655]] and the eventual VOC withdrawal illustrate the challenges of maintaining a land‑based factory in a region dominated by a powerful regional sultanate.

Culturally, the Acehnese period introduced Arabic lexical items into Perak‑Malay, evidencing the broader diffusion of Islamic religious and linguistic influence across the archipelago. The political framework established in the mid‑17th century set the stage for later eighteenth‑century succession disputes within the Perak sultanate and its relations with neighbouring polities such as [[Kedah]] and [[Selangor]].
