**Perak** is a state on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula that was historically a tin‑rich Malay sultanate. It came under Acehnese suzerainty in the late sixteenth century, was reconquered by Sultan Iskandar Muda in the seventeenth century, and later became the principal prize in the Dutch‑Acehnese contest over the Tin Quartiers. The 1650 Treaty of Atjeh‑Perak and the 1659 Treaty of Atjeh‑VOC defined the joint Acehnese‑Dutch control of Perak’s tin, while the 1663 Dutch withdrawal marked the limits of their peninsular authority.

## Early history
Prior to Acehnese intervention, Perak was an independent Malay polity known for its tin exports and its position along the maritime trade routes of the Strait of Malacca.

## Acehnese conquest (1579)
In [[1579]] the forces of [[Atjēh]] (also linked as [[Atjeh|Atjeh]]) launched a successful invasion, bringing Perak under Acehnese control. The source records this as “Perak … in 1579 door Atjēh veroverd”【20260707_232246_DLP-54-Een_16de_eeuwse_Maleise_vertaling_van_de_Burda_van_al_Bu_s_i_ri_OCR.txt, p. 45】.

## Restoration under Sultan Iskandar Muda
In the 17th century, [[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] of Atjēh renewed Acehnese authority over Perak, a fact noted as “in de 17”h eeuw door Sultan Iskandar Muda nogmaals onder Atjéhse heerschappij gebracht”【20260707_232246_DLP-54-Een_16de_eeuwse_Maleise_vertaling_van_de_Burda_van_al_Bu_s_i_ri_OCR.txt, p. 45】.

## Linguistic impact
The period of Acehnese dominance left traces in the local language; analysis of a 16th‑century Acehnese Malay manuscript of the [[Burda]] (Malay translation of the Burda) reveals Perak‑Malay lexical and phonological features, suggesting bidirectional influence【20260707_232246_DLP-54-Een_16de_eeuwse_Maleise_vertaling_van_de_Burda_van_al_Bu_s_i_ri_OCR.txt, pp. 44‑45】.

## Dutch‑Acehnese contest over Perak (17th century)

### Atjeh suzerainty and the Tin Quartiers
Stapel records that the tin of the [[Tinkwartieren|Tin Quartiers]] flowed “mostly via Perak, which stood under Atjeh” (*meest via Perak, dat onder Atjeh stood*). Atjeh's suzerainty over Perak was the lever by which the [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|sultane]] participated in the peninsular tin trade, and the Compagnie's effort to dislodge her was one of the principal causes of the [[Blockade of Atjeh (1656)|1656 blockade]] crisis.

### The 1650 Atjeh‑Perak treaty
The [[Treaty of Atjeh-Perak (15 August 1650)|treaty of 15 August 1650]] was the high point of Compagnie‑Atjeh commercial co‑operation in the peninsula. “The queen of Atjeh bound herself on 15 August 1650 to open the tin trade in Perak only to Atjehers and Hollanders” (*de vorstin van Atjeh zich 15 Augustus 1650 verbond, den tinhandel in Perak alleen open te stellen voor Atjehers en Hollanders*). The 1650 treaty thus completed the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (November 1649)|Truytman treaties]] of November [[1649]] by extending the same Atjehers‑and‑Hollanders‑only formula to the peninsula's richest tin source.

### The 1651 outrage and 1655 peace
The 1650 treaty was almost immediately violated. “Already in the beginning of 1651 the Netherlands office was overrun, whereby all officials found death” (*Reeds in het begin van 1651 werd het Nederlandsche kantoor afgeloopen, waarbij alle ambtenaren den dood vonden*). On [[1 August]] [1651] the Hooge Regeering sent a fleet to demand satisfaction, but obtained none, and “the relationship remained hostile until 1655, when peace was concluded in concert with Atjeh” (*de verhouding bleef vijandig tot 1655, toen in overleg met Atjeh de vrede werd gesloten*).

Perak bound itself by the 1655 treaty to pay a damages compensation of 50,000 reals in tin and to permit the rebuilding of a factory and warehouse. The Compagnie's representative at Perak found the position difficult: “it was extremely difficult for the Compagnie's representative to remain on good terms with the Atjehsche authorities, and at the same time with the native population and chiefs” — the Dutch record’s own characterisation of the Perak Malays is the blunt phrase “the nation in itself is very foul and murderous” (*de natie in zichzelven is zeer vuyl en moorddadig*).

### The 1659 Perak settlement
The [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (5 October 1659)|peace of 5 October 1659]] with the sultane of Atjeh provided “that half of the tin won in Perak should be for Atjeh, half for the Compagnie” (*dat de helft van de op Perak gewonnen tin voor Atjeh, de helft voor de Compagnie zou zijn*). The Compagnie was to pay provisionally only 30 reals the bar (*baar*), to be brought to the old footing of 32½ reals once its arrears were satisfied.

### The 1663 withdrawal
The 1659 Perak settlement was repeatedly evaded. “When however those of Perak in the execution of this agreement made all kinds of chicanes, the government lifted in 1663 also the factory in this place; ‘the trade of Perak shall henceforth be driven on board’” (*Toen echter die van Perak bij de uitvoering dezer overeenkomst allerlei chicanes maakten, lichtte de regeering in 1663 ook het kantoor in deze plaats; ‘de handel van Perak sal voortaen aen boord gedreven worden’*). The Compagnie's withdrawal of its Perak factory in [[1663]] — together with the simultaneous withdrawal of its Atjeh factory — was part of the larger Westkust reorganisation that produced the [[Painansch Contract]] of [[6 July]] [1663].

## Significance
Perak is the principal peninsular prize of the Dutch‑Acehnese contest. The [[Treaty of Atjeh-Perak (15 August 1650)|15 August 1650 treaty]] is the only direct Dutch‑Acehnese‑Malay treaty over a peninsular territory, and the [[1659]] peace's Perak clause is the only Compagnie‑Atjeh joint sovereignty arrangement in the historical record. The 1651 and 1658 outrages at Perak — and the Compagnie's 1663 withdrawal to shipboard trade — define the limits of the Compagnie's peninsular authority: the Compagnie could not maintain a land factory at Perak, but it could police the trade from the sea.

Perak is also the principal point of contact between the Dutch‑Acehnese story and the larger history of the Malay sultanates. The Perak sultanate's own internal politics — its succession disputes, its relations with [[Kedah]] and [[Selangor]] — would dominate the eighteenth‑century Malay Peninsula, but the seventeenth‑century framework of those relations was set by the 1650 and 1659 treaties with the sultane of Atjeh.
