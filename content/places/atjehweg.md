**The Atjehweg** — the Great Atjeh Road (Dutch: *Groote Atjehweg*) — was the major colonial roadway constructed across the lowland of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that linked the colonial capital at [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta-Radja]] with the inland posts of the residency (Dutch: *gewest*) and, by extension, with the coastal ports of [[Olehleh|Olehleh]] and [[Belawan|Belawan]] through which its trade was shipped. Its construction, together with the parallel [[Atjehtram|Atjeh-tram]] and the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] cut through the highland jungle, formed the transport network that opened the interior of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] to intensified trade, settlement, and administrative integration with the wider [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands-Indies]] state after the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]].

## Construction and Impact

The construction of the Atjehweg in the closing phase of the [[Aceh War|Aceh War]] and the subsequent [[Pacification of Atjeh|pacificatie]] under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] enabled the population of the lowland districts to engage more intensively with the external cultures and markets that the road brought within reach. The source document *DLP-84-Atjeh* records that the road's establishment produced, in the lowland areas it traversed, a "more peaceful development and higher prosperity" (Dutch: *rustige ontwikkeling en hoogere welvaart*) than had been possible in the war years — a phrase that captures the colonial state's own diagnosis of the road's economic function. The road's alignment was followed, in its lowland stretches, by the [[Atjehtram|Atjeh-tram]], whose rails ran parallel to the wheeled traffic and whose steam-trams carried both military stores, in the war phase, and smallholder crops, in the civil phase. The road thus belongs, in its physical form, to the same military-infrastructure project as the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] cut through the jungle on military command with the *klewang* (the colonial army's sword) in a direct southerly direction under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] around [[1904]].

## Economic Integration

The economic function of the Atjehweg, once the residency had passed from war-footing to civil government, was to carry the smallholder crops of the lowland villages — pepper from the gardens (Dutch: *pepertuinen*), rice from the river-valley lands, copra from the coconut groves — to the export harbours at [[Olehleh|Olehleh]] and, via the [[Atjehspoorweg|Atjeh railway]], to [[Belawan|Belawan]] and the wider Sumatran market. The road also carried the contract labourers brought in to work the new European estates opened under the [[Agrarische Wet 1870|Agrarian Law of 1870 (Dutch: *Agrarische Wet*)]] on the east-coast lowlands, and the manufactured goods — textiles, kerosene, hardware — that the colonial economy distributed to the villages along the road. The integration of the lowland districts into the wider Netherlands-Indies economy, registered in the daily traffic of the road, was the material foundation of the prosperity that the colonial state claimed for its Atjeh policy after [[1908]]. The road's continued use as the principal axis of communication in northern Sumatra well into the late twentieth century testifies to the durability of the colonial infrastructure.
