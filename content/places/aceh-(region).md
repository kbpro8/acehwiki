**Aceh (region)** is the northernmost peninsula of [[Sumatra]], historically the seat of the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] and later a province of the Republic of Indonesia.  Throughout the seventeenth century the sultanate was a pivotal node in the spice trade network of the [[East Indies]], and the Dutch **East India Company** (VOC) maintained a factory and regular commercial contacts there.  The source, while describing the naval actions of [[1672]]–[[1673]] off the English coast, refers to the “East Indian return fleet” (*Oostindische retourvloot*) that the English and French sought to capture; this fleet consisted of ships returning from the East Indies, whose routes routinely passed the waters of Aceh on their way to Europe.

## Early Dutch‑Acehnese relations

The VOC first established a permanent presence in Aceh in 1602, negotiating a treaty that allowed Dutch merchants to purchase pepper and other spices directly from the sultanate.  The sultanate’s strategic position at the entrance to the Strait of [[Malacca]] made it a valuable ally for the Dutch in their competition with the Portuguese and later the English.  Dutch ships would often stop at the port of [[Banda Aceh]] to load cargoes before joining the larger convoy of the East Indian return fleet.

## Role in the 1670s naval strategy

During the **Second Anglo‑Dutch War**, the Dutch navy under Admiral [[Michiel de Ruyter]] prepared to confront the combined English‑French fleet at [[Solebay]] in order to protect the anticipated arrival of the VOC’s East Indian return fleet.  Although the battle itself took place far from Aceh, the strategic importance of the convoy stemmed from the valuable cargoes gathered in Aceh and elsewhere in the East Indies.  The source notes that the enemy aimed to “lag de Oostindische retourvloot af te wachten” (await the East Indian return fleet), underscoring the economic significance of Aceh’s trade to the Dutch war effort.

## Later developments

Aceh continued to be a contested zone between the VOC and various European powers throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, culminating in a series of treaties and occasional armed confrontations.  The sultanate retained a degree of autonomy until the Dutch‑Aceh War of the late nineteenth century, after which Aceh was incorporated into the Dutch East Indies.
