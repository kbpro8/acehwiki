**Balah** was a small Malay district on [[Sumatra's East Coast]], enumerated by Van der Kemp among the petty states which in [[1824]] had already been reduced to subjection by the Padris. The name is preserved in the historical record chiefly through the letter of the [[Sultan of Deli]] to the governor of [[Penang]], printed by Van der Kemp as Appendix 6 to his article.

## In the Sultan of Deli's letter of 1824

The sole mention of Balah occurs in Appendix 6, where Van der Kemp prints the letter of [[27 August]] [[1824]] from the Sultan of Deli to the new governor of Penang, [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]. The sultan reported that the Padris — the religious reformers then devastating the interior of [[Sumatra]] — had "determined to attack all the different states and render them tributary" and to impose their peculiar laws on the coast. "Many countries", the sultan wrote, "have already been reduced to subjection and rendered tributary, amongst the rest are Tanah Putih, Balah and Panei, and they have now expressed their determination to attack Batu Bara and Delli". The sultan declared that he would himself proceed to [[Batu Bara]] if hostilities broke out, and requested British assistance in resisting the Padri advance.

## Place in the wider argument

Balah's appearance in the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|Sultan of Deli's letter]] is brief but characteristic. Van der Kemp prints the letter not for its information about Balah in particular, but as part of his larger documentation of the state of affairs on [[Sumatra's East Coast]] in the years immediately following the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 1824]]. The letter shows that the East Coast states — including [[Deli]], [[Batu Bara]], [[Tanah Putih]], [[Balah]] and [[Panei]] — were already gravely disturbed by the Padri war, and that the British government at Penang was being appealed to as the natural protector of the coast. This practical dependence on Penang, even after the formal renunciation of British political establishments on Sumatra, is one of the main pieces of evidence which Van der Kemp adduces for his thesis that the British government, while disclaiming political authority, continued to treat the East Coast as a commercial preserve.
