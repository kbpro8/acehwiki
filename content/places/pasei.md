**Pasei** — also **Pasai**, the historic port-kingdom of **Samudra Pasai** on the north coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] (Aceh) — was one of the two chief pepper ports of the Atjeh coast in the early-modern period, the port of call of the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] on the first European visit to the Atjeh coast, and the place off which a fleet of sixty warships of the sultan of [[Djohor|Djohor]] was sighted in [[1601]] — the sight that, combined with the appearance of [[André Furtado de Mendoza|Furtado de Mendoza]]'s Portuguese fleet, turned the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] against the Portuguese and prepared the ground for the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]]. Pasei is one of the most frequently named places in Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The Portuguese visit (1509)

Pasei was one of the two pepper ports — with [[Pedir|Pedir]] — that the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] called at on the way to [[Malaka|Malaka]], "waar de Portugeezen toestemming verwierven om handel te drijven". The visit was the first European commercial contact with the Atjeh coast and the foundation of the European pepper trade with the region. Pasei, like Pedir, would within a generation become one of the chief attractions of the Atjeh coast to European traders.

## The Djohor fleet off Pasei (1601)

In June [[1601]], when [[André Furtado de Mendoza|Furtado de Mendoza]]'s Portuguese fleet of a galleon and a fust appeared off Atjeh, the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] and his court suspected a connection between the Portuguese fleet's arrival and the simultaneous presence of "een vloot van een zestigtal oorlogsschepen van den sultan van Djohor voor Pasei". The Atjehers "versterkten zich, bevreesd voor een aanval van beiden". The sultan, unwilling to make the Portuguese his enemies but angered when they seized an Arab ship in defiance of his hospitality, had his shore batteries fire on the Portuguese ship; one of Furtado's ships was lost, and the crew was disarmed and imprisoned. Van der Woude notes that "de volgende Hollandsche vloot zou van deze gebeurtenissen profiteeren": the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]] found the sultan already alienated from the Portuguese, and obtained the release of [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] without ransom.

## The historical background

Pasei — properly the kingdom of **Samudra Pasai** — was one of the oldest Muslim port-kingdoms on Sumatra, founded (according to the Malay chronicles) in the late thirteenth century. It was the chief pepper port of the north Sumatran coast before the rise of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the sixteenth century, and its Islamisation made it the principal conduit of Muslim commercial and religious influence into the Indonesian archipelago. By the early seventeenth century, when Van der Woude's narrative begins, Pasei had been absorbed into the Atjeh sultanate, but its port retained its commercial significance as one of the two chief pepper ports of the Atjeh coast. The Portuguese missions of [[1509]] and [[1511]] called at Pasei on the way to Malaka; the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers' visit of 1599]] called at Pedir rather than Pasei, but the Portuguese trade with Pasei continued throughout the sixteenth century.

## Significance

Pasei is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal north-coast pepper port of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] (alongside [[Pedir|Pedir]]) and the place off which the Djohor fleet was sighted in [[1601]] — the sight that turned the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] against the Portuguese and prepared the ground for the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]]. Pasei's pepper trade was the proximate commercial attraction of the Atjeh coast to European traders from the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] onward, and its role in the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]]'s strategic calculations — as a port to be defended against both the Portuguese and the Djohor — made it the principal strategic location on the north Sumatran coast in the early-modern period. Pasei's earlier history as the kingdom of Samudra Pasai — the oldest Muslim port-kingdom on Sumatra — also frames its long-term significance as the conduit of Muslim commercial and religious influence into the Indonesian archipelago.
