**The Basin of Meulaboh** is the coastal alluvial basin of the [[Meulaboh]] district on the [[Atjeh's Westkust|West coast of Atjeh]], distinguished in the colonial geological literature as the type-area for the late-Tertiary to Recent geological evolution of the western Atjeh coast, and as the locus of the only colonial-era gold-dredging operation on [[Acheh|Atjeh]] territory — the [[M.A.E.M.]] dredges that began work on the [[Geudong Concession]] in [[1940]]. The basin's geological structure, surface form and alluvial gold-and-platinum content were first systematically mapped by [[J. Zwierzycki|Zwierzycki]] in [[1922]] on sheet I of the 1:1,000,000 geological map of the Netherlands Indies, and the basin's mineral geography was subsequently re-described by [[C. de Groot|De Groot]] ([[1946]]) and synthesised by [[R. W. van Bemmelen|Van Bemmelen]] ([[1949]]).

## Geological structure

Van Bemmelen reproduces, as Fig. 28 of *The Geology of Indonesia*, the Zwierzycki [[1922]] map of the Basin of Meulaboh. The map distinguishes:

- **Alluvium** — the recent alluvial coastal strip, at present subsiding, which shows no river terraces.
- **Neogene** (Pliocene, Miocene) — the late-Tertiary marine sediments of the coastal embayment.
- **Paleogene** (Oligocene, Eocene) — the older Tertiary sediments exposed along the inland margin.
- **Permo-Carboniferous** — the pre-Tertiary basement of the central mountains.
- **Andesitic Effusiva, Andesite, Basalt (B), Diabase (D), Gabbro (G), Serpentine, Granite** — the igneous and metamorphic complexes of the interior.

Van Bemmelen adds a critical correction: the "Losir (Löser)" indicated on Zwierzycki's map as a volcano is "not volcano, as has been indicated on this map, see volume I". With that caveat, the map remains the standard cartographic basis for the surface geology of the West-Atjeh coastal plain.

## Surface form: terraces and the sudden southward bend

The most striking surface feature of the basin, as Van Bemmelen describes it, is the system of pleistocene river terraces that "begin directly at the foot of the central mountains and extend downstream to the sudden southward bend in all larger rivers". The terraces are "pleistocene plains of river deposits, dissected by recent uplifts and rejuvenated erosion", and the sudden southward bend in the larger rivers "are most probably the result of a young fault zone, parallel to the coast. The faulting movements are recent or subrecent, because traces of the former river courses, forming the western extension of the upper courses, have been observed in the alluvial lowland."

The alluvial coastal strip itself, by contrast, "is at present subsiding, and shows no terraces". In the upper course of all larger rivers only one terrace has been found. The basin is therefore a tectonically active landscape: the inland terraces are Pleistocene remnants of an older, more seaward-extended drainage system, while the present alluvial coastal strip is subsiding and receiving the modern sediment load of the rivers.

## Gold and platinum placers

The pleistocene terraces of the Meulaboh basin are the principal repository of the alluvial gold of the West coast of Atjeh. Van Bemmelen records that "the alluvial gold at sites of second-, third-, or higher order of displacement is found in the river terraces between the central mountains and the alluvial lowland. The gold grains are coarse near the mountain border, becoming smaller downstream. The gold-bearing black sands contain fine grains of cinnabar, besides considerable amounts of platinum."

The terrace deposits are "from ten to hundred feet thick", and "remains of these terraces, in so far as they have not yet been worked by the natives, often proved to be very rich". The primary sites of the gold "have to be sought in the pre-tertiary Old Slates Formation and granite contacts of the interior", but "due to the outbreak of the war these primary deposits have not yet been explored."

## The M.A.E.M. dredging operation

Production from the Meulaboh placers began in [[1940]] on the [[Geudong Concession]] of the [[M.A.E.M.]] The operation used two bucket dredges (buckets of 6 cubic feet) with a joint capacity of 270,000 cubic yards per month. The deposit contained 55–60 per cent boulders (the largest of which "could just be handled") and 40–45 per cent fine material. There were two types of gold: fine gold with somewhat higher silver content, which was easily amalgamated, and coarse gold with lower silver content, which was difficult to amalgamate. The platinum production was some kilograms per month. Treatment was by amalgamation and Wilfley tables.

[[C. de Groot|De Groot]]'s [[1946]] report observed that the M.A.E.M. dredges, in [[1941]], used 400 kg of mercury per bucket dredge per clean-up to recover the finer gold, and that "after every clean-up the quantity of mercury had increased" — the drilling samples occasionally contained native mercury, and the black sand contained very fine grains of cinnabar. This is the only documented colonial-era observation of native mercury in Atjeh alluvial deposits; see [[Mercury in Atjeh]].

## Significance

The Basin of Meulaboh is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history on three grounds. First, it is the type-area for the late-Tertiary to Recent geological evolution of the western Atjeh coast, and the Zwierzycki [[1922]] map of the basin is the standard cartographic basis for all subsequent geological work on the region. Second, it is the locus of the only colonial-era gold-dredging operation on Atjeh territory — the [[M.A.E.M.]] dredges of [[1940]]–[[1941]] — and accordingly the only Atjeh locality for which colonial-era gold production figures are recorded. Third, the basin's alluvial deposits are the only Atjeh locality for which native mercury and cinnabar have been documented in the colonial geological literature.
