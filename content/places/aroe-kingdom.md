**Aroe Kingdom** was a maritime polity on [[Sumatra]]'s east coast, centered on the [[Aroebai|Aroe Bay]] near present-day [[Tandjoeng Poera]], that in the sixteenth century was reckoned in the [[Sejarah Melayu]] on a par with [[Pasej]] and [[Malaka]], and which stretched from [[Tamijang]] to [[Siak]] before its destruction by [[Acheh]] in [[1539]].

## Context

P. de Roo de La Faille, working from [[Mendez Pinto's Aroe mission (1539)|Mendez Pinto]]'s account, described [[Aroebai|Aroe]] as the part of Sumatra's east coast where, in his reading, Hindus and Arabs first established themselves in the archipelago, bringing trade, religion and art. He called it a *land van oorsprong*, *kemolan*, to be spoken of only with deep reverence, a zone of *hila-ila* and *boejoet* preserved in [[babad]] and [[sadjarah]] literature.

In older Javanese historiography the region was linked to the origin-land of the [[Kalang]] people — *Djawa-ka Lang-ka*, otherwise rendered *Taroema* — a toponymic complex that later writers decoded as pointing to the sacred east-coast heartland. The kingdom appears in sources as [[Sjahroe'n Noewi|Sjahroe'n Noewi]] (Sejarah Melayu), *Cernovem* (de' Conti, c. [[1430]]), *Sornau* (Pinto, [[1539]]), and *Xarnauz* (Vasco da Gama's Roteiro).

## Pinto's description

When [[Mendez Pinto's Aroe mission (1539)|Mendez Pinto]] arrived in [[Malaka]] in [[1539]] in the suite of Governor [[Pero de Faria]], he recorded the coast of *d'Auru* under veiled names. The roadstead he called *Surotilau* is glossed by de Roo as *Soera-sakti lawoe-kir*, a cautious Malay nickname for the anchorage of [[Malajoer]] in [[Aroebai|Aroe]].

Sailing north along Sumatra, Pinto noted:
- *Ilikandure*, interpreted as *ikan* on *hin-doe ka-ri*, "where Hindu population still remained"
- *Minhatoley*, five miles from the kingdom of [[Pedir]], glossed as *mina-ng-k-a datoe Oleh-leh*, the district of a datoe — a [[sukoe]]-head of Oleh-leh — marking an [[Acheh]] frontier post at the beginning of the passage around Sumatra's northern tip

## Political structure

Pinto's informants described a Malay-style administration. A *Xabandar* commanded five *lanchares* (*lantjaran*) and twelve *balo* (*baloq*). Above them stood the *banda(ha)ra* (bendahara), the "gouverneur du royaume", who directed the *Ourobaltones* (*hoeloebalang*). His personal guard were *Amborrajas* (*hamba radja*), "the most noble of his Court, who n'empêschoit pas que la plupart d'entre eux ne fussent très pauvres."

## Economy

Aroe was anciently a revictualling station for shipping bound through the [[Strait of Malacca]] from east and west. Its most famed export was *minjak tanah*, a whitish earth-oil used as balsam and medicine, loaded at [[Pangkalan Susu]] — the name itself recalling *soesoe*, "milk", for the oil's colour. De Roo equates Pinto's *minha cumbaa* with *minjak sembawa*, the landing-place from which the queen later fled.

Ibn Battuta, visiting the region in [[1345]]–[[1346]], noted a Hindu kingdom (*Moel Djawa*) with elephants, stone walls, cloves and nutmeg on its market — signs of regular trade with the Moluccas via Aroe's entrepôt.

## War with Acheh ([[1539]])

By [[1539]] the [[Sultan of Acheh]], newly zealous after his conversion to Islam, was attacking neighbors "te vuur en te zwaard". The king of the [[Batu Islands]] had already lost three sons to Achehnese raids and sought Portuguese aid. Aroe, with only 6,000 men under arms, faced an Achehnese fleet of some 130 vessels under [[Heredin Mahomet]] — the Sultan's brother-in-law and governor of [[Barus]].

For Acheh, possession of Aroe was strategic: it commanded the Strait and allowed interception of Portuguese and other trade to [[Sunda]], the [[Moluccas]] and [[China]].

The fall came through treachery: a *Cacis* (religious teacher) bribed by Acheh opened a gate, delivering the fortification. The raja was killed; the queen fled to [[Malaka]] and then to [[Johor]], whose sultan married her, attacked the Achehnese at Aroe, and temporarily reconquered the country. The reconquest did not last. See [[Fall of Aroe to Acheh (1539)]].

## Later memory

In the Dutch period only a *soekoe Aroe* survived in memory. When Controleur Van Rijn van Alkemade visited the [[Aroebai|Aroe Bay]] from [[Bengkalis]] in [[1888]], he found forested hills, damar tapping, and local tradition of the old kingdom. He described sailing between [[Pulo Sembilan]] and [[Poelo Kampi|Pulo Koempej]], seeing the half-circular island with 50–150 m hills, white beach, and the fortified houses of [[Datoe Bintara]] — a view that recalled the *benteng* of an Achehnese adventurer who in [[1862]] had defiantly flown the Acheh flag there.

Inland, Van Rijn noted remains of old fortifications on the Si Kondoer and Serang Djaja rivers, said to be *bentengs* of early Aroe princes, and the Si Djanda tributary of the [[Besitang]] river, linked to *Djanda Gidau*, widow of Aroe's last ruler.

De Roo connects Aroe to the thirteenth-century *Kampe* (*P. Kompaj*) that broke from [[Sri Widjaja]], called *Noesa-n-ta-ra* in the [[Pararaton]] — the island under the goddess [[Tara]] where a *pabgjan* (tollhouse) stood. See [[Poelo Kampi]].

## Significance

Aroe exemplifies the east-coast Malay entrepôt eclipsed by the rise of Islamic Acheh. Its fall in [[1539]] marks the moment Acheh secured the northern Strait, a precondition for its sixteenth-century wars with [[Malaka]] and [[Johor]]. The legendary queen [[Poetri Idjo]] — the Green Princess of [[Deli]] and [[Asahan]] tradition — is interpreted by de Roo as a memory of the pre-Islamic royalty of Aroe.
