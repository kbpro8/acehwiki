**Keumala** was the spiritual and political centre of resistance in [[Groot-Atjeh]] during the [[Aceh War]]. Located in the interior, it served as the organisational hub from which the resistance—led by the spiritual leaders and coordinated with [[Panglima Polim]]—maintained its campaign against Dutch authority from the [[1870]]s until its capture on [[14 June]] [[1898]].

## Political and spiritual significance

The court of [[Keumala]]—with everything belonging to it—was regarded by Dutch strategic analysts, notably [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]], as the core of resistance. In his [[1892]] report, [[Snouck Hurgronje]] concluded that the court should be left aside as a matter of indifference to the course of events—yet the Dutch government's actual policy had been the opposite: for years, [[Keumala]] had been protected and cultivated out of respect for the sultanate party, with money and gifts sent to maintain the illusion that the pretender-sultan could serve as a cooperative partner.

The organiser of the resistance—who coordinated the spiritual and military dimensions of the war—received from the government the favours and benefits that enabled him to continue the struggle. The old [[Compagnie]] system of coast closure was considered valid for [[Atjeh]] until [[1890]], yet the Atjeh people—living from what the soil produced—organised resistance from within and could continue to do so peacefully, even in [[1889]].

## The capture (1898)

On [[14 June]] [[1898]], during the [[Pidië Expedition]] led by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], [[Keumala]] was taken. The event—described by Dutch sources with the phrase "savve qui peut" from the sultan's party—marked the definitive end of any realistic hope for restoring the sultanate in [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The capture eliminated the organisational centre that had directed resistance and confirmed that direct military action, guided by accurate ethnographic analysis, was the only viable path to pacification.

## After the capture

Following the fall of [[Keumala]], the resistance lost its coordination hub. The subsequent five-year campaign against [[Panglima Polim]]—culminating in his unconditional submission in [[1903]]—was made possible in large part by the elimination of [[Keumala]] as an organisational base. The event thus represents one of the decisive turning points of the [[Aceh War]].
