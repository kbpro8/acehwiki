The seat of the "court" maintained by the young pretender-Sultan Muhammad Dawot Shah after the fall of Kutaraja to the Dutch, where ulamas including Teungku Tiro paid ceremonial visits, and where the boy-sultan was known for his patronage of cricket-fighting contests.

**Keumala** was an interior settlement in [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] where the young Sultan [[Muhammad Dawot Shah]] held court during the [[Aceh War]] period, when the Dutch had driven the sultanate from [[Banda Acheh]] and the sultan was in the interior. Sayyid families from [[Mecca]] settled in [[Pidie]] and made occasional journeys to Keumala, where the young sultan treated them with the highest honour.

The location of Keumala in the interior highlands placed it beyond easy Dutch reach during the initial phases of the war, making it a symbolic refuge for Acehnese sovereignty during the period of resistance.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Keumala

**Keumala** is a place in [[Pidie]] territory which, after the loss of the [[Dalam (Sultan's Court)|Dalam]] and the death of [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]], became the seat of the fugitive Sultan's "court" of the pretender to the sultanate of [[Acheh]]. In the closing decades of the independent sultanate and during the early years of the Dutch war it was the destination of pilgrimages by uleebalangs seeking the sultan's sealed deeds of recognition, and the source of such political authority as the ruined monarchy still exercised.

In *The Achehnese* Vol. I [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] repeatedly describes Keumala as the place to which the pretender [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] withdrew, and from which he continued to put out the *sarakata* — the sealed deeds of appointment of chiefs — that gave the only surviving form of royal sanction to the holders of uleebalangships. The deeds themselves, which derive their common name from the *chup sikeureung* or "ninefold seal" at the top of the sheet, are composed according to established models with slight occasional modifications; the document translated at the end of the chapter on the constitution, by which the present [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] was recognised, was issued by the pretender at Keumala. The trifling details by which one such *sarakata* differs from another represent the only variations obtainable by the chiefs on this side of the "linie" or Dutch pale, who succeeded in obtaining their deeds by pilgrimages to Keumala.

These pilgrimages were not merely formal. They had a religious aspect: on the occasion of the famous journey of Teuku Ne' of [[Meura'sa|Meura'sa]] and his followers to the court at Keumala, all who accompanied him were implored by their friends and relations to bring back with them some water wherein the pretender to the sultanate had washed his feet. The young ne'er-do-well who stood at the head of the ruined dynasty was thus treated with a reverence that bordered on the devotional. Some members of a sayyid family from [[Mecca]] settled in [[Pidie]] also used to make occasional journeys to Keumala, where the young sultan treated them with the highest honour. At the commencement of the war both the rival claimants to the panglimaship of the [[XXVI Mukims]] fled to Keumala, though Teuku Lamreueng in the end re-established himself in his territory and tendered his submission to the Dutch Government — for which he was murdered in Pidie by the adherents of Teuku Lampaseh.

In all this, Keumala represents the last shadow of the old royal power. The sultanate at once exhibited itself in all its nullity: there was no single individual or body competent or authorised to adopt resolutions as to the fate of Acheh or even of the capital itself, since there was no guarantee that such resolutions would be binding on others. The journeys to Keumala undertaken by the uleebalangs who had tendered their submission to the Government did result in the recognition of Teuku Nya' Banta as Panglima Sagi of the XXVI Mukims, but the influence that the chiefs imagined they would attain by means of these deeds of recognition — viz. some influence in the choice of the successor — was never actually reached. A new title-bearer did not report himself to the capital until the most influential men in his district had agreed on his appointment.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Keumala

**Keumala** (also called **Kuta Keumala**, "the fort of Keumala," in the formal style of the sultans' edicts) was a place in [[Pidie]] territory which became, after the loss of the [[Dalam (Sultan's Court)|Dalam]] in the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese war]], the seat of the fugitive **pretender-sultan** [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] and of his "court." Snouck Hurgronje describes it as the place to which the court of the pretender removed itself when driven from the capital, and which then continued "very much the same as it had been before the war, the scene alone being changed." It lay far from the theatre of the conflict, in the safe interior of [[Pidie]], and was thus chosen for the same reason that had made the original Dalam powerless — its remoteness from the real seats of Achehnese political life.

The court at Keumala was, in Snouck Hurgronje's view, "never a party, but merely a court clique, which continued the traditions of the feeble Dalam that subsisted in the capital in former days." Its effective manager was [[Tuanku Asem]] (Hashim), the young sultan's guardian, a man of royal blood most bitterly hostile to the Dutch Government but reluctant himself to take a serious share in the contest. Under his influence the court bargained in the pettiest spirit over the market value of royal deeds of appointment, and turned everything possible into hard cash for the benefit of the "court." Correspondence was kept up with such men as [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] and [[Teuku Uma]] in regard to money matters and the interests of the Sultan's friends, but Keumala exercised little real influence on the actions of either.

The court's chief remaining function was the issue of **sarakatas** — sealed edicts of appointment bearing the *chab sikureu'eng* or nine-fold seal — to those uleebalangs who had succeeded to their territories and now made ceremonial **pilgrimages to Keumala** in order to obtain the Sultan's recognition. Such pilgrimages had a double value: they gave the chief a visible warrant of his dignity, and they brought some profit to the impecunious court. The journeys of submitted uleebalangs were sometimes arranged between the Dutch Government and the court — a compromise from which [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]] at first sought to dissuade the chiefs, until [[Teuku Uma]] pointed out that the resulting revenues would make the court less demanding on himself. Even after such formal recognition Teungku Tiro "submitted to no legal dictation from Keumala, nay even refused on more than one occasion to satisfy the sultan's demands for money contributions." [[Teuku Uma]] similarly "yielded to the Sultan only as much as he chose, and looked on his sealed letters of appointment as a mere ornament."

The reverence which still clung to the person of the sultan was illustrated by the practice of those who accompanied [[Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa|Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa]] on his famous journey to Keumala: they were implored by friends and relations to bring back with them some water wherein the pretender had washed his feet, the young ne'er-do-well being still regarded by a part of his subjects as *keramat*. Yet this feeling of awe was coupled, within living memory, with the conviction that the sultan exercised no perceptible influence on affairs and was in fact "an expensive luxury like the documents sealed with the *chab sikureu'eng*." The uleebalangs in the sultan's immediate neighbourhood sometimes waged war against him, compelling him to seek help elsewhere; "Keumala, the seat of the sultanate, had to be subdued by force in the first instance by the royal family itself."

The history of Keumala during the war was thus one of steady insignificance. About [[1896]] the pretender was compelled, owing to a quarrel with the local chiefs, to break up his "court" at Keumala and to settle "first in one place and then another." Since the Dutch had begun to deal seriously with the government of [[Great Acheh]] and the subjugation of its dependencies, the pretender had been obliged to lead a wandering life, seeking safety by constantly shifting his abode and finding his asylum as a rule in the [[North Coast|north-coast districts]]. The final break-up of the court at Keumala was thus a decisive moment in the dissolution of the already nominal sultanate.
