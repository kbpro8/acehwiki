**The Strait of Malaka** — the narrow sea-passage between the east coast of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] and the west coast of the Malay Peninsula, connecting the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea and constituting the principal maritime chokepoint of the western Malay-Indonesian archipelago — was the principal strategic theatre of the early Dutch-Portuguese naval war in the East Indies, the scene of the [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign of 1606]], the [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Verhoeff expedition of 1608]], the [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese carrack of October 1602]], and the [[Cornelis Pietersz.|Cornelis Pietersz.]] relief of the Portuguese blockade of Djohor in [[1603]]. The Strait of Malaka is the principal named maritime location in Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The strategic geography

The Strait of Malaka is the principal maritime chokepoint of the western Malay-Indonesian archipelago, connecting the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea and constituting the chief commercial route between the pepper ports of the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] coast and the spice islands of the eastern archipelago. The [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of Malaka in 1511]] gave the Portuguese command of the Strait's narrowest point and the principal commercial city of the region; the rise of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the sixteenth century gave the sultanate command of the Strait's northern entrance; and the sultanate of [[Djohor|Djohor]] controlled the Strait's southern entrance. The contest for the Strait — among the Portuguese at Malaka, the sultans of Atjeh, the sultans of Djohor, and the European East India companies — defined the strategic framework of the early-modern western Malay-Indonesian archipelago.

## The early Dutch-Portuguese naval war (1602-1608)

The early Dutch-Portuguese naval war in the Strait of Malaka was the principal military theatre of the early VOC period. The [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|Anglo-Dutch capture of the Portuguese carrack of 3 October 1602]] — the joint operation of [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]]'s English ships and [[Joris van Spilbergen|Joris van Spilbergen]]'s Dutch ships, having sailed from Atjeh on [[21 September]] [[1602]] and spread themselves across the Strait — was the first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval prize in the East Indies. The [[Cornelis Pietersz.|Cornelis Pietersz.]] relief of the Portuguese blockade of Djohor on [[7 November]] [[1603]] was the first major Dutch-Portuguese naval engagement at the mouth of the Djohor river.

The [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign of 1606]] was the first major Dutch siege of Malaka. [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] appeared before Malaka on [[30 April]] [[1606]] with eleven ships, blockaded the garrison into near-surrender, was forced to engage a Portuguese relief fleet under the Viceroy of India on [[18 August]] [[1606]] (losing two ships but killing the Viceroy), retired to Djohor to repair, and returned on [[20 October]] [[1606]] to destroy the remaining Portuguese fleet on [[22 October]] [[1606]]. The [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Verhoeff expedition of 1608]] was the second Dutch attempt; Verhoeff found the Portuguese garrison reinforced and the sultan of Djohor unwilling to help, and raised the blockade at the end of the year.

## The Portuguese and the Malay sieges

The Strait of Malaka was also the scene of the long Malay-Portuguese contest that had begun with the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of 1511]]. The deposed sultan of Malaka and his successors at [[Djohor|Djohor]] harassed Portuguese shipping and blockaded Malaka in turn; the sultans of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] conducted the great Achehnese siege of [[1 February|1-17 February]] [[1575]] and several smaller attacks; and the Portuguese conducted punitive expeditions against Bintang, Djohor and the east coast of Sumatra. The [[1587]] treaty between Atjeh and the Portuguese, by which [[Aru|Aru]] was again assigned to Djohor, opened a generation of relative peace; the Dutch arrival at Atjeh in [[1599]] reopened the strategic contest.

## Significance

The Strait of Malaka is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal strategic theatre of the early Dutch-Portuguese naval war in the East Indies and the principal named maritime location of the Atjeh-Djohor-Malaka chapter. The Strait's strategic geography — connecting the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea, commanding the approaches to the Atjeh pepper coast and the Djohor river — made it the principal object of contest in the early-modern western Malay-Indonesian archipelago, and the contest for the Strait — among the Portuguese at Malaka, the sultans of Atjeh, the sultans of Djohor, and the European East India companies — defined the strategic framework of the region. The [[Capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh (October 1602)|Anglo-Dutch capture of 3 October 1602]], the [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign of 1606]] and the [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Verhoeff expedition of 1608]] were the principal Dutch-Portuguese naval operations in the Strait in the early VOC period, and the eventual fall of Malaka to the Dutch and their Djohor allies in [[1641]] would close the cycle that the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of 1511]] had opened.
