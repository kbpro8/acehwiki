**Malaka** — the historic port-city at the narrowest point of the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]], conquered by the Portuguese under [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Affonso d'Albuquerque]] in [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|August 1511]] and held by them until the Dutch-Djohor conquest of [[1641]] — was the principal strategic fortress of the Portuguese empire in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago, the chief object of the Achehnese sieges of [[1575]] and the Dutch-Djohor sieges of [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|1606]] and [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|1608]], and the central place in Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The Portuguese conquest (1511)

The full account of the Portuguese conquest is given in the entry on the [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|conquest itself]]. In summary: [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Albuquerque]] sailed from [[Cochin|Cochin]] on [[2 May]] [[1511]] with nineteen ships, anchored before Malaka in mid-June [[1511]], and took the city after assaults on [[25 July]] and [[10 August]] [[1511]]. He built the **Fortaleza de Malaca** with materials from the royal tombs and from demolished mosques, introduced a Portuguese administration and a new coinage, and seized the tin mines as crown property. In Europe, Pope **Leo X** ordered a general thanksgiving; in [[1559]] Pope **Paul IV** raised Malaka to a bishopric.

## The Achehnese sieges (1573-1587)

After the conquest, the deposed sultan of Malaka retired to **Bintang** and later to [[Djohor|Djohor]], from where he and his successors harassed Portuguese shipping and blockaded Malaka in turn. The sultans of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — pressing from the west along the north Sumatran coast — became the most dangerous Muslim rivals of the Portuguese. The great Achehnese siege of Malaka of [[1 February|1-17 February]] [[1575]] nearly succeeded; the 150 Portuguese defenders, ill-supplied and short of ammunition, considered it a miracle when the Achehnese withdrew. The [[1587]] treaty between Atjeh and the Portuguese, by which [[Aru|Aru]] was again assigned to [[Djohor|Djohor]], opened a generation of relative peace.

## The Dutch-Djohor sieges (1606, 1608, 1641)

The [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|Matelieff campaign of 1606]] was the first major Dutch siege of Malaka. [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] appeared before Malaka on [[30 April]] [[1606]] with eleven ships, concluded the first accord with the sultan of [[Djohor|Djohor]] on [[17 May]] [[1606]], blockaded the garrison into near-surrender, was forced to engage a Portuguese relief fleet under the Viceroy of India on [[18 August]] [[1606]] (losing two ships but killing the Viceroy), retired to Djohor to repair, and returned on [[20 October]] [[1606]] to destroy the remaining Portuguese fleet on [[22 October]] [[1606]]. The destruction of the Portuguese fleet was a major naval victory, but the city remained in Portuguese hands.

The [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Verhoeff expedition of 1608]] was the second Dutch attempt. [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] appeared before Malaka on [[23 November]] [[1608]], found the sultan of Djohor unwilling to help and the Portuguese garrison reinforced, and raised the blockade at the end of the year. The failure of the expedition marked the abandonment of the project to take Malaka before the [[1641|Dutch-Djohor conquest of 1641]].

## The Portuguese administration

The Portuguese administration of Malaka was characterised by what Van der Woude calls "willekeur en zeeroof" — arbitrariness and piracy. The Portuguese commanders at Malaka engaged in punitive expeditions against Malay shipping, blockaded the river of Djohor, and seized Muslim merchant vessels in violation of the sultan's hospitality. The Portuguese position at Malaka was thus both the principal commercial asset of the Portuguese empire in the East and the principal political liability — provoking the Malay sieges, the Dutch-Djohor alliance, and the eventual Dutch conquest of [[1641]].

## Significance

Malaka is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal strategic fortress of the Portuguese empire in the Malay-Indonesian archipelago and the central place of the Atjeh-Djohor-Malaka triangle that frames the early Dutch-Indies relationship. The [[Portuguese conquest of Malaka (1511)|Portuguese conquest of 1511]] was the foundational event of the Portuguese presence in the region and the proximate cause of the rise of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] as the chief Muslim commercial rival of the Portuguese. The Achehnese sieges of [[1575]] and the Dutch-Djohor sieges of [[Matelieff's Malaka campaign (1606)|1606]] and [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|1608]] were the principal military expressions of the contest for the Strait of Malaka, and the eventual fall of Malaka to the Dutch and their Djohor allies in [[1641]] would close the cycle that [[Affonso d'Albuquerque|Albuquerque]] had opened in [[1511]]. Malaka's strategic position — at the narrowest point of the Strait of Malaka, commanding the approaches to both the Atjeh pepper coast and the Djohor river — made it the principal object of contest in the early-modern Malay-Indonesian archipelago.
