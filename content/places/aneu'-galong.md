**Aneu' Galong** was a fortress in the interior of [[Great Acheh|Great-Acheh]] which figured in the closing phase of the [[Acheh|Achehnese]] war as one of the strongholds held by the family and following of the celebrated [[Teungku Tiro]]. It was captured by the Dutch troops in [[1896]], an episode memorable in the chronicle of the sabil-warriors for the death in action of **Mat Amin**, the son of Teungku Tiro.

Snouck Hurgronje's footnote records the event laconically: Mat Amin fell when the Netherlands troops carried the fortress of Aneu' Galong. The capture thus forms one of the milestones by which Dutch authority was slowly extended over the highlands in the years immediately preceding General [[Governor Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s decisive offensives. It also marks the moment at which the family of Teungku Tiro lost its most prominent military heir, while the work of collecting the *sabil* contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]] devolved on the slain man's surviving brothers.

Of those brothers, **Teungku Beb** (who died in [[1900]]) and **Teungku Mahidin** — also called **Ma' Et** — together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng|Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died [[1901]]), a man much respected for his piety and learning, "continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the sabil contributions in the dependencies on the North Coast." Aneu' Galong may thus be read as the geographical symbol of a transition: from the personal leadership of Teungku Tiro's house to the more diffuse and increasingly defensive ulama-resistance of the late 1890s.

The fortress itself is mentioned only in this passing footnote of Vol. I, but its capture is significant because it is one of the few dated engagements with which the chronicler of the Tiro family connects a known casualty. With the fall of Aneu' Galong the effective military arm of the Tiro tradition was broken, although the religious authority of the line persisted long after the Dutch had consolidated their hold on Great Acheh.
