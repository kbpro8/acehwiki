## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pahang

**Pahang** is a Malay state on the east coast of the Malay Peninsula mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as one of the targets of the great seaborne expeditions of the sultanate of Acheh in the [[17th century|seventeenth century]].

### The Seventeenth-Century Expeditions

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Pahang in his survey of the political meaning of the sultanate, in the closing pages of his chapter on the Achehnese political system. He writes that "with the shortlived period of prosperity ended all such glorious expeditions as those undertaken in the 17th century against [[Malacca]] and Pahang, but the bold seafarers and pirates of Acheh continued to make themselves dreaded along the coasts of [[Sumatra]] and among the surrounding islands. The monopoly of trade claimed by the great sultans could in the end no longer be enforced."

The Pahang expeditions of the 17th century thus belong to the great age of the sultanate — the age of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] and his predecessors — when the Achehnese fleets were powerful enough to attack not only the Portuguese at Malacca but also the Malay states of the peninsula. Snouck's point is that the surviving sultanate of his own time was the ruined relic of that political and military power.

### Significance

Although Pahang itself appears in Vol. I only in this comparative reference, the mention is significant for Snouck's central thesis: that the sultanate of Acheh in its closing decades was, politically, only the ghost of its former self. The Pahang and Malacca expeditions of the 17th century stand as the high-water mark of Achehnese arms, against which the enfeebled sultanate of the late 19th century — unable even to keep its own piratical Tuankus of [[Kuala Batee]] in check — is to be measured. Vol. II of *The Achehnese* contains further comparative material on the relations of Acheh with the Malay states.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Pahang

**Pahang** is a Malay sultanate occupying the east coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]], drained by the Pahang River — the longest river in peninsular Malaysia — and traditionally oriented towards the [[China|China]] Sea and the trade of the South China Sea rather than towards the Strait of Malacca. Although it appears only once in the surviving correspondence assembled by P. H. van der Kemp in his study of the [[Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819|Aceh agreement of 1819]], the single mention is striking: it places Pahang within a list of places a Dutch official proposed to seize if only the Netherlands would send a sufficient force to the eastern approaches of the Strait.

The mention comes in the private letter of 16 March 1819 from Governor **Thyssen** of [[Malacca]] to Rear-Admiral **Wolterbeek** at Batavia, printed by Van der Kemp in his appendix. Thyssen was alarmed at [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s recent movements and at reports of British intentions against the [[Karimon Islands]] and [[Lingga]]. "Bovendien zoude men dan bezit kunnen neemen van Pahang, Lingen en alle zulke plaatsen als ons convenieerde," Thyssen urged — "moreover, one could then take possession of Pahang, Lingen and all such places as suited us." The implication is striking: a senior Dutch official in the Strait of Malacca, in the spring of 1819, was prepared to contemplate a Dutch forward movement not only on the [[Riouw|Riau]] archipelago but on the east coast of the Malay Peninsula itself.

The strategic logic was that, if Britain was to seize the [[Karimon Islands|Carimons]] and [[Singapore]] and from there press on the Dutch position at Riouw, the Netherlands should answer by extending their own reach along the peninsula — including the east-coast sultanate of Pahang. Such a counter-move would have placed the Dutch astride the South China Sea end of the peninsula and complicated the British position in the Strait immeasurably. In the event, no such action was taken; the Dutch could muster neither the force nor, it seems, the political will. The episode reveals the kind of bold strategic thinking that circulated in Dutch official correspondence of 1819, even when nothing came of it.

Pahang's inclusion in Thyssen's list also reflects the broader Malay political geography of the period. The sultanate of Pahang was at this time closely connected with the [[Johor]] successor states of [[Riouw]] and [[Lingga]]; its ruler, Bendahara Ali, was a member of the same Temenggong Johor dynasty that would shortly consolidate power in Johor. To think of Pahang, Lingen and Riouw in a single breath was therefore not geographical fancy but a recognition that the Malay sultanates of the peninsula's east coast and the Riau-Lingga archipelago formed a single political system. The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch treaty of 1824]] would, of course, divide that system: the Riau-Lingga chain became Dutch, while Pahang and the other peninsular sultanates fell within the British sphere that would, after 1874, become the Federated Malay States.
