**The Umbilin coal field** (also *Oembilin*) is the most important coal field of central [[Sumatra]] and one of the most productive coal fields of the colonial [[Netherlands East Indies]], located on the western coast of Sumatra about 165 km from [[Emma Haven]] (the harbour of [[Padang|Padang]]). The discovery of the coal seams dates from [[1868]], when the mining engineer [[De Greve|De Greve]] found outcrops in the river Umbilin; exploitation began in [[1892]] after the construction of a most difficult railway from the mine to the harbour of Padang, and production reached its maximum capacity of 600,000 tons per year in [[1916]]. The Umbilin coal field is included in this wiki because it is the principal coal-mining operation of the colonial Netherlands East Indies, and accordingly the comparative benchmark against which the disappointing [[Coal in Atjeh|Atjeh coal occurrences]] were assessed.

## The 1868 De Greve discovery

Van Bemmelen's account of the [[1868]] discovery reads:

> "The discovery of the coal seams dates from [[1868]] when the mining engineer DE GREVE found outcrops in the river Umbilin."

The [[1868]] discovery by [[De Greve|De Greve]] is the founding episode of the Umbilin coal industry, and it places De Greve — who is also named (alongside [[J. H. Menten|Menten]] and [[S. W. Tromp|Tromp]]) among the first generation of geologists to study the natural oil wells of the East Indies after the [[1859]] Pennsylvania oil boom — at the head of the documentary sequence of central-Sumatran coal exploration. For the full account of De Greve see the entry on [[De Greve]].

## Geology and reserves

The Umbilin coal fields belong to the Eocene formation, composed of sandstones, clay shales and conglomerates, in which are intercalated 3 coal seams A, B and C, respectively 2 m, 1 m and 6 to 10 m thick. They occur at a mutual vertical distance of 30 m, and dip 12° East. Only the A and C seams have been worked. The total reserves in the area of about 20 sq km have been estimated at more than 200 million tons, distributed across five fields: the Sungei Durian field (93 mill. tons), the Sigalut field (80 mill. tons), the Parambahan coalfield (in exploitation), the Tanah Hitam coalfield, and the Sugar Terrain.

## Production history

The exploitation of the Umbilin coal field began in [[1892]], after the construction of a most difficult railway from the mine to the harbour of Padang. The railway track had to pass the mountain range of the Barisan, about 900 m above sea-level; on account of the steep gradient, rack-rail locomotives had to be used. The first two years' production remained below 200,000 tons a year; then it rapidly increased, in [[1903]] being 300,000, in [[1907]] 400,000 tons, in [[1911]] 500,000 tons, reaching its maximum capacity of 600,000 tons in [[1916]]. The production consisted of 60 % lumps and 40 % small coal. The A-seam is stoped with the long-wall system; the very thick C-seam is worked with the long-wall system for thick seams, divided in benches of moderate thickness, usually 4 benches of 2 m thickness. The empty stopes are refilled by sand by means of the hydraulic-filling method.

## Coal quality

The Umbilin coal is generally stratified and contains sometimes fragments of resin. A combined ultimate and proximate analysis gave the following figures:

- Carbon: 73.05 %
- Hydrogen: 5.22 %
- Oxygen and nitrogen: 12.72 %
- Sulphur: 1.97 %
- Ash: 3.17 %
- Moisture: 3.87 %
- Volatile: 41.05 %
- Fixed carbon: 51.91 %
- Calorific value: 6970 cal.
- Coke-buttons: lustrous, baked.

## Significance for Atjeh

The Umbilin coal field is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because it is the principal coal-mining operation of the colonial Netherlands East Indies, and accordingly the comparative benchmark against which the disappointing [[Coal in Atjeh|Atjeh coal occurrences]] were assessed. Van Bemmelen's summary judgment on the Atjeh coal exploration — "Several explorations for coal have been made in the northern part of Sumatra (Atjeh and Dependencies) but the results were disappointing, as large quantities of coal have not been discovered" — is implicitly a comparison with the Umbilin coal field, where large quantities of coal *had* been discovered and successfully mined. The contrast between the productive Umbilin field (with its 200-million-ton reserves and its 600,000-ton-per-year peak production) and the disappointing Atjeh coal occurrences (with their thin seams of "impure coal of a poor quality") is the central fact of the colonial coal geography of northern Sumatra, and it accordingly determined the shape of the colonial coal-mining industry on Atjeh-relevant territory (the [[Kinandam-Sumatra Mijnbouw Mij.]] concessions at Bukit Durian, Bukit Pulai and Sarik, which produced only 2,000 tons over [[1917]]–[[1927]] for use at the [[Salida]] gold mine).
