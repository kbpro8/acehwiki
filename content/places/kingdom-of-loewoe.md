**The Kingdom of Loewoe** (modern **Luwu**) was a polity on the southeastern coast of [[Celebes]] (today [[Sulawesi]]) that, together with [[Boni (Sulawesi)|Boni]], was the focus of the [[Expedition to Boni and Loewoe (1905)|Dutch marine expedition of 1905]]. The kingdom — described in contemporary Dutch reports as a "*rijk*" (realm) with its own fortified settlements — came into direct conflict with the [[Dutch Navy|Royal Netherlands Navy]] during the early twentieth-century Dutch colonial campaigns to extend control over the eastern Indonesian archipelago. The Dutch records note an "*actie tegen het rijk van Loewoe*" (action against the kingdom of Loewoe) carried out in early [[September]] [[1905]], when naval landing forces from the steamships [[Hertog Hendrik|Hertog Hendrik]] and [[De Ruyter|De Ruyter]] engaged the kingdom's defences and stormed the *kraton* (royal palace) of **Opoe Patoenraë**.

## Context

The Kingdom of Loewoe was one of several autonomous Sulawesi polities that retained a degree of self-rule into the early twentieth century. Dutch colonial authorities regarded such polities as obstacles to the consolidation of the [[Dutch East Indies]] and therefore launched punitive expeditions to enforce direct control. The expedition of [[1905]] formed part of the broader [[Pacification of the Dutch East Indies]] — the Dutch effort to extend control over the eastern Indonesian archipelago following the long-running [[Aceh War]] (1873–1904). The marine operation was undertaken by a detachment of the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Royal Netherlands Marine Corps]] under the overall direction of Governor [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] of [[Celebes]], who oversaw the pacification of [[Boni (Sulawesi)|Boni]], Loewoe, and other recalcitrant states, implemented the [[Korte Verklaring]], and established *landschapskassen* (territorial treasuries).

## The Dutch Expedition against Loewoe

According to the naval log dated [[23 August]] (see page 152 of the source), the landing camp at **Pampanoea** was abandoned, and Lieutenant **R. G. M. A. Heg** was detached to oversee the loading of provisions and the evacuation of the sick. In early September [[1905]], the Dutch forces launched an assault on Loewoe. The landing divisions from the steamships [[Hertog Hendrik|Hertog Hendrik]] and [[De Ruyter|De Ruyter]] were placed under the command of Lieutenant-Commander **F. Bauduin** (class F). The operation initially proceeded without gunfire; however, once the troops began disembarking, they encountered armed resistance from Loewoe's defenders, who opened "*lilla- en geweervuur*" (rifle and musket fire). The Dutch sloops returned fire, but the fortified positions at **Pandjalaë** (likely a local stronghold) could not be taken.

During the landing, a group of locals was observed preparing two rifles; Lieutenant **F. A. Kimball** intervened, extinguishing a burning fuse and removing gunpowder to prevent an accidental explosion. A brief skirmish ensued, resulting in four Loewoe combatants killed and Dutch casualties that included Lieutenant-Commander **J. A. van Zadelhoff** (who later died of tetanus in a [[Makassar|Makassar]] hospital), a boatswain, and a drummer.

By 11 a.m., the Dutch forces had secured the beachhead, and by 2 p.m. they halted approximately 600 m from the residence of **Opoe Patoenraë** (a local leader). Two companies advanced on the residence, attacking the southern façade of the *kraton* (palace). The division from [[Hertog Hendrik|Hertog Hendrik]] stormed the north-western side, while the division from [[De Ruyter|De Ruyter]] remained in reserve. After receiving fire from the village perimeter, a Dutch sailor was seriously wounded. By 2 p.m., the *kraton* was in Dutch possession, and the landing camp was subsequently occupied.

## Outcome

The capture of the Loewoe *kraton* marked a decisive Dutch victory in the region, effectively ending organised resistance from this particular Sulawesi polity. The operation demonstrated the Dutch navy's capability to project power onto inland Celebes territories using coordinated amphibious assaults. The success also paved the way for subsequent Dutch actions against other resistant polities in the eastern archipelago, such as the later campaigns in [[Bali|Bali]] and [[Lombok|Lombok]], although those were geographically distinct.

## Significance

The Loewoe episode illustrates the transition from large-scale conventional warfare (the [[Aceh War]]) to targeted punitive expeditions aimed at subjugating residual autonomous entities across the wider Dutch East Indies. It highlights the role of naval landing forces in extending colonial authority beyond coastal enclaves, and underscores the importance of small-scale engagements in the final consolidation of Dutch rule over the eastern Indonesian archipelago. The 1905 expedition against Boni and Loewoe was the direct continuation, on the post-Atjeh-oorlog pattern, of the mobile naval-infantry methods developed by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in Atjeh — the same year that saw Van Heutsz's elevation to Governor-General and the application of his Aceh-proven methods to the rest of the archipelago.
