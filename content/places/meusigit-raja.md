**The Meusigit Raja** — the Royal Mosque — is the Mohammedan place of prayer in the capital of [[Atjeh|Aceh]], [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta Radja]], built on the site where the first mosque of Koeta Radja was said to have been built during the reign of the Acehnese sun-king [[Sultan Iskandar Muda]]. The Meusigit Raja is the set-piece of the chapter on the mosque in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]): its history — the original mosque, the great fire of about fifty years later, the rebuilding, the historic storming by the Dutch troops in [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]], the burning-down and demolition, the rebuilding by [[generaal Van Swieten|the conqueror of the Acehnese, Van Swieten]] as a gift to the Acehnese population, and the Snouck Hurgronje judgement that the mosque was "an empty monument of our folly" — is the book's set-piece of the colonial state's religious politics in Aceh.

## The original mosque and Sultan Iskandar Muda

The Meusigit Raja is on the site where the first mosque of Koeta Radja was said to have been built during the reign of the Acehnese sun-king, [[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] (ruled c. 1607-1636). The original mosque — the book records — was destroyed in a great fire about fifty years later. The palace of the Sultan, with all the treasures that had been piled up in it, was on that occasion also a prey of the flames. The great fire of about fifty years after Iskandar Muda destroyed both the mosque and the Sultan's palace with all its treasures.

## The rebuilding and the storming of April 1873

The mosque was rebuilt much later on the same site, following the model of the mosque at Medan, but with the application of the primitive atap (sewn coconut-leaf) roof.

At the historic storming of this mosque by the Dutch troops in [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]] — the [[Eerste Atjeh-expeditie (1873)|First Aceh Expedition]] — the building burned down, and later it was completely demolished.

## The Van Swieten rebuilding and the letter of 8 February 1874

The new place of prayer — the Meusigit Raja — was built with the consent and cooperation of the Dutch colonial government. It was the conqueror of the Acehnese — [[generaal Van Swieten|Van Swieten]] — who brought about that the colonial government, entirely at its own cost, built a new mosque, which was given to the Acehnese population to make them forget the war's suffering and to demonstrate, in the Islamic land par excellence, that the Netherlands perfectly respected the Mohammedan religion.

The war-hero General Van Swieten, in a missive of [[8 February]] [[1874]] to some Acehnese chiefs, wrote the well-known lines: that the great mosque would be rebuilt and made into a true place of mercy, from which no more death and destruction, but prayers to the almighty God and works of love would go out.

## The Snouck Hurgronje judgement

[[Snouck Hurgronje|Dr Snouck Hurgronje]] called this house of God — intended as a proof of our reconciling disposition and generosity towards the Acehnese enemy — "one of the many expensive mistakes that marked the beginning of our Aceh interference," and this eminent scholar qualified the mosque as "an empty monument of our folly." The Snouck Hurgronje judgement — an empty monument of our folly — is the book's — and the colonial state's — sharpest critique of the Van Swieten rebuilding.

One should, however, consider that one then lived in the raw reality of the war years.

## The laying of the first stone (9 October 1879) and the transfer (27 December 1881)

The first stone of the mosque was laid on [[9 October]] [[1879]] in the presence of Governor-General [[Van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]], all the civil and military authorities, and many Acehnese chiefs and clergy. The mosque was completed in [[1881]] and on [[27 December]] [[1881]] was solemnly transferred to the Acehnese people by the governor of Aceh and Dependencies, [[Pruys van der Hoeven|Pruys van der Hoeven]], in the name of the Dutch government.

## The B.O.W. and the salary

The maintenance of the Meusigit Raja always rested with the B.O.W. (Burgerlijke Openbare Werken — the Civil Public Works department). At the transfer it was determined that the mosque personnel would receive a monthly salary from the government: the imam f 75, the chatib (Friday sermon reader) f 50, and each of the two bilals f 35. This salary would cease at the ceasing-to-function of the then incumbents — but, the narrator notes, they had still been receiving payment from the government, and this payment took place nowhere else in the Dutch East Indies.

## The Acehnese indifference and the Chinese construction

The building — designed by the B.O.W. in Byzantine style, executed by the lieutenant of the Chinese at Koeta Radja — never enjoyed particular popularity with the population. On the contrary: both during the construction and later, the Acehnese stood at his most indifferent towards it. Not one Acehnese made himself available as a workman at the construction, which was entirely performed by Chinese workforce — that is, by pig-eating unbelievers — and the work was bitterly mocked by the Acehnese.

They (the Acehnese) considered it the forced benefaction of the most unsympathetic unbeliever, the Hollander. They used to compare the mosque-dome to a bas (a kind of fish-trap). The prayers that rose from a temple built by unbelieving dogs they considered to be of no value whatsoever.

## The grievances

The book also registers the Acehnese grievances about the Meusigit Raja: that the mosque at Koeta Radja was festively illuminated on the birthday of H.M. the Queen but that on the Mohammedan feast-days only the ordinary illumination was lit; that the presence of two hotels in the vicinity was anathema; that the place where the ritual washings must be performed was not covered; that it was so full on Fridays that some dared to say it would be better to hold the religious services on the football field.

## The restoration and the twenty thousand

The book's chapter on the Meusigit Raja concludes with the prospect of a restoration — the twenty thousand guilders that the Mohammedan believers in Aceh would bring together, with a twelve-thousand-guilder contribution from the proceeds of the export duty on population-rubber. And then the historic mosque will surpass all former glory, especially because it has now found the sympathy of the Mohammedan population. For all errors have their time. Misjudgements and misunderstandings, misconceptions, hatred and bitterness pass away. *Le cœur oublie!*

## Significance

The Meusigit Raja is significant in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* as the set-piece of the colonial state's religious politics in [[Atjeh|Aceh]]. The book's argument — that the Van Swieten rebuilding was intended as a proof of the colonial state's respect for the Mohammedan religion, but that the Acehnese population had received it with indifference and mockery because it was the forced benefaction of the unbelieving Hollander, built by pig-eating unbelievers — is the book's clearest example of the colonial state's failure to understand the Acehnese religious politics. The Snouck Hurgronje judgement — an empty monument of our folly — is the book's — and the colonial state's — sharpest critique of the Van Swieten rebuilding. The Meusigit Raja is therefore the book's marker of the colonial state's religious politics in Aceh: the failure of the forced benefaction.
