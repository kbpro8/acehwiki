**Pedir** (modern *Pidie*) was a historic coastal town, port and rice‑producing plain on the north‑eastern (the “East” in Van der Kemp’s terminology) coast of [[Acheh]], situated west of [[Penang]] across the [[Straits of Malacca]] and within easy reach of the sultanate’s capital [[Banda Acheh|Banda Acheh]]. Throughout the pre‑colonial, early‑modern and colonial periods it served as a chief pepper‑export centre, a place of captivity for Dutch sailors, the residence of the ousted [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]], and the site where the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] was concluded. In the late nineteenth century it became a focal point of Dutch naval operations during the [[Aceh War (1873–1904)]], notably the bombardment of [[29 December]] [[1873]] by a squadron under [[Captain-terr zee Van Gogh]]. The town’s strategic harbour, fertile plain and position at the mouth of the [[Atjeh River]] made it repeatedly important in Acehnese‑European trade, diplomacy and warfare.

## Early European contacts (1509–1601)

### The Portuguese visit (1509)

Pedir was one of the two pepper ports – with [[Pasei|Pasei]] – that the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] called at on the way to [[Malaka|Malaka]], “waar de Portugeezen toestemming verwierven om handel te drijven”. This was the first European commercial contact with the Atjeh coast and the foundation of the European pepper trade with the region. Within a generation Pedir and Pasei became the chief attractions of the Atjeh coast to European traders; the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman brothers' visit of [[1599]]]] called at Pedir for rice and would later find one of its crew taken prisoner there.

### The Houtman captivity (1599–1601)

After the ambush of [[11 September]] [[1599]] at Atjeh, the surviving Dutch captives – including [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] – were held partly at Pedir. A sloop from the *Leeuw* sent to Pedir for rice was captured; the Dutch sailors aboard were taken prisoner. The **sabander of Pedir** – the harbourmaster acting on the sultan’s authority – rearrested Houtman on a fabricated theft charge after his December [[1600]] escape, an incident that provoked [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]]’s seizure of the Arab junks on [[12 January]] [[1601]].

Five of the surviving Dutch captives escaped from Pedir in a prauw in [[1600]] and reached Van Caerden’s ships safely – the escape that warned Van Caerden of the sultan’s planned attack and led him to advance payment on the pepper contract. Houtman himself escaped from Pedir on [[30 December]] [[1600]] with three companions in a small prauw they had fashioned; the vessel overturned in the surf but the men reached Van Caerden’s ship on New Year’s Eve [[1601]].

## Pepper trade in the early VOC period

Pedir was one of the chief pepper sources of the Dutch East India Company at Atjeh. The [[pepper contract of 28 December 1600]] between [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]] and the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589–1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] covered pepper from the new harvest, a portion of which was to be delivered at Pedir. The [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland mission of August 1601]] bought pepper at Pedir as well as at Atjeh; the [[Jan Grenier|Jan Grenier]] fleet of December [[1601]] called at Pedir on its way to the west coast of Sumatra.

## A regional chiefdom in the early nineteenth century

In the early nineteenth century Pedir was a relatively powerful chiefdom of the Achehnese East Coast. Its chief in [[1815]], [[Tunku Pakih]] (Van der Kemp’s spelling *toekoe Paekie* / *Packie*), was described as “de betrekkelijk machtige potentaat van Pedir” and was reckoned among the principal backers of the revolution that year which raised [[Said Hussein]]’s son to the throne as [[Sultan Saif ul‑Alum|Sultan Saif ul‑Alum]] (Syf). When Syf quarrelled with Tunku Pakih, the latter returned to the party of the deposed Jauhar Alum, who from that time fixed his seat at Pedir. The town thus became the centre of the old sultan’s authority, while Syf transferred his rival court to [[Telok Semawé]].

Tunku Pakih was a persistent source of trouble to Europeans. A few days before [[Captain Coombs]]’ visit to [[Banda Acheh|Acheh]] in early [[1818]] he had seized a brig under British colours, an exploit that became the subject of Coombs’s representations to the Achehnese chiefs. By the time of the [[Raffles–Coombs commission (1819)]] he was dead – “Packie bestond niet meer”, in Van der Kemp’s phrase – but his legacy still shaped Pedir’s relations with the capital.

## The Raffles–Coombs commission and the Treaty of Pedir (1819)

A bitter dispute divided [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] from [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and Coombs over whether the commission should proceed first to Pedir – Raffles’s friend Jauhar being there – or to the capital where the recognised sultan Syf’s party was nominally in power. The Calcutta government later endorsed the Bannerman–Coombs view that going first to Pedir “would undoubtedly have been decidedly contrary to the spirit of our instructions”. Nevertheless, while the commission was at [[Banda Acheh]], Raffles dispatched the Company ship *Minto* under [[Lieutenant Criddle]] to Pedir to notify Jauhar of the commission’s approach – the [[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir]] – without informing either Bannerman or Coombs.

After seven fruitless weeks at Acheh the commissioners sailed for Pedir, where on [[22 April]] [[1819]] they concluded with Jauhar Alum the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]]. By its articles the sultan granted free trade in his ports, promised to exclude other European powers and Americans from fixed residence, and consented to receive a British agent. Van der Kemp judged the treaty “slechts scheurpapier” – mere waste paper – since Jauhar could not enforce a single stipulation.

## Later visits and the early nineteenth‑century decline

A transport vessel sailing from [[Penang]] to [[Calcutta]] in mid‑[[1820]] called at Pedir, and [[Colonel O'Hallaran]] made his formal visit to Sultan Jauhar – the [[O'Hallaran's visit to Pedir (1820)]]. A fellow‑traveller’s letter, printed in a Penang newspaper on [[2 June]] [[1820]], gave a famously unflattering account of the sultan, “a poor creature surrounded only by a set of half starved chooliahs sitting in a wretched common atap house”. Jauhar spoke of his plans to march “all along the sandy beach from Pedir to his good city of Acheen” with great guns in front, but the writer mocked: “I dare say never one half of his present intentions will be executed”. When Governor Phillips urged Jauhar in [[1822]] to remove to the capital, the sultan declined, pleading lack of force; he remained at Pedir until his death in December [[1823]].

## Geography, the Pedir plain and agriculture

The Pedir plain – the *Pedir‑vlakte* – lies on the North Coast between the *hoek Pidië* and the basin of the [[Kr. Peusangan|Kr. Peusangan]]. Its fertile expanse is bounded on the landward side by the *Goudberg* ([[Seulawaih Agam|Seulawaih Agam]], 1726 m) and the *Weesberg* ([[Seulawaih Inòng|Seulawaih Inòng]], 868 m) – the “Gold Mountain” and “Orphan Mountain” of the Atjehsche highlands – and watered by small north‑coast rivers that rise in the deforested highland to the south‑west. The handbook describes the plain as “uitstekend in cultuur gebrachte en geïrrigeerde” – excellently cultivated and irrigated – with about five‑eighths of its 270 km² under sawah. Rice yields were said to reach “méér dan 60 pikol (droge) rijst per bouw” in a normal year.

According to the 1917 *De Buitenbezittingen* the Pedir plain, with some 25 000 *bouws* of sawah, was the principal rice‑exporting region of Atjeh, and the town of [[Sigli|Sigli]] – administrative capital of the *afdeeling Noordkust van Atjeh* – stood at its edge.

## Political and administrative history

### Pre‑colonial suzerainty

Pedir was, in the pre‑colonial period, one of the half‑mythical great Sultanates of the North and East Coasts – alongside [[Samoedra‑Pasé|Samoedra‑Pasé]], [[Perlak|Perlak]], [[Aroe|Aroe]] and the western polity of [[Daja|Daja]]. The expansion of the [[Sultanate of Atjeh|Sultanate of Atjeh]] in the early sixteenth century brought these coast‑states under its loose suzerainty; the appointment of *stedehouders* (lieutenants, equivalent to [[Oelèëbalang|oelèëbalangs]]) and *Sjahbandars* (harbour‑kings) by the Sultan established a pattern of Atjehsche influence that endured, increasingly nominal, down to the Dutch arrival. With the decay of the great Sultanate, Pedir – like the other vassal states – broke away, and the small *landschapjes* of the colonial period emerged from its fragments.

### The Groot‑Atjehsche administrative system

In the early colonial period the *Groot‑Atjehsche* system – [[gampong|gampong]], [[moekim|moekim]], [[oelèëbalang|oelèëbalang]], [[sagi|sagi]] – survived most completely in Pedir. *De Buitenbezittingen* notes that “men het Groot‑Atjehsche bestuursstelsel hier [in Pedir] vrijwel ongeschonden terugvindt”, though the smallest unit was called *meunasa* rather than *gampong*. The [[Kawaj XII|Kawaj XII]] – a federation of twelve gold‑seeker settlements in the highlands between Beutông and Keumala Dalam, ruled by a *keudjeuroeën mas* (gold customs‑collector) appointed by the Sultan – persisted until its dissolution in [[1914]]; its depopulation resulted from the decline of gold workings and the cholera epidemic of [[1878]] and led to its partition between the *onderafdeelingen* of [[Sigli|Sigli]] and [[Meulabôh|Meulabôh]].

## The Aceh War (1873–1879) and Dutch military actions

Pedir was a coastal state whose radja repeatedly opposed the Dutch during the [[Aceh War|Atjeh‑oorlog]]. The town was bombarded by a Dutch squadron on [[29 December]] [[1873]]–[[31 December]] [[1873]] because its radja had marched with his men to the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] to help defend it during the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873–1874)|second Aceh expedition]]. After the bombardment the Dutch established a fortification at Pedir in [[1876]]; the post met opposition from the self‑reliant chief [[Toekoe di Tiroe|Toekoe di Tiroe]]. In [[February]] [[1877]] the fort was moved to [[Segli|Segli]], and in [[May 1878]] Governor [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]] led an expedition to the upper lands of Pedir, where Toekoe di Tiroe remained hostile.

The upper lands of Pedir became a refuge for irreducible hostile chiefs – [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polim]], his son [[Radja Koelala|Radja Koelala]], the [[Imam of Longbatta|Imam Longbatta]] and others – who entrenched themselves at [[Kemala|Kemala]] in the interior south of Pedir during the campaigns against the XXII and XXVI Moekims in [[1879]].

### The 1873 naval action (new source)

The Dutch marine corps history (*De geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers van 1665‑1945*) records that on [[29 December]] [[1873]] a squadron commanded by [[Captain-terr zee Van Gogh]] attacked Pedir, seized the town briefly and then withdrew to a beach bivouac. The operation was part of a broader strategy to control the mouth of the [[Atjeh River]] and to curtail the flow of supplies to Acehnese resistance. The action underscores Pedir’s continued strategic relevance into the late nineteenth century.

### Significance

Pedir's persistent hostility throughout the first phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] illustrates the limits of Dutch authority outside the immediate neighbourhood of [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]]. The landscape's notables — especially the self-reliant chief Toekoe di Tiroe — kept up resistance long after the formal conquest of Great Acheh in [[1879]], and the upper lands of Pedir served as the refuge of the irreducible hostile chiefs until the next phase of the war under Governor [[Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] after [[1898]].

## Economy

The Pedir plain’s rice surplus was, until the European war, mostly hoarded rather than exported – “de Pedirees, liever dan zijn productie‑overschot te verkoopen, dit gedurende jaren opschuurt”. The war‑time pressure exerted by the Dutch administration to release these hoards made Pedir rice available for export to the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|Oostkust van Sumatra]] in [[1914]] and [[1915]], substantially reducing the territory’s dependence on imported rice.

## Significance

Pedir’s significance spans several centuries. In the early modern period it was the principal north‑coast pepper port of Atjeh, the main site of captivity for [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] during the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|1599‑1601 captivity]], and the trigger for [[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]]’s seizure of Arab junks on [[12 January]] [[1601]]. Its pepper trade attracted the first European merchants from the [[Diogo Lopez de Sequeira|Sequeira]] mission of [[1509]] onward, and its rice plain supplied the capital’s population.

In the nineteenth century Pedir became the residence of the deposed sultan [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] and the venue for the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]], a treaty that, despite its waste‑paper character, marked a moment of British‑Dutch diplomatic engagement with Aceh. The town’s harbour and plain continued to be strategically valuable during the [[Aceh War (1873–1904)]], as demonstrated by the bombardment of [[29 December]] [[1873]] and the later Dutch fortifications, and by its inclusion in Dutch marine corps records.

Overall, Pedir illustrates the intertwining of trade, diplomacy and warfare on the Acehnese north coast, linking early pepper commerce, colonial captivity narratives, nineteenth‑century treaty politics and the protracted Dutch military campaign to subdue the Sultanate of Aceh.
