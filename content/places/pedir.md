**Pedir** (modern *Pidie*) is a town and historic region on the north-eastern — what Van der Kemp calls the East — coast of [[Acheh]], situated to the west of [[Penang]] across the [[Straits of Malacca]] and within easy reach of the sultanate's capital [[Banda Acheh]]. In the early nineteenth century it served both as the residence of the ousted [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and as the place where the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] was actually concluded.

## A regional chiefdom

Pedir was in this period a relatively powerful chiefdom of the Achehnese East Coast. Its chief in 1815, [[Tunku Pakih]] (in Van der Kemp's spelling *toekoe Paekie* / *Packie*), was described as "de betrekkelijk machtige potentaat van Pedir" and was reckoned among the principal backers of the revolution that year which raised [[Said Hussein]]'s son to the throne as [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum]] (Syf). When Syf quarrelled with Tunku Pakih, the latter returned to the party of the deposed Jauhar Alum, who from that time fixed his seat at Pedir. The town thus became the centre of the old sultan's authority, while Syf transferred his rival court to [[Telok Semawé]].

Tunku Pakih was a persistent source of trouble to Europeans. A few days before [[Captain Coombs]]' visit to [[Banda Acheh|Acheh]] in early [[1818]] he had seized a brig under British colours, an exploit that became the subject of Coombs's representations to the Achehnese chiefs. By the time of the Raffles–Coombs commission in [[1819]] he was dead — "Packie bestond niet meer", in Van der Kemp's phrase — but his legacy still shaped Pedir's relations with the capital.

## The Raffles–Coombs commission

A bitter dispute divided [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] from [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and Coombs over whether the commission should proceed first to Pedir — Raffles's friend Jauhar being there — or to the capital where the recognised sultan Syf's party was nominally in power. The Calcutta government later endorsed the Bannerman–Coombs view that going first to Pedir "would undoubtedly have been decidedly contrary to the spirit of our instructions". Nevertheless, even while the commission was at [[Banda Acheh]], Raffles dispatched the Company's ship *Minto* under [[Lieutenant Criddle]] to Pedir to notify Jauhar of the commission's approach — the [[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir]] — without informing either Bannerman or Coombs.

After seven fruitless weeks at Acheh the commissioners sailed for Pedir, where on [[22 April]] [[1819]] they concluded with Jauhar Alum the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]]. By its articles the sultan granted free trade in his ports, promised to exclude other European powers and Americans from fixed residence, and consented to receive a British agent. The treaty was, however, in Van der Kemp's verdict, "slechts scheurpapier" — mere waste paper — since Jauhar could not enforce a single stipulation.

## Later visits

A transport vessel sailing from [[Penang]] to [[Calcutta]] in mid-[[1820]] called at Pedir, and [[Colonel O'Hallaran]] made his formal visit to Sultan Jauhar — the [[O'Hallaran's visit to Pedir (1820)]]. A fellow-traveller's letter, printed in a Penang newspaper on [[2 June]] [[1820]], gave a famously unflattering account of the sultan, "a poor creature surrounded only by a set of half starved chooliahs sitting in a wretched common atap house". Jauhar spoke of his plans to march "all along the sandy beach from Pedir to his good city of Acheen" with great guns in front, but the writer mocked: "I dare say never one half of his present intentions will be executed". When Governor Phillips urged Jauhar in [[1822]] to remove to the capital, the sultan declined, pleading lack of force; he remained at Pedir until his death in December [[1823]].

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Pedir

**Pedir** is the historical name of the region and port on the north coast of Sumatra — known natively as [[Pidië|Pidië]] — that gave its name to the *Pedir-vlakte*, the great sawah plain between the [[Goudberg|Goudberg]] and [[Weesberg|Weesberg]] heights and the sea that was, at the time of the Encyclopaedisch Bureau's *De Buitenbezittingen*, the most intensely cultivated and densely populated rice plain of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]]. The Pedir plain, with some 25,000 *bouws* of sawah, was the principal rice-exporting region of Atjeh, and the town of [[Sigli|Sigli]] — administrative capital of the *afdeeling Noordkust van Atjeh* — stood at its edge.

## Geography and the plain

The Pedir plain lies on the North Coast between the *hoek Pidië* and the basin of the [[Kr. Peusangan|Kr. Peusangan]]. Its fertile expanse is bounded on the landward side by the *Goudberg* ([[Seulawaih Agam|Seulawaih Agam]], 1726 m) and the *Weesberg* ([[Seulawaih Inòng|Seulawaih Inòng]], 868 m) — the "Gold Mountain" and "Orphan Mountain" of the Atjehsche highlands — and watered by the small North Coast rivers that rise on the deforested highland to the south-west. The plain is, in the handbook's phrase, "uitstekend in cultuur gebrachte en geïrrigeerde" — excellently cultivated and irrigated — with about five-eighths of its 270 square kilometres under sawah. The rice yields were said to be so abundant that "de landbouwer er in een normaal jaar rekenen mag op een oogst van méér dan 60 pikol (droge) rijst per bouw" — the farmer could reckon on a harvest of more than 60 piculs of dry rice per bouw in a normal year.

## Political history

Pedir was, in the pre-colonial period, one of the half-mythical great Sultanates of the North and East Coasts — alongside [[Samoedra-Pasé|Samoedra-Pasé]], [[Perlak|Perlak]], [[Aroe|Aroe]], and the western polity of [[Daja|Daja]]. The expansion of the [[Sultans of Atjeh|Sultanate of Atjeh]] in the early sixteenth century brought these coast-states under its loose suzerainty; the appointment of *stedehouders* (lieutenants, equivalent to [[Oelèëbalang|oelèëbalangs]]) and *Sjahbandars* (harbour-kings) by the Sultan established the pattern of Atjehsche influence that would endure, in increasingly nominal form, down to the Dutch arrival. With the decay of the great Sultanate, Pedir — like the other vassal states — broke away, and the small *landschapjes* of the colonial period emerged from its fragments.

## The Atjehsche bestuursstelsel

In the early colonial period, the *Groot-Atjehsche* administrative system — [[Gampong|gampong]], [[Moekim|moekim]], [[Oelèëbalang|oelèëbalang]], [[Sagi|sagi]] — survived most completely in Pedir. *De Buitenbezittingen* notes that "men het Groot-Atjehsche bestuursstelsel hier [in Pedir] vrijwel ongeschonden terugvindt" — one finds the Groot-Atjehsche administrative system here virtually intact — though with the distinction that the smallest administrative unit was called *meunasa* rather than *gampong*. The [[Kawaj XII|Kawaj XII]] — the federation of twelve gold-seeker settlements in the highlands between Beutông and Keumala Dalam, ruled by a *keudjeuroeën mas* (gold customs-collector) appointed by the Sultan — was, until its dissolution in [[1914]], a peculiarly Pedir institution. Its depopulation, the result of the decline of the gold workings and the cholera epidemic of [[1878]], led to its partition between the *onderafdeelingen* of [[Sigli|Sigli]] and [[Meulabôh|Meulabôh]].

## Economy

The Pedir plain's rice surplus was, until the European war, mostly hoarded rather than exported — "de Pedirees, liever dan zijn productie-overschot te verkoopen, dit gedurende jaren opschuurt" — the Pedir farmer, rather than sell his surplus, would store it for years, sometimes "zwart van ouderdom en aangetast door ongedierte" (black with age and infested with vermin) but still fit for consumption. The war-time pressure exerted by the Dutch administration to release these hoards made Pedir rice available for export to the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|Oostkust van Sumatra]] in [[1914]] and [[1915]], and substantially reduced the territory's dependence on imported rice.
