**Kampong Djawa** (Kampong Java, "Javanese Kampong") was the village on the left bank of the [[Atjeh-rivier]] opposite the Dutch bivouak at [[Penajoeng]] on the right bank, which housed Chinese traders and was occupied by the Dutch early in the war. Its position opposite Penajoeng made it a natural observation point for the left bank of the river and a base for operations against the enemy kampong belt of [[Lampoeloe]], [[Lampoe Oek]], and [[Longbatta]] upstream.

## The Chinese traders and the left bank

The kampong's name — *Djawa* (Java) — points to its original character as a settlement of foreign traders in the Atjeh sultanate. Chinese traders had long been established at Atjeh, importing arms, buskruit and levensmiddelen through Penang and Singapore; Kampong Djawa was their principal residence on the left bank of the Atjeh-rivier. The Dutch occupation of the kampong early in the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873–1874)|second expedition]] gave the [[Bezettingskorps in Atjeh|bezettingskorps]] a useful bridgehead on the left bank opposite the main bivouak at Penajoeng. From Kampong Djawa the Dutch could observe the enemy's left bank and sally against any attempt to *verschansen* (entrench) along the river. By [[1875]] the kampong had become a separate *kampement* for Chinese *werklieden* and *koelies* imported from Penang and Singapore for the construction works of the Dutch occupation.

## In the war

Kampong Djawa appears throughout Borel's narrative as a familiar landmark of the left bank. On [[19 June]] [[1874]] a column of 150 bayonets and an artillery section marched from [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] to clear the left bank north of Kampong Djawa, while [[Luitenant Sloet van Zwanenburg|luitenant Sloet van Zwanenburg]] led a patrol from Penajoeng on the right bank. The day was unfavourable: the Atjehneezen held [[Lampoeloe]]; Sloet was discovered in his ravine, badly shot up, had to retreat in haste, severely wounded, and died of his wounds days later. The left-bank column could find no position to enfilade and returned empty-handed. By [[1878]] Kampong Djawa had become a *redundant* post on the western side of the Atjeh-rivier, its function as a Chinese *kampement* overtaken by the larger Dutch establishment at K. Radja. Borel notes that the kampong was then among the redundant posts whose maintenance drained the limited strength of the garrison.
