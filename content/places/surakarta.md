**Surakarta** (also called **Solo** or **Sala**) is a royal city of central [[Java]] which is mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I in Snouck Hurgronje's survey of the Achehnese custom of giving proper names to particular cannons. It is cited as the home of **Setomi**, one of a series of personified guns of the Java-Sea region that are compared with the Achehnese sacred cannon **Lada Sichupa**.

## Named Cannons of Java

Snouck's reference to Surakarta comes at the very beginning of the legend of the cannon *Lada Sichupa* of [[Acheh]]. "It is well known," he writes, "how common has been the custom amongst native peoples of giving proper names to certain cannons, which they regarded more or less as personified and even worshipped as sacred after a time. We may instance *Si Penjagio* at [[Batavia]], and its consorts *Si Ajiak* in [[Banten]] and **Setomi** at Surakarta, with many more. Similarly in Acheh there were many such guns with proper names, among them that mentioned above, which bore the title *Lada Sichupa* — 'a *chupa* of pepper.'"

## The Comparative Setting

The comparison with Surakarta is part of Snouck's wider effort to show that Achehnese civilisation, in its cannons and their legends as in much else, must be understood as one local variant of a common Indonesian (and indeed Malay-Indonesian) cultural pattern. The named cannons of Java — those of [[Batavia]], [[Banten]], and Surakarta — provide the comparative material against which the Achehnese *Lada Sichupa* stands out. The legend of *Lada Sichupa* itself, narrated immediately after the comparison, connects the gun to a tradition about the cannon having been brought from [[Pidie]] and having been used in the defence of the [[Dalam]] of the Sultan of [[Acheh]].

## Significance

Surakarta's appearance in Vol. I is brief, but it places the city in a network of comparative references by which Snouck seeks to position Achehnese institutions in the wider Indonesian world. As one of the great royal cities of central [[Java]], Surakarta had its own court traditions, its own *kraton* and its ownnamed cannon, the **Setomi**, which Snouck treats as a parallel instance of the personification of artillery that he had observed at [[Batavia]], [[Banten]], and elsewhere. The reference is part of his continuing argument that the political and religious life of Acheh cannot be understood in isolation but must be set against the background of the broader Malay-Javanese culture area.
