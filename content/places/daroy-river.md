## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Krueng Daroy

**Krueng Daroy** (Achehnese *Kruëng Daroy*) is a river of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], at the source of which — in a cave traditionally chosen for such devotions — the ulama **Habib Samalanga** practised his *tapa* (hermitical seclusion) and imposed the same discipline upon his followers. The river is one of the sacred watercourses of the Achehnese, mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje in connection with the religious exercises of the most celebrated of the Achehnese sayyid-warleaders.

### The Tapa of Habib Samalanga

In the chapter on the ulamas and the political leadership of the [[Acheh War|Acheh War]], Snouck describes the rivalry between [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] and **Habib Samalanga** for the leadership of the jihad. The Habib, in order to appear in the eyes of his followers as at least the equal — and if possible the superior — of "the Teungku", had to distinguish himself from the latter in some particular way. "This he did; his speciality was *tapa* — or seclusion from the world. He would remain isolated (*kleunèt*) for from seven to forty successive days in a cave at the source of the **Krueng (river) Daroy**, a place which was from ancient times chosen for such devotions."

The choice of the cave at the source of the Krueng Daroy is significant. *Tapa* in the sense of absolute hermitical seclusion was, in Snouck's account, admired but seldom practised by the Achehnese; the choice of a place "from ancient times chosen for such devotions" places the cave in the category of recognised hermitages, alongside the cave of [[Lam Guha|Lam Guha]] and the other Achehnese hermit-sites. The Habib's recourse to the cave was thus both a personal discipline and a public assertion of his religious standing.

### The Teungku Lam Guha Reference

A footnote to the same passage records that "a Javanese whose tomb is now an object of veneration at [[Ulee Lheue|Ulee Lheue]] is known by the name of **Teungku Lam Guha**, having done *tapa* for successive years in this cave of the Daroy." The Javanese — apparently an earlier holy man, of Javanese origin, who had settled in [[Acheh|Acheh]] and had practised *tapa* in the same cave — was buried at [[Ulee Lheue|Ulee Lheue]] on the coast, and his tomb was, by Snouck's time, an object of veneration. The name *Lam Guha* — "in the cave" — preserves the memory of his hermitage at the source of the Krueng Daroy, and the veneration paid to his tomb is one of the channels by which the sanctity of the cave was extended to the coast.

### The Geography of the Krueng Daroy

The Krueng Daroy is a river of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], flowing from the highland of the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]] or the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]] down to the coast in the neighbourhood of [[Banda Acheh|Banda-Acheh]]. The "source" of the river — the spring or the headwater at which the cave was situated — is the kind of remote and wooded spot which the Achehnese associated with the practice of *tapa*. The choice of such a spot for the hermitage is consistent with the broader Achehnese (and Malay) tradition by which the high places, the springs, and the caves of the interior were regarded as the natural seats of holiness.

### The Function of the Cave in Achehnese Religious Life

The cave at the source of the Krueng Daroy is one of a small class of Achehnese hermitages which appear in Vol. I of *The Achehnese*. The same chapter on the ulamas mentions the *tapa* of other Achehnese holy men — including the hermits of the [[Gunong Lhee|Gunong Lhee]] and the [[Krueng Blibeh|Krueng Blibeh]] — and the broader institution of *tapa* is treated at length in the chapter on the mystics and the saints. The cave of the Krueng Daroy is thus one of the recognised hermitages of [[Acheh|Acheh]]; its association with the Habib Samalanga on the one hand and with the Javanese Teungku Lam Guha on the other places it in the double tradition of the Achehnese jihad and the older Achehnese sanctity.

### The Tapa as Punishment

Snouck adds that "his followers had also occasionally to submit to a similar course of *tapa*; it was in fact one of the ordinary punishments imposed by him for various offences." The use of *tapa* as a punishment — imposed by the Habib upon his followers — is one of the small facts by which the practical working of the Achehnese religious movement is illustrated. The cave of the Krueng Daroy was thus not only the seat of the Habib's personal devotions but also the place at which the discipline of his following was maintained; it was at once a hermitage and a penitentiary, and the Krueng Daroy takes its place in the Achehnese sacred geography as one of the principal sites of the religious revival of the late nineteenth century.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Krueng Daroy

**Krueng Daroy** (Achehnese *Kruëng Daroy*) is a river of [[Acheh|Acheh]] rising in the highlands, whose source is the site of a celebrated cave associated from ancient times with the religious practice of *tapa* — that is, with the seclusion, fasting, and devotional exercises of Achehnese ulamas and holy men. The cave at the source of the Krueng Daroy is mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I principally in connection with the devotional practice of [[Habib Samalanga|Habib Samalanga]] and, in a footnote, with that of [[Teungku Lam Guha|Teungku Lam Guha]].

Snouck Hurgronje's account of the rivalry between [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] and [[Habib Samalanga|Habib Samalanga]] in the early 1890s introduces the cave of the Daroy as the Habib's chosen place of seclusion. "His specialty," Snouck writes of the Habib, "was *tapa* or seclusion from the world. He would remain isolated (*kaleuët*) for from seven to forty successive days in a cave at the source of the Krueng (river) Daroy, a place which was from ancient times chosen for such devotions." The cave was thus no private fancy of the Habib but an inherited seat of Achehnese religious austerity, hallowed by long usage and selected by him for the practice of the very devotion with which he sought to distinguish himself from his rival. The Habib's followers had also occasionally to submit to a similar course of *tapa*; it was, indeed, one of the ordinary punishments imposed by him for various offences. Whoever was brought before him convicted of a particular sin — and especially the sin of consorting with the Dutch, the one which made the offender a complete outlaw — was condemned to isolation in the cave, there to do penance for some days and prepare himself for conversion from his heresy.

The footnote to the passage records the wider traditional association of the cave with the practice of *tapa*: "A Javanese whose tomb is now an object of veneration at [[Ulee Lheue|Ulee Lheue]] is known by the name of [[Teungku Lam Guha|Teungku Lam Guha]], having done *tapa* for successive years in this cave of the Daroy." The Javanese hermit — whose posthumous name *Lam Guha*, "in the cave," records the manner of his devotions — is a characteristic figure of the older Achehnese piety: a foreign-born ascetic who, by prolonged seclusion in a hallowed spot, won for himself the reputation of sanctity and a venerated tomb in one of the principal burial-grounds of the capital. The same cave, chosen "from ancient times" for such devotions, thus connects the Habib's nineteenth-century austerity with the earlier Javanese hermit and, through him, with the older, anonymous succession of those who had used the place before him.

The river Daroy itself is one of the highland watercourses of Great Acheh, descending from the interior towards the coast. Its source, in the highlands, lies in the kind of secluded, well-watered spot that is naturally chosen for a hermitage; the cave at the source, supplied with water and sheltered from intrusion, supplied the physical conditions of *tapa*. The name *Daroy* is not otherwise explained by Snouck, and the river's lower course receives no detailed treatment in Vol. I. The cave at its source, however, occupies a recognised place in the religious topography of the country — alongside such other hallowed spots as the tombs of [[Sayyid Teungku Anjong|Teungku Anjong]] at [[Gampong Java|Gampong Java]] and of [[Teungku di Kuala|Teungku di Kuala Abdora'oh]] at [[Singkel|Singkel]] — and was, in the closing years of the nineteenth century, still in active use as a place of devotional seclusion.
