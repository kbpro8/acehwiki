**Singapore** is the island city‑state at the southern tip of the [[Malay Peninsula]] that was founded as a British trading post in [[January]] [[1819]] and became a crown colony after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] confirmed British control. Situated at the mouth of the [[Strait of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]], it rapidly developed into the chief entrepôt of the [[Straits of Malacca|Malacca Straits]] and a pivotal hub for regional trade, including the smuggling routes linking the western coast of [[Borneo]] to the [[Sultanate of Aceh]]. Its strategic position placed it at the centre of Anglo‑Dutch rivalry in the archipelago and made it a focal point for British anti‑piracy patrols that intersected with Acehnese commerce.

## Founding and Early Colonial Administration

In late [[January]] [[1819]] Sir [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] planted the British flag on the island, founding the settlement that within a decade would eclipse [[Penang]] as the principal European port on the Straits. Raffles’ decision followed a recommendation from [[Major William Farquhar|William Farquhar]] of [[Malacca]] to consider the [[Karimon Islands]]; the allure of the Karimon site caused Raffles to postpone his planned mission to [[Acheh]] and to inspect the islands, only to find Singapore more suitable. He returned to [[Penang]] by [[16 February]] [[1819]], having secured the island for the British. 

Raffles’ own correspondence dated [[12 June]] [[1819]] to [[Sir Robert Harry Inglis]] records the foundation as the platform from which he reported the conclusion of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] with the old king of Aceh. The Dutch Governor [[Timmerman Thyssen]] wrote on [[11 November]] [[1818]] that the British move “appeared to be a settled plan of Penang,” reflecting Dutch alarm at the unexpected British foothold.

## Linguistic and Publishing Significance

*The Achehnese* (Vol. I) cites Singapore chiefly for its distinctive Malay dialect. The local Malay differs from northern Peninsula Malay in several lexical items: *jambatan* replaces *titi* for “bridge,” and *longtang*—normally “interval” in Javanese calendars—means “narrow drain or ditch” in Singaporean Malay. Administrative terms introduced by the British, such as *mukim* (parish) and *sawah* (irrigated rice‑fields), also entered local usage, reflecting the broader impact of the Straits Settlements on regional language.

Singapore was a major centre of Malay printing in the early nineteenth century. Works printed there include:

* The lithographed Malay version of the **Indra Bangsawan** (published in the month of [[Muharram]] A.H. 1310 by Haji Muhammad Tayib).  
* The **Tambih rapilin**, produced at the lithographing establishment of Haji Tirmidi.  
* Various editions of the **wasiat nabi** (the “last admonition of the Prophet”), printed and re‑printed in Malay.

These publications illustrate Singapore’s role as a conduit for literary and religious texts circulating between the Malay world, [[India|Hindustan]], and the Islamic scholarly centres of [[Medina]].

## Singapore and the Aceh Trade Network

The island quickly became a focal point for the East Coast trade of Sumatra. By the late [[1820s]] Singapore attracted coffee, pepper, rattans, wax and gold dust from the [[Sumatra's East Coast]] in exchange for Siamese salt, Javanese tobacco and European manufactured goods. The Dutch, fearing the loss of their own commercial influence, intensified hostility toward Singapore. 

On [[29 November]] [[1827]] the resident [[John Prince]] in [[Batavia]] reported that the Dutch government’s antagonism was so strong that “there are no exertions or pains they would hesitate to take for its destruction.” The Dutch response included the **Dutch Salt Price Reduction**, lowering the export price of Java salt from 70 to 30 rupees per coyan, a “prefatory step” to undermine Singapore’s trade. The subsequent **Bellona mission** (sent by Commissioner‑General [[Du Bus|Commissioner‑General Du Bus]]) aimed to persuade the chiefs of [[Siak]], [[Kampar]], [[Jambi]] and other East Coast states to source salt from [[Riouw|Riouw]] rather than Singapore, underscoring the strategic competition.

British officials such as [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] reinforced Singapore’s position. Fullerton’s letter to the Sultan of Siak dated [[7 February]] [[1828]] and his circular to nine Siak chiefs were part of a broader diplomatic effort to secure the island’s commercial dominance against Dutch pressure.

## Cession to Britain and International Recognition

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London of 17 March 1824]] transferred Singapore, together with [[Malacca]], from Dutch to British sovereignty in exchange for [[Benkoelen]] and the Dutch factories in [[Hindostan]]. Dutch negotiators had previously, on [[24 July]] [[1820]], included a clause ordering the evacuation of “Singa‑Poura,” reflecting ongoing Dutch anxiety over the loss of the island.

## Cultural Connections and Literary Transmission

Singapore’s printing activity facilitated the diffusion of South [[India]]‑originated mysticism and romance literature throughout the archipelago. Works such as the Malay version of the **Hikayat Indra Bangsawan**, the **Malim Deman** tale of the [[Menangkabau]] Malays, and the Batak story of **Malin Deman** found a receptive audience in Singapore’s presses. Indian scholars like [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Raniri]] of [[Gujerat]] contributed to the region’s religious literature, while Syrian and Egyptian pandits arriving via [[Mekka]] also left their mark on Acehnese and Malay literary traditions.

## Significance

Singapore’s foundation illustrates the intersection of colonial ambition, linguistic diffusion, and commercial rivalry in early nineteenth‑century Southeast Asia. As a British foothold, it reshaped the balance of power between the British Empire and the Dutch East Indies, intensified competition over the lucrative Acehnese trade, and served as a crucible for the spread of Malay literary and religious texts across the archipelago.
