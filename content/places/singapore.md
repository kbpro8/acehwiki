A number of Achehnese who remained in [[Acheh]] 'as traders or teachers' had come through the pilgrimage route via [[Acheh]] to Singapore and on to Arabia. [[India]] (Hindustan) [[India]] is cited throughout as the ultimate source of many Islamic mystical traditions, hikayat materials, and religious legends that reached [[Acheh]]. 'There can be no doubt... that this mysticism was brought hither by the pioneers of Islam from Hindustan.' 'The most important works on mysticism in vogue in the Archipelago were penned by Indian writers, or else are derived from a body of mystics which flourished in [[Medina]] in the 17th century and which was strongly subject to Indian influence.' [[Raniri]] from Gujerat is mentioned as a non- Achehnese pandit who wrote the Qirat al-mustaqim in [[Acheh]]. The 16th-17th century pandits who held mastery in [[Acheh]] were 'not Achehnese, but either Syrians or Egyptians who came to [[Acheh]] from Mekka, or else natives of [[India]], such as [[Raniri]] from Gujerat.' 'The South-Indian Islam, the oldest form in which Mohammedanism came to this Archipelago still survives in these works.' Many popular romances in Achehnese and Malay can be traced to South [[India]]: 'The great majority of romances in both tongues the birthplace ... is that portion of South [[India]] which is also the source whence are derived the popular mysticism and the popular religious legends.' Persian names (Jamshid, Bahramshah, Qubad, etc.) entered the Achehnese hikayats through Indian intermediaries. The [[Menangkabau]] Malays' hikayat Malim Deman is related to the Achehnese [[Malem Diwa]]. The Batak story of Malin Deman also has isolated points of resemblance.

Multiple Malay religious and literary works were published in Singapore: 'A lithographed edition of the Malay version of Indra Bangsawan was published in the month of Muharram A.H. 1310 by Haji Muhammad Tayib at Singapore.' The Tambih rapilin was 'printed at the lithographing establishment of Haji Tirmidi in Singapore.' Also various editions of the wasiat nabi were 'printed and reprinted in Malay at Singapore.'

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Singapore

**Singapore** (Malay **Singapura**) is the British colony at the southern tip of the [[Malay Peninsula]] cited in *The Achehnese* Vol. I chiefly for the Malay spoken there, which is repeatedly contrasted — in vocabulary and in custom — with the Malay of the northern Peninsula and with the Achehnese. Although a comparatively minor presence in the book, Singapore furnishes several useful illustrations of the linguistic and administrative diversity of the Malay world.

The Malay of Singapore differs in a number of small but characteristic words from the Malay of the northern part of the Peninsula. *Titi*, a dialectic variation of *titian* ("a bridge"), is the word for "bridge" in the Malay of the northern part of the Peninsula; in [[Malacca]] and Singapore it is *jambatan*. The word *longtang*, which means "interval" in the Javanese month-names (and is so used in the calendar of [[Java]] and of Acheh), curiously enough means "a narrow drain or ditch" in the Malay of Singapore. The city is also noted as the place of publication of a Malay work — the Ganjamaia, Singapore Edition — in which the expression "the Judge, the Almighty Lord" (= God) is found in the same sense as in some Achehnese contexts.

The administrative vocabulary of the British settlements in the Straits has also left its mark on the language of the region. The word *mukim*, in its stricter sense, corresponds almost exactly to the English word "parish"; in [[Penang]] and Province Wellesley the English Government adopted the word to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes. Singapore, as one of the Straits Settlements, shares in the general British colonial usage that brought such Malay terms into administrative vocabulary, and so forms part of the wider background against which the institutions of [[Acheh]] are described. *Sawah*, the Malay word for irrigated rice-fields used in the southern parts of the Peninsula (Johor, Malacca etc.) and also in Java, gives way in Penang, Province Wellesley, [[Kedah]] etc., and in Acheh, to *blang* — a distribution in which Singapore, on the southern fringe, naturally follows the southern usage.

These linguistic comparisons, though incidental to the main argument of the book, serve to remind the reader that the Achehnese are not merely a people of the East Indian Archipelago in general, but specifically a people whose language and customs stand at the northern extremity of a continuum that runs down the Peninsula to the great British ports of the Straits. Singapore and Penang, as the chief European ports of the region, are also the chief places of comparison against which the insular individuality of Acheh is measured.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Singapore

**Singapore** is the island at the southern end of the [[Malay Peninsula]] on which, in late January [[1819]], Sir [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Thomas Stamford Raffles]] planted the British flag, founding the settlement that within a decade would eclipse [[Penang]] and become the chief entrepôt of the [[Straits of Malacca|Malacca Straits]]. Van der Kemp treats Singapore chiefly as the unexpected and, for the Dutch, deeply unwelcome by-product of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Acheh commission]] — the [[Founding of Singapore]] being the [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|detour]] that interrupted, and very nearly derailed, the mission to [[Acheh]].

## The Karimon detour

In January [[1819]], while [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] was preparing at [[Penang]] to sail for [[Acheh]], he received a letter from Major [[Major William Farquhar|William Farquhar]] of [[Malacca]] recommending the [[Karimon Islands]] as a site for a British station. The invitation so took Raffles's fancy that he postponed the entire Acheh mission and, "without previous intimation either to that Government or to his colleague Captain Coombs", sailed off to inspect the Karimons, taking with him the artillery that Bannerman had destined for Acheh. The Karimons did not answer; Singapore did. Raffles was back at Penang by [[16 February]] [[1819]]. "Hij had voor zijn land in dien korten tijd eene groote daad verricht", Van der Kemp concedes — "zoo waarlijk de grondslag voor eene belangwekkende stad gelegd, een anti-Batavia!". The new foundation threw Dutch officials at Malacca into alarm; Governor [[Timmerman Thyssen]] wrote to [[Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]] on [[11 November]] [[1818]] that the taking of the Karimons and "het koningrijk Adcheen" appeared to be a settled plan of Penang.

## Raffles's own account

Raffles himself dated his letter to [[Sir Robert Harry Inglis]] from Singapore on [[12 June]] [[1819]], and there gave his account of the Acheh commission: "We remained there (in Atjeh) nearly seven weeks, during the early portion of which we were directly opposed in our polities, but at length after a paper war which actually occupies above a thousand pages of the Company's largest sized paper, he came round to my opinion…". The newly founded Singapore was thus the platform from which Raffles reported the conclusion of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] to his friends in London. [[Carnegy|Carnegy's]] letter from Penang of [[27 July]] [[1819]] — quoted by Van der Kemp — already noted that "we have a flag on [[St. John's]] and in war time, a few heavy guns on St. John's will give us complete command of the Straits of that place", and reported that Raffles had made a treaty with the old king of Acheh which "excludes all other Europeans and Americans".

## Cession to Britain

By the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of London of 17 March 1824]] the Netherlands ceded Singapore to Britain, together with Malacca, in exchange for Benkoelen and the Dutch factories in [[Hindostan]]. Van der Kemp treats this cession as a costly surrender, made for the sake of an undivided Dutch sovereignty over [[Sumatra]]. The concept-convention of [[24 July]] [[1820]] — the [[Concept-convention of 24 July 1820]] — had already contained an article by which the British Government would "donner les ordres nécessaires pour l'évacuation de Singa-Poura"; and the Dutch plenipotentiaries had repeatedly demanded the disavowal of Raffles's proceedings in the Archipelago. Singapore, once ceded, rapidly became the principal object of Dutch anxiety.

## Singapore and the East Coast trade

By the late 1820s Singapore was drawing to itself the trade of [[Sumatra's East Coast]] — coffee, pepper, rattans for the China market, wax and gold dust, exchanged for Siam salt, Java tobacco and the manufactures of Western India and Europe. The resident [[John Prince]], reporting from [[Batavia]] on [[29 November]] [[1827]] on the [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] of Commissioner-General [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Du Bus]], warned that the Batavian government's hostility to Singapore was so great "that there are no exertions or pains they would hesitate to take for its destruction". The [[Dutch Salt Price Reduction]] — the lowering of the export price of Java salt from 70 to 30 rupees per coyan — was, in Prince's reading, a "prefatory step" to that destruction. The Bellona mission itself, sent to encourage the chiefs of [[Siak]], [[Kampar]], [[Jambi]] and the other East Coast states to take their salt from [[Riouw|Riouw]] instead of from Singapore, was the practical expression of that hostility. [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s [[Fullerton's letter to Sultan of Siak (7 February 1828)|letter to the Sultan of Siak of 7 February 1828]] and [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular to the nine Siak chiefs]] must be read against this background.
