Java is the most frequently cited comparison for Achehnese customs, learning, and culture throughout the book. The Java pesantrens (religious schools) and pondoks are compared with Achehnese rangkangs. The Javanese method of religious instruction (bandungan and sorogan) is compared with Achehnese methods. Java is also the destination of pilgrims who pass through [[Acheh]]. The Javanese primbons (memorandum-books) are compared with Achehnese mystical manuals. The Javanese play (wayang) is compared to Achehnese sadati performances. In the Hikayat [[Malem Diwa]], [[Malem Diwa]] swallowed by a whale 'drifts to Java where he is cast on shore.' The hikayat Ureueng Jawa (story of a Javanese) is one of the hikayat ruhe. The Achehnese sometimes follow the Arabs in applying the name 'Jawa' to the Malays as well as the Javanese: 'an Achehnese abusing a Padang man will call him Jawa paleh = miserable Malay!' Also referenced: In Java the tiger-games and marbles are widely spread. In Java a form of rain-making involves giving the cat a bath. In Java the Ni Towong superstition involves a figure composed of a creel dressed as a woman, placed in a cemetery by old women. Javanese and Sundanese follow different methods of religious instruction than the Achehnese. Madiun and Surabaya are large towns where Javanese students are drawn to study. The prang desa (village war) in Java required police intervention before it was brought under control.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Java

**Java** is the most populous island of the [[East Indian Archipelago]] and the chief seat of Dutch colonial power, with [[Batavia]] as its principal city. Throughout *The Achehnese* Vol. I, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] uses the institutions of Java — particularly its Mohammedan marriage customs, its *pangulu* system, and its agricultural calendar — as the standard of comparison against which the divergent usages of [[Acheh]] are measured. Where Acheh preserves archaic features, Java has either developed new ones under Hindu and Islamic influence or has been moulded by recent Dutch legislation.

The contrast is especially sharp in matters of family law. In Java the *ta'liq* marriage contract, by which a husband's extra-judicial divorce is hedged about with conditions in favour of the wife, was ordained by the rulers of the country in olden times, not only in the kingdom of Mataram but also in such places as [[Chirbon]] and [[Banten]]; the Javanese tradition attributes the institution to their great Sultan Ageng. Acheh, by contrast, is wholly ignorant of the *ta'liq*. So too with the officials who celebrate marriages: in Java they are called *naib* or district *pangulus* (with their subordinates, the *ketip*), in [[Mekka]] *mumlik*, in [[Medina]] "licentiates for the making of marriages," in [[Egypt]] *fiqih*, and in Acheh *teungku meunasah*. Snouck Hurgronje repeatedly returns to these administrative divergences as evidence that what is sometimes described as a single "Mohammedan law" of the Archipelago is in fact a patchwork of local adats.

Java is also the chief source of the technical vocabulary that has filtered into Achehnese usage. Sweetmeats are called *peunajah* in Acheh and are, as in Java, very various in form and name; the *kukisan* of Java reappears in Acheh as the *punchcue'* used for steaming glutinous rice; the Achehnese *langgar*, *bale'* or *tajug* — the prayer-house of the gampong — bears the same appellations as in Java. The *ratib Samman*, the noisy litany that takes its name from the saint of [[Medina]], is popular in Java as well, and may be heard almost every week in many Batavian kampongs. Even the Javanese month-names of the Mohammedan calendar — *Apit*, *Sila*, *Longtang*, *Sura* and the rest — recur in Acheh with local modifications, and the Javanese legend of the Kalangs, in which a people is descended from the incestuous offspring of a woman and a dog, has its Achehnese counterpart in the myth of the origin of the inhabitants of [[Nias]].

Beyond the parallels of custom and vocabulary, Java supplies a cautionary lesson in the writing of legal history. Mr. L. W. C. Van den Berg, in his essay on the divergences from Mohammedan law as to family and inheritance in Java and Madura, employed as data for determining the ancient institutions of Java regulations which appear in various law-books compiled under Hindu influence — a method Snouck Hurgronje dismisses as folly, since a complete revolution in Javanese civilisation would at once replace those Hindu codes with Mohammedan ones. The same warning applies to Acheh: its *Adat Meukuta Alam*, like the *Hukum Kanun of Malacca*, expresses not a stable body of ancient law but a temporary desire for order and reform, the real living adats being silently taken for granted while the new matter in the books has had a very brief existence in view of the constant changes of government.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Java

**Java** (also **Jawa**) is the great island of the [[East Indian Archipelago]] which, throughout *The Achehnese*, serves [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as the chief point of comparison for the institutions and customs of [[Acheh]]. Unlike the Achehnese — described as a people of more warlike temper, devoted to independence and to a comparatively pure form of Islam — the Javanese are presented as more thoroughly subdued, more deeply marked by Hindu influences, and more systematically organised under a long series of Hindu and Mohammedan kingdoms whose institutions have left traces in their modern law and custom.

The comparison is most fully developed in the discussion of Mohammedan family and inheritance law. Snouck Hurgronje sharply criticises [[L. W. C. van den Berg|Mr. L. W. C. van den Berg]]'s essay on the "Divergences from the Mohammedan law as to family and inheritance in Java and Madura," which employed as data for the ancient institutions of Java regulations appearing in various law-books compiled under Hindu influence. Such a method, Snouck argues, confuses the formal codes of a Hinduised court-civilisation with the actual living law of the Mohammedan Javanese. The Achehnese, on the other hand, had no comparable body of Hindu-derived statute; their *adat* and *hukom* (customary and religious law) stood side by side in a way that the Javanese, long subject to the literary influence of Hindu codes, had ceased to exemplify.

In matters of religion and ceremonial, Java is repeatedly cited as a region where the same Mohammedan feasts take on a different complexion. The [[Maulud (Mo'lot)|birthday of the Prophet]], for instance, is "celebrated in the Javanese courts" with extraordinary brilliance, and its public observance is universal "even in the smallest of Javanese villages." The blessing of consecrated water (*tajul-mumat*) in the mosques on *Rëbo Wëkasan*, the preparation of *bubur* (kanji) porridge on the 10th of [[Ashura|Muharram]], the baptism-like *Mandi Safar* of the last Wednesday of Safar — all these find their parallels in Achehnese practice, but with local variations that Snouck carefully notes. Java supplies the term *sedekah, slametan* or *rijst* for what the Achehnese call *kanduri*; it supplies the chronogram-name *Sura* for the month of Muharram; and it preserves, more clearly than Acheh, the popular superstitions connected with the 12th of Maulud, on which no work of importance should be initiated.

In political and social organisation the contrast is equally instructive. The Sundanese institution of *buyut* or *chadu* — taboos affecting particular families or tribes — is compared with the Achehnese *pantang*-rules of the *Ja Sandang* tribe. The custom of the village priest (*modin, katib* or *lebe*) acting as butcher throughout a great portion of Java contrasts with the Achehnese practice, where the slaughter is performed by the *teungku of the meunasah* in return for a portion of the neck. The Javanese manner of folding the head-cloth, by which the origin of the wearer may be inferred, has its analogy in the lowlands of Acheh. Even the dread stories of the dead walking if their shrouds are not unfastened at burial — crying "ngéh, ngéh, loosen!" in the first forty days after death — find their counterpart in Acheh. These parallelisms serve throughout to throw into relief the points at which Achehnese usage diverges — most often, in Snouck Hurgronje's view, by preserving a more directly Mohammedan and a less Hinduised form of the common Malay-Indonesian inheritance.
