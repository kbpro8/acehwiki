**Krinchi** (modern *Kerinci*) is the name by which Snouck Hurgronje most often refers to the upland Sumatran region of [[Korinchi (Krinchi)|Korinchi]] and to its inhabitants, who form one of the recurring foreign elements in his account of Achehnese society. The name is mentioned twice in *The Achehnese* Vol. I — once in the discussion of the *jakeuet* (treated in the separate article on [[Korinchi (Krinchi)|Korinchi]]), and once, with which the present article is concerned, in the chapter on marriage.

The marriage reference occurs in Snouck's exposition of the rules governing the choice of a spouse. The Achehnese adat, in entire agreement with the rule of Mohammedan law, requires that a woman be married to a man not inferior to herself in social position; the pride of the Achehnese withholds them, as a general rule, from giving their daughters to foreigners, unless the latter are of Arab blood or have been long settled in the country and have attained some position. To other strangers they give only women who have some slave blood in their veins. The passage continues: "An exception to this rule is made in favour of devout hajis from [[Java|Java]], and especially those from Krinchi who in earlier times often made a long stay in Acheh or settled there permanently." The Krinchi settler thus appears as the classical exception to the Achehnese rule against intermarriage with foreigners.

The reasons for this exceptional position are not far to seek. The Krinchi man was, in the older Achehnese imagination, the type of the devout pilgrim-settler — one who had performed the [[Hajj|pilgrimage]] to [[Mecca|Mecca]], had acquired some reputation for religious learning, and was prepared, on returning from the holy cities, to make Acheh his permanent home. The religious prestige attaching to such a man overrode, in the eyes of Achehnese parents, the ordinary objections to a foreign match. The adat that permitted him an Achehnese wife was thus a religious concession, comparable to the exceptional respect accorded, in the same chapter, to Arab sayyids and to other strangers of known religious reputation.

The juxtaposition of Krinchi with [[Java|Java]] in the same sentence is also significant. Snouck treats the two together as the principal sources, in "earlier times," of the devout haji-settlers who were honoured in Acheh with the privilege of intermarriage. The two were, however, distinct communities. Java supplied, in addition to its devout hajis, the numerous Javanese mercenary soldiers, tradesmen, and labourers who formed a recognised element in the population of [[Banda Acheh|Banda-Acheh]] and the other coast-towns; Krinchi, by contrast, supplied principally religious wayfarers, whose reputation rested not on numbers but on individual piety. The wandering Krinchi haji who "in earlier times wandered from place to place in Acheh" and was a notable recipient of the *jakeuet*, as Snouck records elsewhere, was the same figure whom the adat permitted to settle and marry in the country.

The mention of Krinchi in the marriage chapter is the only occurrence of the name in that context; the article on [[Korinchi (Krinchi)|Korinchi]] may be consulted for the parallel reference to the *jakeuet* and for the wider account of the Krinchi country and people.

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Krinchi

**Krinchi** (Achehnese *Krinchi*, more commonly *Korinchi*; modern spelling *Kerinci*) is the name of a mountainous region of west-central [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] whose inhabitants, in earlier times, were noted among the stranger-settlers of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. The name appears in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* in Snouck Hurgronje's account of the stranger-communities among whom the Achehnese gave or refused their daughters in marriage.

### The Krinchi Settlers in Acheh

In the chapter on Achehnese marriage, Snouck observes that the Achehnese have always objected, and still object, to giving their daughters in marriage to the **Klings** (*Kling*, the Achehnese term for the Tamil and Telugu settlers of the Coromandel coast), who are specially known as "strangers" (*ureueng dagang*). "Only such Klings," he continues, "as have..." — and he then turns to the other stranger-communities of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. Among these he names "those from [[Java|Java]], and especially those from **Krinchi** who in earlier times often made a long stay in Acheh or settled there permanently."

The mention is brief, but it places the Krinchi settlers alongside the Javanese as one of the principal stranger-communities of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. The Krinchis — inhabitants of the high valley of Korinchi in west-central [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] — were, in earlier times, numerous enough in [[Acheh|Acheh]] to be recognised as a separate community, and their settlement in the country was sufficiently common to be classed with that of the Javanese.

### The Geography of Krinchi

Krinchi (Kerinci) is a high valley in the Barisan range of west-central [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], lying to the south of the [[Minangkabau|Minangkabau]] country and separated from it by the high divide of the range. The valley is one of the most isolated of the Sumatran highlands; its inhabitants speak a Malay dialect of their own, and their intercourse with the outside world was, in the nineteenth century, conducted principally by way of the rivers which flow down from the valley to the west and to the east. The isolation of the valley, and the difficulty of gaining a livelihood in it, were the principal causes of the emigration of its inhabitants; like the hajjis of the same region, who are mentioned elsewhere in *The Achehnese* as notable recipients of the Achehnese *jakèuët*, the Krinchi settlers of [[Acheh|Acheh]] were one of the channels by which the surplus population of the valley sought a livelihood abroad.

### The Place of Krinchi in the Marriage Adat

The mention of Krinchi in the chapter on marriage is significant for the Achehnese marriage adat. The Achehnese, in Snouck's account, drew a sharp distinction between the communities with whom they would and would not intermarry. The Klings, though allowed to settle in [[Acheh|Acheh]] and to carry on their trade, were regarded as strangers in the fullest sense, and the better class of Achehnese refused to give them their daughters. The Javanese and the Krinchis, by contrast, were stranger-communities of a different kind: their settlement in [[Acheh|Acheh]] was, in the long run, more easily absorbed into the Achehnese population, and the objections to intermarriage with them were correspondingly weaker. The mention of Krinchi alongside Java is thus one of the small facts by which the gradations of Achehnese social exclusiveness are illustrated.

### The Religious Connection

The connection between Krinchi and [[Acheh|Acheh]] was not only demographic but religious. The same region which sent its settlers to [[Acheh|Acheh]] also sent its hajjis; and the wandering hajjis of Krinchi, mentioned elsewhere in *The Achehnese* as notable recipients of the *jakèuët*, were the religious counterparts of the Krinchi settlers. The two classes together — the settler and the hajji — represent the principal forms in which the Krinchi connection with [[Acheh|Acheh]] was maintained; the connection was one of the channels by which the wider Sumatran world was brought into the Achehnese society, and by which the religious and demographic life of the highlands was extended to the coast.
