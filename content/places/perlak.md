**Perlak** — modern Peureulak, on the east coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in northern Sumatra — is named in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as the earliest Muslim polity of the Indonesian archipelago, preceding the better-known neighbouring sultanate of [[Samudra]] on the [[Pasei|Pase-rivier]]. In the chapter *De oudste cultuurstroomen*, Dr. [[F. H. van Naerssen|Van Naerssen]] records that the [[Marco Polo|Venetian traveller Marco Polo]], passing through the Strait of Malacca in [[1292]], found Perlak already Muslim, while the rest of the Sumatran coast he described was still heathen. Van Naerssen's specific phrasing is: "the city of Perlak, where in 1292 one had not yet arrived so far" — i.e. so far in the rest of the archipelago, where Polo's Muslim informants still characterised the surrounding population as "unclean" because they ate all kinds of flesh.

## Earliest Muslim polity of the archipelago

Van Naerssen's account places Perlak at the head of the Islamisation of the Indonesian archipelago. Polo's criterion for distinguishing Muslim from non-Muslim populations in [[1292]] — the avoidance of unclean flesh — is, Van Naerssen notes, "exactly the same as we now still see happen when Islam penetrates into new territory." The same criterion, applied to the rest of the Sumatran coast in 1292, found only Perlak to be Muslim. The neighbouring polity of [[Samudra]] on the Pase-rivier, by contrast, "was still heathen in 1292. But the Muslim founder of the little realm lies buried there in 1297" — his tombstone having been imported ready-made from [[Cambay]] in [[Goedjarat]]. The [[1415]] Chinese reports cited by Van Naerssen confirm that, by the early fifteenth century, "Islam has already spread over the whole north coast of Sumatra and is also established on the east coast as far as the little realm of [[Aru]] with its own Sultan."

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Perlak's significance is foundational: the polity sits at the head of the long Islamic history of the Atjeh region, predating the better-known [[Samudra]] sultanate by at least a decade (1292 vs. 1297) and the [[Aceh War|Dutch-Aceh war]] of the nineteenth century by some six centuries. The Polo reference of [[1292]] is the earliest external documentary evidence for an established Muslim community in the Indonesian archipelago. Perlak's later absorption into the wider Atjeh sultanate — together with [[Samudra]] and [[Aru]] — placed the Atjeh sultanate, at its height, in direct succession to the earliest Muslim polities of the archipelago.

## Account from *Atjeh in de Tweede Kamer* ([[1897]])

Perlak occupies a prominent place in the [[Atjeh debate in the Tweede Kamer (16-17 November 1897)|Atjeh debate]] of [[16 November|16]]–[[17 November]] [[1897]] as the district on which Dutch capital had fixed its eye before Dutch authority had been established there.

### Troelstra's charge

[[Pieter Jelles Troelstra|Troelstra]] told the chamber on [[16 November]] [[1897]] that Dutch capital had already directed its eye towards "eene landstreek, behoorende tot de kuststaten van Atjeh, waar men op dit oogenblik feitelijk nog niets te zeggen heeft. Ik bedoel de landstreek Perlak, welke goede tabaksgronden en tevens petroleumbronnen schijnt te bevatten." — a tract belonging to the coastal states of Atjeh, where at this moment one actually has as yet no say at all. I mean the tract of Perlak, which appears to contain good tobacco grounds and also petroleum springs. Concessions had already been applied for some time before for the working of petroleum wells in Perlak, and this forwardness of capital had even been mocked in some papers, which found it "wat Amerikaansch" that in such a state of affairs capital should already stretch out all its claws towards the riches probably present there.

It was possible, he argued, that with a view to making such a district fruitful for exploitation by Dutch capital, the continuation of the war and the subjection of Atjeh, including the coastal states, was thought desirable; but then let it be said openly, so that the chamber might weigh whether the interests of the capitalists coincided with those of the great majority of the people in the [[Netherlands]] and in the Indies.

### Cremer's answer

The Minister of Colonies [[J. T. Cremer|Cremer]] answered directly: asked whether the war was perhaps waged with the object of being able to use capital in [[Tamiang]] or in Perlak, he said "neen, maar wel het omgekeerde geloof ik, namelijk, dat het kapitaal, gebracht in Tamiang en Perlak enorm veel zal kunnen medewerken tot pacificatie van Atjeh en dat, wanneer de hoofden en de bevolking van die landstreken zien, hoever hunne buren ter Oostkust van Sumatra hun vooruit zijn in welvaart en ontwikkeling, zij zullen snakken naar een dergelijke gelegenheid om zelven ook tot die welvaart te geraken."

### The prince of Perlak's declaration

On [[17 November]] [[1897]] Cremer returned to the chamber expressly to place on record the [[Verklaringen van de kustvorsten van Atjeh|declarations of the coastal princes]], and named Perlak first among them. Of the sixty-two princes on that coast who had made declarations of subordination to Dutch authority, "Onder hen is ook de vorst van Perlak, wiens Rijk gisteren met name is genoemd. Sinds [[1885]]–1886 heeft hij onze souvereiniteit erkend." — Among them is also the prince of Perlak, whose realm was named yesterday. Since 1885-1886 he has recognised our sovereignty.

The declaration bound the ruler to acknowledge that his *landschap* belonged to the territory of [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Netherlands India]], to recognise the Queen represented by the [[Governor-General of Netherlands India|Governor-General]] as lawful sovereign, and to fly no flag but the Dutch, together with seventeen further articles reserving to the Dutch the decision in almost every branch of administration, including the raising and lowering of taxes, the taking over of import and export duties and of tax farms, and the payment to the government of the share of revenue that by ancient usage had accrued to the former Acehnese sovereigns.

### Troelstra's rejoinder

On the same day [[Pieter Jelles Troelstra|Troelstra]] held that the minister had conceded everything: that the concessionaries in Tamiang and Perlak had an interest in the Atjeh war, and that they longed for its end because they needed peace for their industry — but for such an end only as guaranteed the establishment of Dutch power in Atjeh.
