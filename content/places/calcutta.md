**Calcutta** (modern **Kolkata**; the seat of **Fort William**) was the capital of British India and the residence of the Governor-General of [[Bengal|Bengalen]] whose government directed British relations with [[Acheh]] throughout the events narrated in P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819*. From Calcutta, [[Lord Moira]] dispatched the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Acheh commission]] of [[1819]]; to Calcutta, [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] came to lobby the Supreme Government against the Dutch; and at Calcutta the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf ul-Alum]] was given a pension of 500 dollars a month after his piratical cruise.

## Raffles's appeal to the Supreme Government

Raffles, lieutenant-governor of [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]], wrote from Bencoolen to [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Djohor]] that he was ready to go to Calcutta and there promised "to invoke the help of the Bengal government for Djohor." He carried out the threat: "the lieutenant-governor appeared in the British-Indian capital, where he opened the government's eyes to the imaginary danger that the Dutchers were coming to monopolise the China road." At that moment Captain [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] was also in Calcutta, urging — in opposition to Raffles's view — the recognition of Syf as sultan. The Calcutta government under Moira dispatched the commission to Acheh; Lord Moira's letter to London of 14 January [[1819]] hoped "that we may be in time to establish our political influence at Acheh, to the exclusion of that of the Dutch or other foreign nations."

## Source of instructions and complaints

The Calcutta government's instructions to the commissioners were "incomplete," Van der Kemp notes, and lay at the root of the subsequent quarrels between Raffles, Coombs, and [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]]. The Penang government alleged that Raffles had received verbal orders from the Governor-General contradicting the written instructions; Raffles, in a letter dated Calcutta, 31 December [[1819]], formally denied the allegation. The report of the Calcutta government of 7 October [[1820]] to London, repeatedly cited by Van der Kemp, supplies much of the English documentation reproduced in his appendices.

## A refuge for Syf and a way-station for troops

When Syf ul-Alum was driven from [[Telok Semawé]] in early [[1820]] by the man-of-war *Dauffiless*, he seized a junk, plundered twelve praus, and sailed to Calcutta. There the complaints of those he had robbed caught up with him; but "the judges found no grounds to condemn him: Syf had acted as a sovereign!" He departed undisturbed on 21 July 1820, the Bengal government granting him a monthly pension of 500 dollars, charged — vainly, as the Penang officials remarked — to Sultan Djohor's account. Earlier, in 1814, Captain [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh (1814)|Canning]] had proceeded from Calcutta to Acheh to regulate trade; and in mid-1820 the troopship which carried [[Colonel O'Hallaran|Colonel O'Hallaran]] to [[Pedir]] had gone from Calcutta via [[Penang]].

## After the 1824 treaty: a "dead letter"

Once the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 17 March 1824]] was signed, the London Court of Directors sent it to Calcutta with an explanatory dispatch of 4 August [[1824]] (Van der Kemp's Bijlage 9). The Bengal government, by missive of 28 January [[1825]], asked Penang for advice, itself recognising the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|Acheh treaty]] as a [[dead letter]]. Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] composed his [[Fullerton's minute on Acheh (21 March 1825)|minute of 21 March 1825]] for the Bengal government; and the [[Ibbetson's intervention proposal (February 1824)|Ibbetson dispatch]] of 25 February [[1824]] from Penang to the governmental secretary [[George Swinton]] at Calcutta supplied the Supreme Government with the first formal proposal of armed British intervention at Acheh. Falck wrote to Elout on 17 September [[1824]] that Sumatra's impulse could now "better come from nearby [[Batavia]] than from the gentlemen at Calcutta, who were always thinking of other things" — a barb Van der Kemp preserves verbatim.
