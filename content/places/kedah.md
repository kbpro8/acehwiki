**Kedah** is a Malay state on the western side of the [[Malay Peninsula]], facing the [[Straits of Malacca]]. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Kedah is invoked chiefly as a point of comparison for Achehnese institutions, especially for the territorial meaning of the Arabic word *mukim* and for the vocabulary of rice-cultivation.

## The Word Mukim in Kedah and Acheh

Snouck Hurgronje, having observed that the Achehnese have distorted the original Arabic meaning of *mukim* (a person who settles or resides) into a territorial designation for a parish-like subdivision, notes that "this word has in Kedah the same modified meaning as in Acheh." The source he cites is [[Newbold|T. J. Newbold]]'s *British Settlements in the Straits of Malacca* (II: 20). The further observation, that in Kedah each mukim consisted originally of at least 44 families, is "a clear indication of the original intent of this territorial subdivision" — namely, to provide the requisite congregation of forty adult males for the Friday service, with a margin for absentees.

## Sawah and Ladang: The Vocabulary of Rice-Fields

In a translator's footnote to the agricultural chapter, the word *sawah* is glossed as the Malay term for irrigated rice-fields used in the southern parts of the Peninsula — [[Johor]], [[Malacca]] etc. — and also in [[Java]]. "In [[Penang]], [[Province Wellesley]], Kedah etc. the word is *bladang*." The contrast between *sawah* and *ladang* (the latter denoting unirrigated, swidden fields) is a basic dichotomy of Malay rice-cultivation; its survival in Acheh under slightly different terms shows how the agrarian vocabulary of the Peninsula shaded into that of northern Sumatra.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Kedah

**Kedah** (Dutch and older English **Quedah**) is a Malay sultanate occupying the north-western corner of the [[Malay Peninsula]], around the estuary of the Kedah River and the adjacent plain facing the Strait of Malacca. Its sultan was historically the overlord of the adjacent islands of [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]] (Penang) and the mainland fortress of Kuala Kedah, and its proximity to the great trade route through the Strait made it a strategic player in the politics of the region. In P. H. van der Kemp's narrative, Kedah appears at the very origin of the British stake on the peninsula — the 1786 cession of Penang — and again, more obliquely, in the wideranging correspondence that attended the [[Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819|Aceh agreement of 1819]].

Van der Kemp's opening pages place Kedah at the root of the British involvement in northern Sumatra and the Strait. He notes that in 1786 Captain Francis Light, considering a British settlement, "achtte hij Atjeh goede geschikter dan Atjeh voorkwam" — found [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]] more suitable than [[Acheh]] — and so, "bij een verdrag met den radja van Quedah voor eene pachtsom van 10.000 dollars het eiland aan Groot-Britannië werd afgestaan." The cession of Penang by the raja of Kedah in return for an annual payment of ten thousand Spanish dollars was, in Van der Kemp's account, the practical alternative the British chose to a direct establishment at Aceh — an alternative that, in turn, kept Aceh itself within the British field of vision across the next half-century.

Van der Kemp reproduces the original cession document, in which the raja of Quedah informs the Governor-General of [[Bengal]] that "captain Light Deva Rajah came here and informed us that our friend requested Pulo Penang," and that he had thereupon granted the island "to plant the Honble Company's English flag." The cession was signed "on the 20th day of the Moon Shaval, in the year 1199 Regira" — that is, in 1786. The document is reproduced because it was the precedent on which later British conduct in the Strait rested: it showed that the British model for expansion in the region was through treaties with Malay rulers for specific pieces of territory, rather than through conquest.

Kedah's own subsequent history was more troubled. The sultanate was invaded by [[Siam]] in 1821, in punishment for Kedah's failure to send the *bunga mas* tribute; the sultan took refuge in [[Penang]] and sought British assistance against the Siamese, but the British — bound by their own arrangements with Siam — declined to intervene. Van der Kemp's text touches on this broader Malay-Siamese entanglement only obliquely. In one of the appendix letters, the writer observes that "I am not in favour of the present understanding with the Siamese Government as it regards the affairs of Achin but of Kedah and «c" — a fragmentary reference that captures how closely, in the minds of British officials, the politics of Aceh, Kedah and Siam were entwined.

The 1786 Kedah-Penang arrangement thus stands at the head of a chain of events that led, by a circuitous route, to the British mission to Aceh in 1819 and the [[Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819|Aceh agreement]] that Van der Kemp's article analyses. Without Kedah's cession of Penang, the British would have had no forward base from which Raffles and Coombs could have been dispatched to Aceh; and without Aceh, in turn, the treaty of [[1824]] would have looked very different.
