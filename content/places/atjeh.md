**Atjeh** was a coastal sultanate on the northern tip of [[Sumatra]] (present‑day [[Aceh (province)|Aceh]]) that existed from the early [[16th century|16th century]] until its incorporation into the [[Netherlands|Dutch East Indies]] in the early [[20th century|20th century]]. Its strategic position on the [[Strait of Malacca]] made it a focal point of the pepper trade, Islamic scholarship, and a series of protracted conflicts with the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] and later the [[Netherlands]] during the [[Aceh War|Aceh War (1873–1904)]]. The sultanate’s capital was [[Bandjar|Bandjar]] (modern [[Banda Aceh]]), and its territory included the offshore [[Aroe Islands]]. Dutch military operations, especially those of the [[Korps Mariniers (Netherlands)|Dutch Marine Corps]] from [[1873]] through [[1908]], produced a dense record of engagements, honours such as the [[Ridder Militaire Willemsorde]], and a lasting legacy in colonial military history.

## Geography and Political Structure

Atjeh comprised the present‑day province of [[Aceh (province)|Aceh]] and a chain of offshore islands, most notably the [[Aroe Islands]]. The sultanate’s seat of power was the fortified city of [[Bandjar|Bandjar]], later renamed [[Banda Aceh]]. Governance rested on a hereditary line of sultans who claimed descent from the early Islamic missionaries who arrived in the archipelago in the [[13th century|13th century]]. The political organisation combined a central court with semi‑autonomous coastal and interior districts, allowing the sultanate to control the vital maritime corridor of the Strait of Malacca.

## Relations with European Powers

From the early [[17th century|17th century]] the Dutch sought to dominate the pepper trade in Atjeh. The [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] concluded several treaties with the sultans, the most important being the [[1629]] treaty that granted the VOC a monopoly on pepper exports from the region. After the VOC’s dissolution in [[1799]], the Kingdom of the [[Netherlands]] continued to press its claims, leading to a series of diplomatic missions and, eventually, the [[Aceh War|Aceh War (1873–1904)]].

## Dutch Marine Corps Operations (1873–1908)

### Early Expeditions ([[1873]]–[[1876]])

* The **April [[1873]] expedition** was launched to suppress local resistance and secure the coastal approaches.  
* A second expedition in **September [[1873]]** reinforced the earlier gains.  
* A sustained campaign during [[1875]]–[[1876]] saw the construction of forts and the establishment of a permanent garrison network. These actions are recorded in Doren’s history of the marine corps (pp. 91‑99) and are reflected in the award rolls for the [[Ridder Militaire Willemsorde]].

### Krijgverrichtingen in Atjeh ([[1873]]–[[1875]])

* **Krijgverrichtingen in Atjeh (1873–1874)** – a series of engagements led by officers such as [[H. B. Stenfert]].  
* **Krijgverrichtingen in Atjeh (1874–1875)** – continued campaigns with decorations granted to [[J. J. Korndörffer]], [[W. F. Clifford Koek van Breugel]], [[J. L. Cadet]], [[G. P. C. Bijsterveld]], and [[H. Jansma]].

### Operation Simpang Olim ([[1878]])

A punitive expedition against the kampong Simpang Olim in [[1878]] earned honourable mentions for participating marines, illustrating the Dutch policy of targeting isolated settlements that resisted authority.

### Inland Advances ([[1890]]–[[1893]])

* In [[1890]] the marines advanced inland to the town of [[Edi (Atjeh)|Edi]], a strategic centre on the east coast.  
* The following year a punitive expedition was sent to the **Aroe Islands** (today the Aru Archipelago) to quell a rebellion threatening Dutch shipping.  
* In [[1893]] operations against the interior region of [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] were conducted under the command of the Dutch officer **Heuter**, whose name appears in the corps’ records.

### Later Campaigns ([[1901]]–[[1908]])

* **Expedition Samalanga and Peusangan (1901)** secured Batoe Island and surrounding districts, earning the decoration of [[J. A. Gauw]].  
* The **expedition to Boni and Loewoe (1905)** on the island of [[Celebes|Sulawesi]] formed part of the broader [[Pacification of the Dutch East Indies]].  
* The **action against Kloenkoeng (1908)** on the island of [[Bali]] marked one of the final marine operations in the region.

## Cultural and Religious Significance

Atjeh was renowned for its strict adherence to Sunni Islam, which shaped both its legal system and foreign policy. The sultanate’s courts served as centres of Islamic jurisprudence, producing scholars such as [[Nuruddin al‑Alaʿi]]. The resistance to colonial encroachment was framed in religious terms, with the war presented as a jihad defending the Islamic way of life against foreign intrusion.

## Significance

The prolonged resistance of Atjeh became the longest‑running colonial conflict in the Indonesian archipelago, influencing Dutch military doctrine, colonial administration, and the eventual integration of Aceh into the modern Indonesian state. The extensive list of honours awarded to members of the [[Korps Mariniers (Netherlands)]]—including the prestigious [[Ridder Militaire Willemsorde]]—testifies to the intensity of the fighting and the central place of the Aceh War in Dutch military history. Moreover, Atjeh’s role as a hub of Islamic learning and pepper trade left a lasting cultural imprint on the region.

## Account from *DLP-84-Atjeh* — chaplaincy, soldiery and religious landscape

In the late nineteenth century, the colonial capital [[Koeta-Radja]] was surrounded by a ring of forts (*forten* (forts)), many of them garrisoned by Christian soldiers — the [[Ambonese soldiers|Ambonese]] contingents of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]] — and the broader soldiery of the [[Dutch East Indies]] colonial army. A period of relative peace was punctuated by renewed *military action* under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]], producing continuous troop movements and combat through the final phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].

The chaplaincy to these Christian soldiers was exercised by the *soldier-dominee* [[Dominee Thenu|Ds. Thenu]] and the *soldier-pastor* [[Pastoor Verbraak]] — the one Protestant, the other Catholic — who served the dual religious and moral needs of the Christian Ambonese and European soldiery.

The source describes the broader social and cultural life of Aceh: agricultural practices such as sugar-cane pressing and rice cultivation; household objects, toys and weapons — reflecting a society in which indigenous traditions coexisted with European influences. The religious landscape is described as one in which [[Catholicism]] and [[Protestantism]] were present among the Christian soldiers, while the majority of the native population adhered to [[Islam]] — referred to in the source as *Mohammedaan* (Mohammedan) — and retained elements of [[Animism]].

## Account in du Croo’s Generaal Swart (1943)

Throughout *Generaal Swart, Pacificator van Atjeh* the name stands both for the territory and for the problem: "Atjeh was, after all, Atjeh — every setback still caused an involuntary fright." [[Maurice Henri du Croo|Du Croo]] defined the *[[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacificatie]]* as the conversion of Atjeh "from an exceptional, expensive, improper province governed with *[[klewang|sabre]]* and carbine into an ordinary and virtually normal part of the Netherlands Indies." Its administrative form was the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]], divided in [[1908]] into the *afdeelingen* [[Groot Atjeh]], the [[Noordkust van Atjeh|Noordkust]], the [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust]], the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust]] and [[Bambél]]. Swart's judgement in [[1927]] was: "I am convinced of the truth of a remark by an Acehnese chief, that people would be sorry if Netherlands authority withdrew."
