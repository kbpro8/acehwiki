**Sumatra's East Coast** (Dutch *Sumatra's Oostkust*; in the British correspondence "the East Coast of Sumatra" or simply "the Eastcoast") was, in Van der Kemp's usage, the long stretch of littoral states lying along the Strait of Malacca opposite [[Penang]] and [[Malacca]] — the chain of small Malay polities running from the Achehnese dependencies of [[Pedir]] and [[Telok Semawé]] south-eastward through [[Deli]], [[Langkat]], [[Siak]], [[Kampar]], [[Jambi]] and the adjacent districts. Although formally part of [[Sumatra]], this coast was in the early nineteenth century politically distinct from [[Acheh]] itself and constituted, in Van der Kemp's phrase, a constellation of "petty states" with which the Penang and Singapore merchants carried on a direct and growing trade.

## A Penang commercial sphere

The commercial connection between the East Coast and [[Penang]] is one of the running themes of Van der Kemp's article. When the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] fled [[Acheh]] in [[1820]] he sailed along "Sumatra's Oostkust" in the colonial schooner *Wilhelmina* before the crew mutinied; the survivors were interned by Syf at [[Pasangan]] and [[Telok Semawé]]. The Penang government's interest in the coast was frankly mercantile. Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] observed in his minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] that "the success of the chiefs" in establishing their independence "opened all the ports on the coast", and that the more ports open, the greater the competition among sellers and the better the prices for British trade; hence Penang's settled policy was to resist any European — that is, Dutch — interference that "would be of event much to be deplored".

## The Dutch supremacy question

After the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]] of [[17 March]] [[1824]] the question whether Dutch "suprematie over Sumatra" extended to the East Coast became acute. By Article 2 of the treaty, differential duties were permitted in dependencies of either power; by Article 3, they were forbidden in independent native states. The reservation of [[Acheh]]'s independence therefore, as Fullerton at once perceived, blocked the Dutch from passing from Article 3 to Article 2 against the petty states of the coast, since those states were traditionally Achehnese dependencies. The coast accordingly became, throughout the 1820s, the principal theatre of the Anglo-Dutch "paper war" which Van der Kemp anatomises in his sixth and seventh sections.

## The Said Akil affair

The most striking episode was the protracted [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Said Akil affair at Deli]]. The third son of the Penang trader [[Said Hussein]], Said Akil married a daughter of the [[Sultan of Deli]] and, on his father-in-law's death, installed himself in [[1826]] as *Raja Mooda* of Deli, plundering traders and provoking complaints not only from the [[Shahbandar of Deli]] but from the neighbouring traders of [[Batu Bara]] and the [[Sultan of Acheh]]. The episode illustrated both the impotence of the Penang government to control its own subjects and the Achehnese claim that Deli was an Achehnese dependency under the protective clause of the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]].

## The Bellona mission

In [[1827]] the Commissioner-General [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Du Bus]] at [[Batavia]] resolved to send the frigate *Bellona* to the East Coast, both to revive the trade of [[Riouw]] and to undercut the Singapore salt market by delivering Java salt at thirty rupees the coyan instead of seventy. John Prince, late Resident of [[Singapore]], warned Penang from Batavia in November [[1827]]; Fullerton replied with his minute of [[16 March]] [[1828]] and a [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular of 21 May 1828]] addressed to nine of the coastal chiefs, assuring them of free trade at Malacca. He had already, on [[7 February]] [[1828]], written to the [[Sultan of Siak]] from Singapore; the Sultan's reply of [[4 June]] [[1828]] was cordial. Thus the East Coast became, in the years immediately after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 1824]], a kind of test-case for the whole Anglo-Dutch settlement in the Eastern Archipelago.
