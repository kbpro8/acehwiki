**Japan** — the East Asian empire whose maritime commerce in the nineteenth century increasingly intersected with the shipping lanes of the [[Strait of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] and the waters off [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Acheh]] — was, in the source *DLP-84-Atjeh*, a reference point for the colonial administration's calculation of the strategic value of the Atjeh residency, both as a node on the sea-route to the Far East and, in the twentieth century, as the imperial power whose occupation of the Netherlands Indies in [[1942]] would bring the colonial order to an end. Dutch and British vessels bound for Japan often called at Atjeh for provisions and to negotiate safe passage, and the rise of Japanese demand for Southeast Asian spices and raw materials added pressure on the Dutch colonial administration to secure the Atjehnese coast in the years preceding the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]].

## Relevance to Aceh in the Nineteenth Century

The relevance of Japan to Atjeh in the nineteenth century was principally commercial and strategic. The opening of Japan to foreign trade after [[1854]] and the rapid modernisation of the Meiji period created a new market for the raw materials of Southeast Asia — tin, rubber, oil, and the spices that had once passed through the [[Dutch East India Company|Dutch East India Company]]'s network — and the shipping lanes that connected the producing regions to the Japanese market passed through the [[Strait of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] and so within sight of the Atjehnese coast. The Dutch colonial administration, conscious of the growing Japanese presence in the region, sought to secure the Atjehnese coast as a node on the sea-route to the Far East, and the resulting pressure on the Sultanate of [[Sultanate of Acheh|Acheh]] was one of the factors that led to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]]. The British, who had their own strategic interests in the region, were equally concerned to keep the Strait open to their own commerce, and the Anglo-Dutch negotiations of the [[1870s]] over the future of Atjeh were conducted with an eye to the wider strategic context of the Far East.

## The Twentieth Century and the Japanese Occupation

In the twentieth century, the relevance of Japan to Atjeh took on a different and ultimately decisive character. The Japanese occupation of the Netherlands Indies in [[1942]] brought the colonial order to an end and inaugurated a period of profound disruption in the Atjehnese economy and society. The plantations of the east-coast lowlands — rubber, palm oil, sisal — were requisitioned for the Japanese war effort, the [[Atjehspoorweg|Atjeh railway]] was dismantled for its rolling stock and rails, and the Gajo highlands, which the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] had opened to the colonial economy, were once again isolated from the coast. The Japanese occupation also produced, in the Atjehnese memory, a complex legacy of both collaboration and resistance that shaped the subsequent revolution and the integration of Atjeh into the Republic of Indonesia. The long nineteenth-century connection between Japan and Atjeh — a connection of commerce and shipping lanes — thus ended in the violent rupture of the Pacific War, and the colonial order that had been built on the Atjehnese coast for the protection of those shipping lanes was swept away.
