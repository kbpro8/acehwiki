The great Portuguese stronghold and Malay capital on the Malay Peninsula. '[[Sultan Iskandar Muda]]... made several attempts to drive out these rivals of his power from Malacca. For instance, he attacked that port in [[1628]] with a fleet of gigantic proportions.' In the [[Hikayat Malem Dagang]], [[Si Ujut]] is described as a son of the raja of Malacca; the fleet sails to Malacca, 'the king of which place (the father of [[Si Ujut]] and [[Raja Raden]]) has fled with all the inhabitants of the coast to the hills in the interior.' The Portuguese disappearing from the field of vision of the Achehnese while the Dutch came to represent European danger.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Malacca

**Malacca** (modern **Melaka**) is the Malay state on the [[Malay Peninsula]] whose *Hukum Kanun of Malacca* is cited in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as a parallel to the *Adat Meukuta Alam* of [[Acheh]]. Both are codes of sovereigns' decrees, expressive not of decay but of a temporary desire for order and reform. In both, the real living adats are silently taken for granted and not committed to writing, while the new matter which the books contain must have had a very brief existence in view of the constant changes of government. They principally deal with the details of court ceremonial, the exaction of fines and the regulation of the slaves of the court.

The parallel between the two codes is part of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s larger argument that what are sometimes described as the ancient "laws" of a Mohammedan people of the Archipelago are in reality only the occasional and short-lived decrees of a sovereign, superimposed upon an indigenous customary law that was never reduced to writing. The case is of course somewhat different with regard to certain codes dealing with the decrees of sovereigns in regard to a limited number of subjects, such as the *Hukum Kanun* of Malacca and others; these are expressive not of decay, but of a temporary desire for order and reform. Of these the same holds true as of the *Adat Meukuta Alam* in Acheh.

The name of Malacca also occurs in the comparative vocabulary of the Peninsula. *Sawah*, the Malay word for irrigated rice-fields used in the southern parts of the Peninsula (Johor, Malacca etc.) and also in [[Java]], gives way in [[Penang]], Province Wellesley, [[Kedah]] etc. — and in Acheh — to *blang*. *Titi*, a dialectic variation of *titian* ("a bridge"), is the word for "bridge" in the Malay of the northern part of the Peninsula; in Malacca and [[Singapore]] it is *jambatan*. Such regional variations within the Malay world are used by Snouck Hurgronje to mark the linguistic frontier that separates Acheh, with its pronounced northern and western peculiarities, from the more southerly Malay of the Peninsula.

In the historical memory of the Achehnese, Malacca also figures as the object of the great expeditions of the 17th century. With the short-lived period of Achehnese prosperity ended all such glorious expeditions as those undertaken in the 17th century against Malacca and Pahang, but the bold seafarers and pirates of Acheh continued to make themselves dreaded along the coasts of [[Sumatra]] and among the surrounding islands. The monopoly of trade claimed by the great sultans could in the end no longer be enforced, and the central authority dwindled to a mere shadow.
