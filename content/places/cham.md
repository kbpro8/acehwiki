The **Cham** — the people, language and ruined kingdom of **Champa** on the coast of modern Vietnam — are named in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* as one of the two kindred tongues against which the [[Acheh|Achehnese]] language has been compared with important results. The other is [[Bahnar]], a representative of the Bahnaric branch of the Mon-Khmer family.

In the opening chapter of Vol. I, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes that "a comparison of the Achehnese language (which exhibits noteworthy points of difference from the kindred tongues of neighbouring peoples), with those of Cham and Bahnar has at the very outset given important results," but immediately adds the caution that "we must for the present refrain from deciding what may be deduced therefrom as regards the kinship or historical connection between the peoples."

The comparison is significant because the Achehnese are commonly supposed to be, in part at least, descendants of Cham refugees who fled the Vietnamese destruction of their Hinduised kingdom between the eleventh and the fifteenth centuries. Snouck's mention of Cham is hedged about with scholarly reserve — the linguistic comparison is admitted as the basis for "important results," but no positive conclusion as to historical connection is drawn in Vol. I. The restraint is in keeping with the methodological rule repeatedly laid down in the work, that philological and ethnographic speculation should outrun the evidence no further than the evidence itself permits.

The place of Cham in the linguistic and ethnographic opening of Vol. I is one of several frames within which the Achehnese are to be understood. The other frames include the [[Gayo]] highlanders to the south-east, the [[Batak]] sub-groups of [[Batak Karo|Karo]], [[Batak Pakpak|Pakpak]], [[Batak Tuba|Tuba]] and [[Batak Maloyeng|Mandailing]] across the highlands of north [[Sumatra]], the [[Menangkabau]] of the west coast, and the legendary [[Mante]] traditions of an earlier population. The Cham comparison extends this frame beyond Sumatra to mainland Southeast Asia, and so places the Achehnese at one of the cultural cross-roads of the Indonesian and Indochinese worlds.
