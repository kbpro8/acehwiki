**Atjeh**, as [[G. F. W. Borel|Borel]] describes it in *[[Onze vestiging in Atjeh (Borel, 1878)|Onze vestiging in Atjeh, critisch beschreven]]*, was the independent Muslim sultanate of northern Sumatra against which the Netherlands launched two expeditions in [[1873]] and [[1874]], and which the Dutch formally annexed in early [[1874]] after the [[Fall of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|fall of the Kraton]]. Borel calls it "een rijk met een roemrijke geschiedenis dat eeuwen lang onafhankelijk geweest was," and treats it as a serious adversary — not a scattered nest of pirates — whose people he characterises as "woest, dapper, vrijheidlievend, onafhankelijk, fanatiek."

## Geography and political structure

The Atjeh of Borel's account is divided between [[Groot-Atjeh]] — the densely populated heartland around the [[Kraton of Atjeh|Kraton]] at the mouth of the [[Atjeh-rivier]] — and a string of vassal coastal states including Pedir, Samalangan, [[Pasangan]], [[Gedoeng]], Analaboe, Simpang Olim and Edi. The interior was organised into *sagi* (confederations) of *mukim* (parish) units — among them the [[XXII Moekims]], [[XXVI Moekims]], [[VI Moekims]], [[IV Moekims]], [[IX Moekims]], [[III Moekims]] and [[XXV Moekims]] — each with its own chiefs and its own responsibility for defence. Borel stresses that the [[Sultan of Atjeh (d. 1874)|sultan]]'s authority was looser than European readers assumed: real military power lay with the territorial and religious heads, which is why killing or removing a sultan never ended resistance. The death of the sultan in early [[1874]] and the Dutch [[Annexation proclamation of Atjeh (1874)|annexation proclamation]] did not, as [[Jan van Swieten|van Swieten]] had predicted, dissolve the enemy's cohesion.

## Borel's estimate of the enemy

Borel's portrait of the [[Atjehnees volkskarakter (Borel)|Atjehnees volkskarakter]] is admiring despite its stereotypes. The Atjehneezen were "uit eene geheel bijzondere stof gekneed," "een schitterend voorbeeld van wat een volk vermag dat zijne onafhankelijkheid wil bewaren." He credits them with meesterlijke field-works — the [[Mesigit of Atjeh|Mesigit]] at the Kraton was constructed, he says, like the work of trained engineers — and with a *klewang* (curved sword) technique in close combat that no European bayonet could match. The sea-borne import of arms and buskruit through Penang and Singapore kept them supplied long after the Dutch coastal blockade began. Borel's verdict, written in [[1878]] after five years of war, is that the Dutch had underestimated the enemy from the start — a mistake he likens to the Russian underestimate of the Turks in [[1877]].
