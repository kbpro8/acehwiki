**Celebes** (modern **Sulawesi**) is the large K-shaped island of the Indonesian archipelago lying between [[Borneo]] and the Moluccas, east of [[Sumatra]] and the [[Malay Peninsula]]. In P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* the island — more precisely its southern portion, **Zuid-Celebes**, with its principal port of **Makassar** — appears only as a single footnote, but in a context that connects it directly to the wider commercial rivalry which forms the background of the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] and the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 17 March 1824]].

## Makassarese salt and the Java-salt price reduction

Van der Kemp's footnote (at p. 234, in Appendix X) explains a passage of a dispatch from the Singapore authorities complaining of Dutch commercial hostility. The [[Netherlands India|Batavian Government]] had, on the advice of the well-known member of the Council of the Indies Van de Graaff, sharply reduced the export price of government salt from [[Java]] — from 70 to 30 rupees per *coyan* — so that Java salt would no longer be displaced in the **Buitenbezittingen** (the Outer Possessions) by *Siaraeesch, Makassaarsch en Bimaneesch zout*: that is, by salt produced in [[Siak]] (or rather Siak's coastal dependencies), Makassar (Celebes) and [[Bima]] on Sumbawa. Van der Kemp refers the reader to his own *Zoutwerk*, pp. 153–154, for the broader picture.

## The Singapore grievance

The complaint, as the Singapore merchants framed it, was that this price cut was an act of "specific enmity" against [[Singapore]]. Van der Kemp drily observes that the reduction stood "in no other connection with the bitter enmity against Singapore" than that every interested party tries to bring his wares to market or draw trade towards himself; only when British interests happened to be hurt did such competition acquire, in English eyes, the name of hostility. Celebes thus enters Van der Kemp's narrative as one of the producing regions whose cheap salt had for years supplied the [[Sumatra's East Coast|East Coast of Sumatra]] and the rest of the Outer Possessions, frustrating the Dutch government's monopoly hopes.

## Place in the wider story

The salt trade was not a sideshow. In the discussions between [[Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and the Dutch plenipotentiaries during [[1824]], commercial access to the East Coast of Sumatra and the right to ship Siamese salt through [[Singapore]] to Sumatra were presented as vital British interests. Celebes, as the source of *Makassaarsch zout*, was one of the regional producers whose competition the Java monopoly was designed to suppress; its mention by Van der Kemp helps to situate the [[Acheh]] question within the larger field of Netherlands-Indian commercial policy, of which the Acheh agreement of [[1819]] was only a single, if momentous, expression.
