The **Eastern Archipelago** — also called the **East Indian Archipelago**, the **Indian Archipelago**, or the **Malayan Archipelago** — is the general geographical term used by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] for the island world of which [[Acheh|Acheh]] forms the westernmost part, and from whose common Mohammedan civilization the institutions of Acheh are, in his account, ultimately derived. The term is used in *The Achehnese* in close conjunction with the more general term [[East Indian Archipelago|East Indian Archipelago]]; the two are essentially interchangeable.

## Islam introduced from Hindustan

The creed of Islam, Snouck argues, would appear to have made its first advances towards the Eastern Archipelago from the coastlands of the southern part of British India — that is, from the [[Deccan|Deccan]]. The Mohammedans of the Deccan, whose manners are portrayed in the *Qanoon-e-islam* of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]], are Shafi'ites, just like those of the Malayan Archipelago; their national ideas and customs have arisen to a great extent under strong Shi'ite influences. These "adventitious additions to their creed were adopted by the Malay and other inhabitants of the Eastern Archipelago along with the creed itself." The Deccan form of Mohammedanism, Snouck concludes, "exercised an influence on that of the Indonesians superior in force to that of any other agency."

The popular religious literature of the Archipelago bears the marks of this Indian and Persian provenance. The stories which are such favourites in the Eastern Archipelago — of the Titanic wars and numerous love adventures of [[Amir Hamzah|Amir Hamzah]] (the uncle of Mohammad), of the romantic adventures of Mohammad ibnu'l-Hanafiyyah (the son of [[Ali|Ali]]), of the hero Sam'un, Raja Badar and many more — are all in conflict both with the history and the legendary tradition of Islam as fixed in [[Arabia|Arabia]].

## Shi'ite tendencies and the sayyids

Islam as first introduced into the Eastern Archipelago from [[Hindustan|Hindustan]] "has always shown Shi'ite tendencies." This is reflected in the reverence paid to the descendants of the Prophet — the sayyids and sharifs — whose conception as sacred, inviolable and entitled to the reverence of all Believers gradually became part of the orthodox teaching of the Archipelago. Native-born descendants of sayyids quickly assume the character inherited from their mothers; they lose their knowledge of the Arabic tongue, and though they continue, in spite of occasional opposition, to belong to the dreaded and respected religious nobility, they cannot, in the long run, compete with their kinsmen who come over from Arabia. The title of such sayyids in earlier times was *Teungku Sayet* (= Sayyid); the celebrated [[Habib Abdurrahman Zahir|Sayyid Abdurrahman Zahir]] introduced the custom of substituting for these two words the title *Habib* (literally "beloved") used in Arabia as an honorific designation of Sayyids.

## The month of feasts and the day of Ashura

The third month of the Mohammedan year, Rabī‘ al-awwal, is in every Mohammedan country — but especially in the Eastern Archipelago — a month of feasts. According to the now generally accepted tradition, the 12th of this month was the date both of the birth and of the death of the Prophet. The choice of the eighth month (Sha'bān) for the commemoration of the dead — the *kanduri bu* of Acheh — is "a further corroboration of the introduction of Islam into Acheh from the Deccan," since in Arabia the commemoration generally takes place in the seventh month. The Ashura day, of which orthodox Islam takes but little notice, has in [[Java|Java]] given its name *Sura* to the month, and the cooking of special viands on the Ashura day is found throughout the Archipelago: in Hindustan the chosen dish is *khichri*; in Cairo it is *hubub* ("seeds" or "grains"); in Java the *bubur sura* consists of various grains or seeds.
