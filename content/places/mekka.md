The holiest city of Islam, central to Achehnese religious life. The most important teachers returned from Mekka, and it became more and more the spiritual centre of Islam for the Indonesians. [[Cheh Marahaban]] studied at Mekka, where he acted as haji-shaikh (guide and protector of pilgrims). Many teachers from Mekka came to [[Acheh]]: 'Even up to the present time teachers and exponents of mysticism occasionally come, chiefly from Mekka, to make a profit of their learning or their sanctity among religiously disposed chiefs.' ## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Mekka

**Mekka** (more commonly written **Mecca** in English, or **Makkah** in modern Arabic) is the holy city of Islam in Arabia and a recurring point of reference throughout the latter half of *The Achehnese* Vol. I, where the spelling "Mekka" is preferred. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] had already devoted a two-volume work to the city and its people, and he draws repeatedly on that earlier study to interpret the religious life of [[Acheh]] and of the wider [[East Indian Archipelago]].

In the chapters on marriage and domestic institutions, Mekka is named alongside [[Medina]] and [[Cairo]] as one of the centres of Mohammedan civilisation where the legal equality of the children of slave-concubines with those of free wives is actually observed in practice. In Acheh, as in the inland parts of Arabia, this custom is only partially followed: the legal privileges are the same, but the social position is markedly different. The marriage-makers of Mekka are called *mumlik* — an "absolute" identity of function with the district *pangulus* of [[Java]] that Snouck Hurgronje had already discussed in his *Mekka*, Vol. II. Of late, the increasing influence of Mekka has caused the bridegroom to dictate the words of the marriage-formula in the first person; in most localities this has now become customary. A *fatwa* pronounced by the authorities at Mekka on the application of Sayyid 'Uthman is cited against Van den Berg's identification of the *kuwasa kakin* with the function of the *wali*'s agent.

In popular religion, Mekka appears both as a place of study and as a place of dangerous syncretism. The special few in Acheh who, through travels in distant countries, had formed new opinions regarding the proportion existing between the power of Islam and that of the unbelieving world, kept the results of their experience a secret — "just as at Mekka." The *zar* spirits that "possess" the women of Mekka are compared with the Achehnese *burong* spirits that attack women in childbed; the Arabian *Umm al-subyan* or *qarinah* discussed in *Mekka*, Vol. II, is the analogue of the Malay *pontianak*. The *maulids* most in use in Acheh have been handsomely lithographed at [[Cairo]] by Hasan at-Tochi Ahmad and published in a single volume that enjoys the greatest popularity both at Mekka and throughout the whole Indian Archipelago. The annual commemoration of the departed, on the model of the first two anniversaries and the 1000th day observed in [[Java]], has been extended in many places to all anniversaries that pious descendants survive to celebrate, "under the influence of Mekka."

Mekka is also a force in the religious controversies of the calendar. In many districts of the Archipelago the method of *hisab* (calculation) has been adhered to for the beginning of the months, although according to the Shafi'ite school it should only be employed for the indifferent affairs of daily life. "It is only lately under the influence of Mekka and [[Hadramaut]] that the *ruya* has been more universally accepted." The doctrine that the sacrifice of a goat for a child (*hakikah*) in the holy city is an extremely meritorious act has been fostered by the people of Mekka, who thus reap the profits on the sale of the goats and at the same time enjoy their religious prestige. The Achehnese *hajis* who enrol themselves as members of a *tariqah* in Mekka introduce mystic practices and formulas that would otherwise find no place in the country's religious life.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Mekka

**Mekka** — the spelling adopted by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] throughout *The Achehnese* and in his earlier monograph *Mekka* (Vols. I and II, to which the present work repeatedly refers) — is the same holy city of Islam more commonly written **[[Mecca]]**. The form **Mekka** is used especially when the author speaks of the city as a working society of **Mekka folk** — the merchants, pilgrim-brokers, *mumliks* (marriage registrars), and resident sayyids whose influence reached out, through the constant flow of pilgrims from the [[East Indian Archipelago]], to every Muslim community of the Indies, including [[Acheh]]. Snouck's own residence in Mekka in [[1884]]–[[1885]] had given him, as he says in the introduction, an intimate knowledge of "the influence of Mohammedan fanaticism upon the obstinate resistance of the Achehnese to Dutch rule."

As a centre of trade and of religious profiteering, Mekka appears in *The Achehnese* chiefly in connexion with the *haqiqah* sacrifice. The law recommends that on the seventh day after birth a sacrifice of two goats or sheep be offered for a male child and one for a female; the people of Mekka, Snouck notes, "have done their best to foster the doctrine that it is an extremely meritorious act to offer this sacrifice for the child in the holy city. The Mekka folk thus of course reap the profits on the sale of the goats, and at the same time enjoy their share of the meat." Many Achehnese were, however, aware that the *haqiqah* was more properly offered at home. The same commercial relationship linked Mekka to the trade in religious objects. The **Mekka cap** — knitted of cotton, much lower in the crown than the Achehnese *kupiah nieukeutob* — was imported in quantities by returning *hajis*, though Achehnese women were said to produce a finer article at a price of from seven to twelve dollars.

Mekka was also the source of much of the devotional literature current in Acheh. The collection of *maulids* printed at [[Cairo]] by [[Hasan at-Tochi Ahmad]] "enjoys the greatest popularity both at Mekka and throughout the whole Indian Archipelago." The treatise of the pandit [[Pandit Raniri|Raniri]] on the death of [[Sultan Meukuta Alam]] — confirming the date [[1636]] — was printed at Mekka in 131 Heg. on the margin of an edition of a commentary, and was thus accessible to Achehnese scholars. [[Cheh Marahaban]], son-in-law of Teungku Lam Gut and *ulama* to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the sultanate, was "long a resident in Mecca"; [[Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih|Teungku Anjong]] of [[Gampong Jawa]] was himself a sayyid whose family had its members settled in Mekka and at [[Luar Batang]] in [[Batavia]]. Such sayyids supplied a continuous intercourse between the holy city and the Achehnese gampongs.

The influence of Mekka was particularly felt in the gradual reform of religious practice. The **ru'ya** doctrine — the determination of the commencement of the fasting month by actual observation of the new moon — was "lately, under the influence of Mecca and [[Hadramaut]], more universally accepted." The custom of celebrating the anniversaries of the dead "so long as pious children or grandchildren survive to cherish their memory" was introduced into the Indies from Mekka; before its introduction the Achehnese had limited such commemorations to the 100th day. The *mumliks* of Mekka — official marriage-registrars examined and licensed by the *qadhi* — served Snouck Hurgronje as the type with which to compare the district *pangulus* of [[Java]] and the *teungku meunasah* of Acheh; the "absolute identity" of their functions with those of the Javanese officials is dwelt with in his *Mekka*, Vol. II. Even the demonology of the holy city offered parallels to Achehnese belief: the *zars* which were wont to "possess" the women of Mekka are compared by Snouck to the *burongs* that seize upon Achehnese women in childbed, both classes of spirits being made to express their wishes through the mouths of their victims.
