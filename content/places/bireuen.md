**Bireuën** was the hoofdplaats of the onderafdeeling Bireuën of the [[Afdeeling Noordkust van Atjeh|Afdeeling Noordkust van Atjeh]], the principal settlement of the vlakte of the Kr. Peusangan, and the critical junction at which the great Gajo-weg left the coast to climb toward the *Laoet Tawar* and the Gajoland beyond. *De Buitenbezittingen* treats Bireuën as the northern terminus of the road system that opened the Gajo-lands: "De aanleg van deze eenige verbindingsschakel tusschen de vruchtbare hoogvlakten en de Noordkust vond in den beginne veel bestrijders" — the construction of this single connecting link between the fertile high plains and the Noordkust met with much initial opposition.

## The Peusangan plain

The vlakte of Bireuën, "eertijds eene bloeiende landbouwstreek" (formerly a flourishing agricultural region), is watered by the right delta-arm of the Kr. Peusangan — the Kr. Blang Mé or Kr. Tingkeum — whose repeated floods, fed by the sand carried down from the Eastern Barisan hellingen, had by the late nineteenth century turned the eastern half of the plain into "eene troostelooze woestenij." The Dutch responded with the *indijking* of the Kr. Blang Mé and the *afdamming* of the Kr. Pandjoeë, the left delta-arm, protecting the rich western oevervlakte of Bireuën from the same fate. After the works, "is men onmiddellijk met gunstig volg overgegaan tot eene ontginning der verlaten streek." The plain is one of the four "meest belangrijke" of the Noordkust, with the Pidië-vlakte, the Samalanga-vlakte, and the Keureutoë-vlakte of the Pasé-streek.

## Administrative division

The onderafdeeling Bireuën comprised three *oelèëbalangschappen*: [[Gloempang Doewa|Gloempang Doewa]], [[Peusangan|Peusangan]], and [[Samalanga|Samalanga]]. Peusangan, itself formed by conquest from three formerly independent lands — eigentlich Peusangan, Djeumpah and Lheuëboe — was governed directly by its oelèëbalang after the elimination of the two subordinate hoofden and the abolition of the former *oelèëbalang-tjoet* layer of fifteen intermediaries. Samalanga, originally a colony of peperplanters from Pedir and Meureudoe, had been divided by its oelèëbalang into seven *keudjeuroeënschappen*; of these only two survived, the rest being brought under direct rule, and Samalanga was further subdivided into eigentlich Samalanga, Peudada, Plimbang, and the II Moekims Toedóng.

## The Gajo-weg and the Atjeh-tram

The bivak at Bireuën was the starting-point of the *Gajo-weg*, the military trace driven across the gebergte to the bestuursvestiging of [[Takengön|Takengön]] on the Laoet Tawar — "met groote moeite en kosten dwars over het gebergte tot stand gebracht." Originally laid as a *colonne-weg* for the weeks-long transporten of the Gajo-lands bezetting, the road was by [[1917]] "geheel voltooid en in den drogen tijd reeds voor autoverkeer geschikt," opening the Pegasèng-, Ketèl- and DörUbekken to commerce. The Atjeh-tram station at Bireuën — depicted in Plaat 5 of the source — connected the town with [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]] to the north-west and Koeala Simpang to the south-east. The onderafdeeling counted 20,337 registered adult Atjehers at end [[1915]].

## Account from *Atjèh and the War with Japan* (1949) — Bireuën

**Bireuën** is the subdistrict headquarters-town and subdistrict of the [[Noordkust|Noordkust]] of [[Atjèh|Atjèh]], "with its more than 100,000 inhabitants the most populous after [[Sigli|Sigli]] of Atjèh," the subdistrict where the [[Poesa|Poesa]] — the All-Aceh Union of Ulemas — was founded in [[1939]] under the high patronage of Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah of [[Peusangan|Peusangan]], and the strategic junction on the *Gajóweg* between the coastal plain and the [[Takéngón|Takéngón]] highlands. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Bireuën as the political and educational centre of the [[Poesa|Poesa]] movement, and as the point through which the retreating Dutch troops and the civilian evacuation transports from [[Koetaradja|Koetaradja]] passed on their way to [[Takéngón|Takéngón]] in March [[1942]].

## The subdistrict and its self-governing territories

The subdistrict Bireuën "contained only three *landschappen*. Of these three, [[Peusangan|Peusangan]], in which the subdistrict headquarters Bireuën is located, counted almost 50,000 inhabitants, the one Meureudoe bordering [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] had over 40,000 and the smaller coastal Gloempang Doea 11,500 souls." In this subdivision "the equally influential as loyal Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah of Peusangan had left his mark," so that "the 5th column action had little chance." Beside him were "the somewhat sickly Teukoe Tjhi' Moehamad of Samalanga and the elderly, loyal Teukoe Béntara Istia Moeda Peureudan of Gloempang Doea (confirmed in [[1903]])." Economically Bireuën "was in all respects a favorable position. The very extensive *sawah* area, the export of e.g. *pinang* and second crops made a relatively high level of prosperity possible, while moreover numerous irrigation works were in execution."

## The birth of the Poesa

The [[Poesa|Poesa]] or *Persatoean Oelama-Oelama Seleroeh Atjèh* "was founded in [[1939]] in Bireuën under the high patronage of Teukoe Hadji Tjhi' Moehamad Djohan Alam Sjah, self-governing authority of [[Peusangan|Peusangan]]." The movement's first expression was "the *Pergoeroean Normal Islam* (Normal Islam School), established in Bireuën, a secondary religious school that took charge of the training of religious teachers according to the principles of the association. Besides purely religious subjects, history, geography, economics, etc. were also taught at this institution." As main teachers served [[Teungkoe Isma'el Jacoeb|Teungkoe Isma'el Jacoeb]], Teungkoe Hadji Aboe Bakar "an *oelama* from Bireuën," and Teungkoe Moehamad Noer, son-in-law of [[Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh|Teungkoe Moehamad Daoed Beureu-éh]]. The school became the educational nucleus from which the [[Poesa|Poesa]] expanded across the residency.

## The war months and the Japanese occupation

In the war months Bireuën became the funnel of retreat. The third major evacuation transport, which departed from [[Koetaradja|Koetaradja]] in the afternoon of [[11 March]] [[1942]], "stranded at Bireuën in connection with the Japanese landing at [[Koeala Boega'|Koeala Boega']]." The train that on [[12 March]] brought the last soldiers' wives and children and European citizens from Koetaradja was also stranded at Bireuën, since "all transport means were in use for transporting military supplies" and only a small part could proceed to [[Takéngón|Takéngón]]. On the afternoon of [[13 March]] [[1942]] "the last Dutch troops left Bireuën to retreat in the direction of [[Takéngón|Takéngón]]." On [[14 March]] "before dawn, the first Japanese troops marched through Bireuën, after which the definitive occupation followed." After the occupation, the European internees — about a hundred men, women and children — were "first in the prison, later in the girls' continuation school located opposite the prison." Bireuën was "the only place in Atjèh where the rape of European women by the invading Japanese troops … is reported," and in the first week of occupation seven Indonesian soldiers were shot dead by a Japanese officer as "*rampokkers*."

## The Heinzenknecht bust and the Gajoweg

Bireuën is a town in [[Atjeh|Aceh]] at the head of the [[Gajoweg]] — the southern terminus of the military road that runs 103 km south to [[Takengeun]] on Laut Tawar (Lake Tawar) in the [[Gajolanden|Gajolanden]] (Gayo Lands). In *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]), Bireuën is the place where the [[Gajoweg]] begins — at the bronze bust of [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht) on the yard of the controller — and the place from which the lieutenant's journey through Aceh departs.

The Gajoweg begins at the bronze bust of [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht) on the yard of the controller at Bireuën. The bust is the book's — and the Gajoweg's — marker of the cost of the road's construction: [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht), the brave Bavarian who laid the road under [[Gouverneur-Generaal Van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], was basely murdered by a convict labourer during that work. The bronze bust at Bireuën is therefore both the beginning of the Gajoweg and a memorial to its builder.

The bronze bust stands on the yard of the controller at Bireuën — the yard of the Dutch civil administrator at Bireuën. The controller's yard is the book's marker of the colonial administrative presence at Bireuën: the town was a controller's post, the lowest level of the Dutch civil administration in the Outer Territories. The bronze bust of [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht) on the controller's yard is therefore the book's marker of the colonial administration's presence at the head of the Gajoweg.

Bireuën is the departure point of the lieutenant's journey through Aceh — the journey that the lieutenant describes in the chapter *Jagerslatijn*. The journey follows the Gajoweg from Bireuën south: at km 11 the bridge over the Peusangan River, at km 36 the administrative border of the Gayo Lands, at km 46 the halting-place Blang Rakal, and finally at km 103 [[Takengeun]] on Laut Tawar. Bireuën is significant in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* as the town at the head of the Gajoweg and the departure point of the lieutenant's journey through Aceh. The book's argument — that the Gajoweg begins at the bronze bust of [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht) on the controller's yard at Bireuën — is the book's marker of the cost of the colonial army's road-building in [[Atjeh|Aceh]]. Bireuën is therefore the book's frame for the Gajoweg: the town from which the road begins, and the place where the bronze bust of the murdered road-builder stands.
