A port of Arabia. [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel]] studied at Jiddah as part of his long sojourn in Arabia. The tomb of 'Sittana Hawwa (Eve)' at Jiddah is mentioned as a saint's grave of exceptional length, comparable to certain long saints' graves in [[Java]].

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Jeddah

**Jeddah** (modern spelling *Jiddah*) is the port-city of the [[Hejaz|Hejaz]] on the [[Red Sea|Red Sea]], the principal sea-gate of [[Mecca|Mecca]] and the place of exile and retirement of several Achehnese notables mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* Vol. I. The city appears in the work chiefly as the place of death of the sayyid **Habib Abdurrahman Zahir**, the celebrated political and religious leader of [[Acheh|Acheh]] in the early years of the [[Acheh War|Acheh War]].
**Jeddah** (also spelled *Jedda*, *Jidda*) is the port of [[Mecca|Mecca]] on the Red Sea coast of [[Arabia|Arabia]], mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the place of death of the celebrated sayyid [[Sayyid Abdurrahman Zahir|Sayyid Abdurrahman Zahir]], known to the Achehnese as "the Habib."

### The Death of Habib Abdurrahman Zahir

In the chapter on the sayyids, Snouck describes the political career of Habib Abdurrahman Zahir — known in Acheh simply as "the Habib" — and records, in a translator's footnote, that "this ambitious sayyid died at Jeddah in 1896." The Habib, after his remarkable career as the centraliser of power in [[Acheh|Acheh]] and the leader of the jihad against the Dutch, had finally accepted a pension from the colonial government and migrated to [[Arabia|Arabia]]. The choice of Jeddah as his place of retirement is consistent with his character as a sayyid and as a man of the world: Jeddah, as the port of [[Mecca|Mecca]], was the natural refuge of those Achehnese notables who, having left their own country, wished to live in the neighbourhood of the holy cities and among the cosmopolitan society of the Hejazi coast.

### Jeddah as the Gate of Mecca

Jeddah was, in Snouck's account, the principal sea-port of [[Mecca|Mecca]] and the point of arrival for pilgrims from the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]] and from every other Mohammedan country. The same chapter on the sayyids mentions, in passing, that "sundry Achehnese hajis had enrolled themselves as members of a tariqah in [[Mecca|Mecca]]" — and the road by which those hajis travelled to Mecca was the road from Jeddah. The port was thus the natural point of contact between the Achehnese and the wider Mohammedan world; the sayyids who came to [[Acheh|Acheh]] from [[Hadramaut|Hadramaut]] and the Hejaz landed there, and the Achehnese pilgrims who went to [[Mecca|Mecca]] sailed from it.

### The Pilgrimage and the Sayyids

Snouck's broader argument about the religious life of the Achehnese includes a careful account of the influence of [[Mecca|Mecca]] and [[Hadramaut|Hadramaut]] on the Islam of the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]]. "It is only lately under the influence of Mecca and Hadramaut that the *ruya* [the actual sighting of the new moon] has been more universally accepted" in the determination of the Mohammedan calendar. In [[Acheh|Acheh]] itself, the "calculation" was the method followed from the earliest times; but the growing influence of Mecca in the late nineteenth century was beginning to displace the older Achehnese practice. Jeddah, as the gate of Mecca, was the conduit through which this influence passed, and the sayyids who retired to it were the intermediaries by whom the new orthodoxy was communicated to the Archipelago.

### Jeddah in the Cosmopolitan Geography of the Sayyids

The death of Habib Abdurrahman Zahir at Jeddah is one of the small facts by which Snouck's work links the local history of [[Acheh|Acheh]] to the wider cosmopolitan geography of the Mohammedan world. The sayyids of Acheh were, by descent and by education, citizens of that wider world; they travelled between [[Hadramaut|Hadramaut]], the Hejaz, and the Archipelago with a freedom that few other Achehnese enjoyed; and the city of Jeddah — cosmopolitan, commercial, and the indispensable port of pilgrimage — was one of the natural poles of their movement. To die at Jeddah, within reach of [[Mecca|Mecca]] and yet not withdrawn from the society of the port, was a fitting end for a sayyid who had spent his life negotiating between the religious and the political, the local and the cosmopolitan.
