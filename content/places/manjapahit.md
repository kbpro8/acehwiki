**Manjapahit** — more commonly **Majapahit** — is the name of the great Javanese Hindu-Buddhist empire which, at the height of its power in the fourteenth century, dominated much of the [[Indian Archipelago|Eastern Archipelago]]. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje cites Manjapahit not as a political entity but as the locality into which, in a well-known Malay *pantun*, one of the seven Pleiades stars is said to have fallen in olden times.

## The Malay Pantun

The verse, given by the author in the original Malay and in translation, runs:

> *Bintang tujoh tinggal anam,*
> *Jatoh sabiji di Manjapahit.*
> *Ais tuboh sarasa dimanit,*
> *Lagi tambah deng an penyakit.*

"Seven stars, six only now remain. One has fallen into Manjapahit. Athirst is my body as though I had fever, increased still more by (other) sickness."

The "Seven Stars" — known elsewhere in Malay as the *Bintang Ketujoh* or Pleiades — are the constellation which popular superstition in the [[Malay Peninsula]] and in [[Java]] associates with the fertility of the soil and with drought and rain. The falling of one star into Manjapahit is thus a mythological touch linking the disappearance of a Pleiad with the fall of the great Javanese empire itself.

## Mythological Survival

The survival of the name Manjapahit in a Malay *pantun* current in Acheh is itself a small monument to the long-continued prestige of the Javanese empire among the peoples of the archipelago. Even after the empire had disappeared, its name lived on in the popular imagination as a byword for an ancient and vanished power, and the *pantun* quoted by Snouck Hurgronje illustrates how the Achehnese, who never formed part of the Majapahit dominions, nonetheless inherited from the common Malay stock of folklore a memory of that empire.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Manjapahit

**Manjapahit** — more commonly **Majapahit** (Javanese *Madjapahit*) — was the great Hindu-Javanese empire which, at its apogee in the fourteenth century under [[Hayam Wuruk]] (Rajasanagara) and his minister Gajah Mada, held sway over much of what is now Indonesia and the Malay world. Although the empire itself was extinguished in the early sixteenth century, its name lived on for centuries in Malay, Achehnese and Javanese literary memory, and especially in the Malay **pantun** (the four-line quatrain that is the most characteristic verse-form of the archipelago), where Majapahit functions as a stock name of vanished grandeur — paired, in one well-known formula, with the Pleiades (*Bintang Tujuh* / *Ketika*) as a symbol of the remote and unattainable.

## Place in Van der Kemp's article

In P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* the name **Manjapahit** appears only as a cultural-poetic allusion — a passing Malay reference of the kind that an Achehnese court or a Malay-speaking intermediary might use to give a phrase the weight of antiquity. The empire itself is not the subject of any sustained discussion; the reference is incidental to the broader Malay cultural setting in which the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] was negotiated. The name surfaces in the same linguistic and literary register that produced, in Van der Kemp's footnotes and appendices, the Achehnese terms of address, the Arabic titulature of the sultans, and the Malay letters reproduced from the archives of [[Penang]] and [[Calcutta]].

## Historical and literary background

The historical Majapahit was founded in 1293 by Raden Wijaya after the collapse of the [[Singhasari]] kingdom and the failed Mongol expedition against Java. Its capital lay in the Brantas river basin of eastern [[Java]], near modern Trowulan. The Nagarakretagama of 1365 lists among its dependencies places as distant as [[Sumatra]] (Jambi, Palembang), the Malay Peninsula, [[Borneo]], and even portions of the eastern islands. Whether or not that list represents effective political control, the empire's reputation — its name in particular — exercised a powerful influence on later Malay political thought: Malay dynasties for centuries afterwards claimed descent from the "kings of Majapahit" as a legitimating device.

In the pantun tradition the name Majapahit is folded into the *_pembayang* (the first two, image-bearing lines of the quatrain), where it typically serves as the conventionalised, atmospheric counterpart to a piece of folk cosmology. The pairing with the Pleiades is one such formula: the rising of the Pleiades marked agricultural and maritime seasons throughout the archipelago, and the conjunction of an extinct Javanese empire with a celestial timekeeper gave the verse its proverbial gravity.

## Significance for the Acheh narrative

The fleeting presence of the name Majapahit in Van der Kemp's apparatus is a reminder that the negotiations conducted at [[Acheh]] in [[1819]] did not take place in a cultural vacuum. The sultans whose claims [[Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and [[John Coombs|Coombs]] were called upon to adjudicate — [[Sultan Djohor|Djohor]] and [[Sultan Syf|Syf]] — inhabited a world in which Malay literary references, Islamic titulature, and memories of pre-Islamic Javanese greatness all coexisted. The Achehnese court was itself the heir to a long tradition of Malay letters, and its diplomats and letter-writers drew on the same stock of names — including Majapahit — that animated pantun and hikayat throughout the region.
