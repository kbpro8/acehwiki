The **Malay Peninsula** is mentioned throughout *The Achehnese* as the principal comparative region for the institutions of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] repeatedly sets the customs of the Malays of the Peninsula — together with those of [[Java|Java]], the [[Straits Settlements|Straits Settlements]] and the [[East Indian Archipelago|East Indian Archipelago]] — beside those of the Achehnese, in order to bring out both the kinship and the divergence between them. The Peninsula is, in this comparative method, the country of the Malays proper, as distinct from the Achehnese on the one hand and the Javanese on the other.

## The meunasah and its absence

The most instructive contrast between Acheh and the Peninsula is in respect of the *meunasah* — the village chapel which, in Acheh, serves at once as the nightly resting-place of all the grown youths of the gampong, a rest-house for strangers, a house of prayer, and a place of assemblage on special occasions. Among the Malays of the Peninsula, [[A. S. O'Sullivan|the translator]] observes, such nocturnal separation of the men is "practically unknown." The *manasah*, as the Malays call it, is to be found in some Malay kampongs, but by no means in all; it is, however, devoted entirely to religious uses, and is not — like the Achehnese *meunasah* — a sort of "club" or common lodging-house as well as a chapel. The *bale* or public meeting-house is also a common feature of the Malay kampong.

## Words and customs shared with the Peninsula

A number of Achehnese words and customs are noted in *The Achehnese* as having their parallels in the Peninsula. The Tamil word *tandil* — originally meaning a collector of moneys, but among the Malays denoting the headman of a gang of coolies or of the crew of a boat — is in common use throughout the Malay Peninsula and the [[Straits Settlements|Straits Settlements]]; it survives as an hereditary title of certain chiefs in the highland districts of the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast of Acheh]]. The word *mukim* itself — for the minor subdivision of a district, corresponding almost exactly to the English "parish" — has been adopted by the English Government in [[Penang|Penang]] and Province Wellesley for administrative purposes. The Malay word *sawah* for rice-fields is in use in the southern parts of the Peninsula (Johor, Malacca) and in Java; in Penang, Province Wellesley and Kedah, the word is *ladang*. The Malay *babu-kabu* or *k'chabu* (for the tree whose pods contain a substance resembling cotton) is identical with the Achehnese; the mangrove wood known in the Peninsula as *api-api* is the kind most generally employed for fuel in Acheh.

## Butchers, bathing and pregnancy

Many details of domestic and religious life are noted as common to Acheh and the Peninsula. In the Malay Peninsula the butcher is usually the *imam* of the mosque or a *lebei*; as recompense, he is entitled to the hide of the slaughtered animal, just as the Achehnese butcher receives the *keredan* or neck of every animal he kills. Among the Malays of Penang and Province Wellesley, the *Mandi Safar* or "bathing of Safar" is one of the most popular of festivals, and its method of observance is "almost identical" with that of the Achehnese. Very similar superstitions prevail among the Malays of the Peninsula in respect of pregnant women, who must always carry a knife or other iron implement when they walk abroad, and must let no one walk behind them; it is also regarded as *pantang* to break the burning brands in the oven, for to do so would be to endanger the child.

## Calendar and fast

The Mohammedans of the Peninsula follow the *ruya* doctrine for fixing the beginning of the fasting month, whereas the Achehnese follow the method of calculation. The *ruya* is "universally adopted among the Malays of the Straits Settlements"; in this respect the Peninsula is grouped with [[Yogyakarta|Yogyakarta]] and [[Batavia|Batavia]], and contrasted with Acheh. During the fasting month, the same may be said of the Malays of the Peninsula as of the Achehnese: those who have fixed employment work most unwillingly, while those who are beholden to no master do not work at all; in more populous places, especially in the large towns, the rule is relaxed.
