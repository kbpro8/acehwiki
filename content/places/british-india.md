## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — British India

**British India** — the colonial possessions of the British Crown on the Indian subcontinent — appears in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as one of the chief comparative reference points for Achehnese religious life, and as the place of origin of many of the legends, festivals and literatures that had passed eastward into the Malay Archipelago. Snouck Hurgronje often uses the term **Hindustan** or "the Deccan" as loose synonyms for British India when discussing these transmissions.

### Source of Achehnese Religious Customs

Snouck's central claim in his chapter on Achehno-Mohammedan feasts is that "the Deccan form of Mohammedanism exercised an influence on that of the Indonesians superior in force to that of any other agency." He notes that "it is clear beyond all doubt that the Deccan form of Mohammedanism exercised an influence on that of the Indonesians superior in force to that of any other agency. This may at once be gathered from the character of the popular religious literature, even were there no other proof." The popular stories of the Titanic wars of [[Amir Hamzah]] (the uncle of [[Prophet Muhammad|Mohammad]), of the romantic adventures of [[Mohammad Ibnu'l-Hanafiyyah|Mohammad (ibnu'l) Hanafiyyah]], of the hero Sam'un, of [[Raja Badar]] and many more, "all in conflict both with the history and the legendary tradition of the Arabs," come "more particularly in British India," Snouck observes, "nor is it possible that they should have been disseminated to such an extent in any country closer to Arabia."

### The Hasan-Husain Festivals

The Hasan-Husain (or Muharram) festivals — examined in connection with the plains of [[Kerbela]] — Snouck explicitly attributes to "the Shi'ite portions of British India." The festivals, he observes, "particularly common in Hindustan, and which remind one more of a fair or carnival than of a funeral pageant; all this specially belongs to [[Persia]] and the Shi'ite portions of British India, and need not occupy our attention here." In different parts of Netherlands-India, and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra, "the Hasan-Husain festival is celebrated on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the Qanoon-e-islam" of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]], translated by [[G. A. Herklots|G. A. Herklots]]. Snouck notes that "it has been thought that it was introduced along the sea-board by the sipahis (sepoys) who immigrated thither during the English domination," though he judges it more probable that "even previously to this a Hasan-Husain feast enjoyed much popularity both in Sumatra and elsewhere."

### The Saint of Nagore

British India is also the resting-place of the saint **Mirah Sab** (Meurasab), whose tomb at [[Nagore]] was the object of Achehnese votive offerings. Snouck writes that "when a gift of this sort has been vowed to the Prophet, to the saint Mirah Sab or Meurasab (who lies buried at Nagore in British India) or even to [[Teungku Anjong]], whose tomb is in [[Gampong Jawa]], the vow may be fulfilled by handing over the thing promised to a teungku for his own use." The custom illustrates the spiritual traffic that bound Acheh to the Coromandel coast.

### Hindus in the Hasan-Husain Feasts

Snouck adds in a footnote that "according to the Qanoon-e-islam, pp. 122, 142, Hindus in British India also take a considerable part in the Hasan-Husain feasts, pay vows to the holy relics paraded round on these occasions, etc." — a fact that helps to explain the carnival-like character of the festival even where, as in Acheh, it was kept up mainly by Indian settlers.
