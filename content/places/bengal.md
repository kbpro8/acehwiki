**Bengal** — the [[British India|British Indian]] presidency whose capital at [[Calcutta]] was the seat of the Governor-General — appears in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the metropolitan address to which British relations with Acheh were reported, and as the source of a folk comparison embedded in Achehnese demonology.

## The Governor-General of Bengal and Acheh

In [[1786]] [[Warren Hastings]], then Governor-General of Bengal, received from the Sultan of [[Acheh]] a discourteous letter in reply to an expression of goodwill. In that same year, [[Captain Francis Light]] pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal that a settlement at [[Pulau Pinang|Penang]] possessed greater advantages than one on the Achehnese coast. Light's report — "Acheen is a good road but no place of security against an enemy there. The country is fertile beyond description and very populous. The inhabitants rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous. To form a settlement there of safety and advantage, a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary" — was sent from the Straits to the Bengal Presidency, as was [[James Price]]'s letter of the same tenor. The Chairman of the East India Company wrote in turn **to Bengal** on the 4th of August [[1824]] concerning the pretensions of "Johor Allum as king of Acheen." Thus the diplomatic channel for early British relations with Acheh ran through the Governor-General of Bengal.

## The Bengali in Achehnese Folk Imagination

In the chapter on Achehnese demonology, Snouck Hurgronje notes that the *biō*, the "bogey man" with which Achehnese children are frightened, has a terrifying aspect that "is compared with that of the Bengalis with their gigantic turbans." The Bengali — a figure presumably familiar in the markets and ports of nineteenth-century Acheh, where Indian Muslim traders were a common sight — thus furnished the Achehnese with a stock image of the marvellous and the alarming. More dangerous than this last-named class of ghosts, the author adds, are the wraiths of persons who have met their deaths by violence, but the *biō*'s terrifying aspect draws explicitly on the figure of the Bengali.
