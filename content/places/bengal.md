**Bengal** — the [[British India|British Indian]] presidency whose capital at [[Calcutta]] was the seat of the Governor-General — appears in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the metropolitan address to which British relations with Acheh were reported, and as the source of a folk comparison embedded in Achehnese demonology.

## The Governor-General of Bengal and Acheh

In [[1786]] [[Warren Hastings]], then Governor-General of Bengal, received from the Sultan of [[Acheh]] a discourteous letter in reply to an expression of goodwill. In that same year, [[Captain Francis Light]] pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal that a settlement at [[Pulau Pinang|Penang]] possessed greater advantages than one on the Achehnese coast. Light's report — "Acheen is a good road but no place of security against an enemy there. The country is fertile beyond description and very populous. The inhabitants rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous. To form a settlement there of safety and advantage, a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary" — was sent from the Straits to the Bengal Presidency, as was [[James Price]]'s letter of the same tenor. The Chairman of the East India Company wrote in turn **to Bengal** on the 4th of August [[1824]] concerning the pretensions of "Johor Allum as king of Acheen." Thus the diplomatic channel for early British relations with Acheh ran through the Governor-General of Bengal.

## The Bengali in Achehnese Folk Imagination

In the chapter on Achehnese demonology, Snouck Hurgronje notes that the *biō*, the "bogey man" with which Achehnese children are frightened, has a terrifying aspect that "is compared with that of the Bengalis with their gigantic turbans." The Bengali — a figure presumably familiar in the markets and ports of nineteenth-century Acheh, where Indian Muslim traders were a common sight — thus furnished the Achehnese with a stock image of the marvellous and the alarming. More dangerous than this last-named class of ghosts, the author adds, are the wraiths of persons who have met their deaths by violence, but the *biō*'s terrifying aspect draws explicitly on the figure of the Bengali.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Bengal

**Bengal** (Dutch **Bengalen**) was the British Indian presidency whose Governor-General at [[Calcutta]] was responsible, throughout the events narrated in P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819*, for Britain's political relations with [[Acheh]]. The Bengal government dispatched the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Acheh commission]] of [[1819]]; ratified (or, in the event, suffered) the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of Pedir]]; and, after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London of 1824]], pronounced that treaty a [[dead letter]].

## Hastings, Light and the eighteenth-century background

The earliest Acheh–Bengal contact that Van der Kemp records is the rebuff administered to [[Warren Hastings|Warren Hastings]] — "gouverneur-generaal van Bengalen" — by the Sultan of Acheh's letter of 7 November [[1786]]. Hastings had sent muskets "conformable to the model transmitted"; the sultan returned them as worthless and requested "that in future you will send me such as may be useful, or I do not want any." In that same year [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]] reported to the Governor-General of Bengal on the relative merits of [[Acheh]] and [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]], preferring the latter as "more healthy and entire free from that oppression, war and confusion" which had driven every European nation from Acheh. The Raja of [[Kedah|Quedah]] formally ceded Pinang to "the governor-general of Bengalen" through Light, in a letter preserved in Van der Kemp's first appendix. A letter from the Raja of [[Tringano|Trengano]] to Governor-General [[John Macpherson|John Macpherson]] of 24 February [[1787]] is also mentioned, invoking his friendship against the Dutch.

## Moira, Raffles and the Acheh commission

When [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] arrived from [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]] in the British-Indian capital, "the Bengal government" — meaning Lord [[Lord Moira|Moira]] and Council — agreed to dispatch a commission to Acheh to exclude the Dutch. Raffles had promised Sultan [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Djohor]] "the help of the Bengal government"; the government's letter to London of 14 January [[1819]] (cited by Van der Kemp) is the formal record of the decision. The Bengal government gave the commissioners (Raffles and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]) instructions, but "incomplete" ones, in Van der Kemp's judgement; the dispute over whether Raffles had received additional verbal orders from the Governor-General was the subject of his formal denial, dated Calcutta 31 December [[1819]]. Article 9 of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] required ratification by the Governor-General of Bengal within six months, though the treaty was to take effect immediately.

## Recognition of the treaty as a "dead letter"

After the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]] the London Court of Directors wrote to Bengal (4 August [[1824]]) noting that the Acheh treaty had never been anything but a dead letter. The Bengal government, by its missive of 28 January [[1825]] (Van der Kemp's [[Bengal inquiry to Penang (28 January 1825)|Bijlage 9]], in effect), asked Penang for advice, itself recognising the treaty as such: Sultan Djohor had never been able to enforce its provisions, his death in [[1823]] had deepened the divisions in his house, and the [[Three Sagis|three Sagis]] with the [[Queen-Mother of Acheh|Queen-Mother]] were now in command. Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] thereupon composed his [[Fullerton's minute on Acheh (21 March 1825)|minute of 21 March 1825]] for the Bengal government. The [[Ibbetson's intervention proposal (February 1824)|Ibbetson dispatch of 25 February 1824]] was sent from Penang to the governmental secretary at Calcutta.

## Bengal as a Dutch bargaining counter

In the diplomatic exchanges preceding the 1824 treaty, Bengal (in the broader sense of "the Dutch possessions in Bengal") figured as a Dutch asset to be ceded. [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] and [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] had agreed already in [[1814]] on the worthlessness of the Hindostan factories; [[Elout|Commissioner-General Elout]] in February [[1817]] recommended taking [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]] in exchange for them. Van der Kemp records Falck's later view "that, even if one gave up the Netherlands possessions in Bengalen," commerce could still be desired in the same regions where territorial possession was considered a burden. The British-Indian officials of Bengal, for their part, "urged upon our officials there how much wiser it would be to give up the factories for a compensation in the Archipelago," and it was by this route that Bencoolen, with Fort Marlborough, finally passed to the Dutch under article 10 of the 1824 treaty.
