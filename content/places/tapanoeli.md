**Tapanoeli** (modern **Tapanuli**) is a bay and residency on the west coast of [[Sumatra]], included by the Dutch in the cession of [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]] under article 10 of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]]. In P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819*, the **baai Tapanoeli** is, together with the point of [[Anjer]] on [[Java]], the second of the two places for which the Dutch plenipotentiaries of [[1824]] promised the British special rights of access — a concession that [[Elout|Minister Elout]] sharply criticised and that the King ultimately rejected.

## Article 10 of the draft convention

The proposed article promised that "the Bay of Tapanoeli, included in the cession of the foregoing article, shall at all times remain open and accessible to ships under the British flag, and they shall there, during their stay, be treated on the same footing as the ships of His Majesty the King of the Netherlands." The "foregoing article" was that by which the Dutch took over [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]] with [[Fort Marlborough]] in exchange for the Dutch factories in [[Hindostan]] and a cash payment; the Bay of Tapanoeli was ceded to the Dutch along with the Bencoolen residency. By promising the British continued access to the bay on equal terms with Dutch shipping, the plenipotentiaries gave away, in Elout's view, a durable right which they need not have granted.

## Elout's nota of 12 February 1824

Elout devoted a passage of his nota of 12 February [[1824]] to the Bay of Tapanoeli: "The Bay of Tapanoeli is well situated for service. Nobody ought to be excluded. But what interpretation may hereafter be put on the *treatment of British and Dutch ships on the same footing*? (One remarks in passing that the expression as to treatment in the Bay of Tapanoeli ought to give no wider occasion for interpretation than that of Anjer.)" He classed the article with those of Anjer and the [[Karimon Islands|Carimons]] as grants of "special and durable privileges which one fears may be contested from more than one side," and advised that the King accept a cash payment instead. The King followed his advice, and the special clauses were dropped from the definitive treaty.

## A too-ready concession

Van der Kemp prints the article and the critique side by side as part of his indictment of the Dutch negotiators. The plenipotentiaries "who could dispose of Anjer and Tapanoeli for England's benefit had of course no objection to this concession for a still independent port" — meaning, he explains, that they treated the bay as if it were a Dutch asset to be bargained away, when in truth it belonged to the wider Sumatran sphere which article 9 of the same treaty had just placed under exclusive Dutch influence. The Tapanoeli episode thus illustrates, in miniature, the looseness with which Falck and Fagel negotiated the treaty of 1824.
