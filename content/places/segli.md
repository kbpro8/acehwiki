**Segli** — *Sigli* in the modern Indonesian spelling — was a coast-state and port on the north coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], to which the Dutch establishment at [[Pedir|Pedir]] was moved in [[February 1877|February 1877]] after the original post at Pedir proved less favourably situated for the supervision of trade. The post at Segli was attacked by a great force under [[Toekoe di Tiroe|Toekoe di Tiroe]] in [[May 1878|May 1878]], leading to an expedition by the governor [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]] to Gighen and the upper lands of Pedir. Under the [[Establishment of Civil Government in Aceh (6 April 1881)|administrative reorganisation of March 1881]], Segli became one of the three controleurs of the assistant-residency of the [[Noord- en Oostkust|Noord- en Oostkust]], alongside [[Edi|Edi]] and [[Samalanga|Samalanga]]. In [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s [[1893]] tract *De onderwerping van Atjeh*, Segli is also the seat of the *postcommandant te Segli* — the military post-commander charged with the civil administration of the Pedir-Gighen stretch of the coast — and one of the three open call-ports established by the [[Demmeni]] *besluit* of [[13 October]] [[1884]] for the entire *Noord- en Oostkust*.

## The Move from Pedir, February 1877

When in [[1876]] the Dutch set up a fortification at Pedir, they met all kinds of opposition and difficulties from the population, among whom [[Toekoe di Tiroe|Toekoe di Tiroe]] proved particularly hostile. After the arrival of military reinforcements in [[June 1876|June 1876]] the resistance gave way, and by the end of that month the post was in a sufficient state. "It later proved, however, to be less favourably situated for the supervision of trade, as continually great quantities of rice were imported, which seemed to find their way to the enemy." On the north coast it was therefore decided in [[February 1877|February 1877]] to move the establishment from Pedir to Segli, and to that end a column in [[February 1877|February]] marched along the coast from [[Kroëng Raja|Kroëng Rajah]] to Segli.

## The Attack of May 1878

In [[May 1878|May 1878]] the governor [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]] led an expedition to [[Gighen|Gighen]] and the upper lands of Pedir, where [[Toekoe di Tiroe|Toekoe di Tiroe]] remained hostile and the post at Segli had been attacked by a great force. The actual object of the expedition — the kampong [[Garouw|Garouw]], the headquarters of Toekoe di Tiroe — was not reached, but the taking of [[Telok Kadjoe|Telok Kadjoe]], which had been mistaken for Garouw, had the consequence that the radja of Gighen offered his submission and promised the surrender of Toekoe di Tiroe.

## The Administrative Reorganisation, March 1881

Under the [[Establishment of Civil Government in Aceh (6 April 1881)|administrative reorganisation of March 1881]], Segli became one of the three controleurs of the assistant-residency of the [[Noord- en Oostkust|Noord- en Oostkust]] (with [[Telok Semawe|Telok Semawé]] as chief place), alongside [[Edi|Edi]] and [[Samalanga|Samalanga]].

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893)

In Van Heutsz's [[1893]] tract *De onderwerping van Atjeh*, Segli is the north-coast port on the *landschap* of Pedir in the [[Noordkust]] of the [[Atjeh|Atjehnese]] [[Onderhoorigheden]] that served as the seat of the *postcommandant te Segli* — the military post-commander charged with the civil administration of the [[Pedir]]-[[Gighen]] stretch of the coast — and as one of the three open call-ports established by the [[Demmeni]] *besluit* of [[13 October]] [[1884]] for the entire *Noord- en Oostkust*. The port's significance rose to its high-water mark in [[1890]]–[[1891]] when, under [[Van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]]'s progressive closure of the *Noord- en Westkust*, the *postcommandant te Segli* enforced the rigorous *sluiting* on the most strategically weighty stretch of the Atjehnese coast, and a Chinese *toko* inside the Dutch versterking te Segli became Van Heutsz's textbook example of how even a tiny licensed purchase, multiplied by the closure's scarcity, became a powerful fiscal lever.

### The 1884 partial closure: one of three open call-ports

Segli's emergence as a Dutch-administered open port came with the [[Demmeni]] *besluit* of [[13 October]] [[1884]], which prescribed the three call-ports through which all foreign trade with the *Noord- en Oostkust* had to pass. Van Heutsz records the arrangement: *alleen Edi, Segli en Oleh-leh voor de geheele Noord- en Oostkust* (only [[Edi]], Segli, and [[Olehleh]], for the entire Noord- en Oostkust). The three call-ports were the operational core of Demmeni's hybrid scheme — a *partieele blokkade* (partial blockade) combined with a *soort scheepvaartregeling* (a kind of shipping-regulation) — under which all foreign ships had to call first at one of the three before proceeding to any of the not-closed points of the coast. The arrangement survived the withdrawal of the posts at [[Telok Semawe|Telok Semawé]] and [[Samalanga]] — *deze waren, toen de posten Telok Semawé en Samalanga waren ingetrokken, alleen Edi, Segli en Oleh-leh voor de geheele Noord- en Oostkust* (these were, when the posts Telok Semawé and Samalanga had been withdrawn, only Edi, Segli and Oleh-leh for the entire Noord- en Oostkust) — and Segli retained its open-port status into the Van Teijn governorship.

### The postcommandant te Segli: civil and military authority combined

The institutional form by which Segli was administered was the *postcommandant te Segli* — a military post-commander charged, in addition to his military command, with the civil administration of the surrounding coast. The arrangement was the [[Atjeh]]-pattern of combining the *Civiel Gezaghebber* and military command in a single officer. The postcommandant te Segli appears in Van Heutsz's catalogue of Van Teijn's enforcement instruments as the officer entrusted with both the civil administration and the military execution of the closure on the most weighty stretch of the coast — the *gebied van Pedir en Gighen*:

> "zij toegepast werd door een Gouverneur als Generaal van Teijn, die met veel beleid en energie te werk ging en daarbij krachtig en vol toewijding gesteund werd, in de eerste plaats door H. M. marine en verder op het gewichtigste deel der kust het gebied van Pedir en Gighen door den met het civiel bestuur belasten postcommandant te Segli, die evenzeer van het groote nut van eene rigoureuse sluiting overtuigd tot herstel van ons gezag en door onttrekking van middelen aan de Atjehers … met veel energie te werk ging" (it was applied by a Governor like Generaal van Teijn, who proceeded with much discretion and energy and was powerfully and devotedly supported, in the first place by H. M. navy, and further on the most weighty part of the coast — the territory of Pedir and Gighen — by the post-commander at Segli charged with the civil administration, who, equally convinced of the great usefulness of a rigorous closure for the restoration of our authority and the deprivation of means from the Atjehers, proceeded with much energy).

### The 1890–1891 enforcement: Pedir-Gighen under the postcommandant

The high-water mark of Dutch closure-policy on the Pedir-Gighen stretch was reached in the beginning of [[1890]], when Van Teijn's progressive closure of the *Noord- en Westkust* — begun in January [[1888]] and extended in stages — was *in 't begin van 1890 eindelijk was uitgestrekt over de geheele Noord- en Westkust* (in the beginning of 1890 finally extended over the entire Noord- en Westkust). The closure was enforced, on the Pedir-Gighen stretch of the coast, by the *postcommandant te Segli*. Van Heutsz records the institutional arrangement as the empirical proof of the closure's coercive capacity: by the beginning of [[1891]] *die zoo krachtig en stelselmatig toegepaste sluiting reeds in ruim één jaar tijd begin 1891 een effect had teweeggebracht, dat zelfs de verwachting van Generaal van Teijn overtrof* (that closure, so forcefully and systematically applied, had within little more than a year by the beginning of 1891 produced an effect that exceeded even General van Teijn's own expectations). The closure reduced Pedir to starvation: by [[1891]] *de rijke passar te Pedir door de sluiting geheel verarmd en zoo goed als verloopen was* — the rich market of Pedir had been impoverished by the closure and was as good as ruined — and the crushed *Tengkoe Pakeh Pedir* submitted verbally: *Mijnheer, repliceerde Tengkoe Pakeh, Uwe hand drukt zwaar, maar ik onderwerp mij aan Uwen wil* (Sir, replied Tengkoe Pakeh, your hand presses heavily, but I submit to your will).

### The Chineesche toko at Segli: a fiscal proof-of-concept

The Segli *toko* experiment was Van Heutsz's textbook example of how even a tiny *vergunning* (license), multiplied by the closure's scarcity, became a powerful fiscal lever. The experiment is recorded in Van Heutsz's catalogue of the operational instruments of the closure:

> "Bij onze versterking te Segli had een Chinees een, tijdens de sluiting, peperdure toko. Ter aanmoediging van het passarbezoek in Sigli, waar dagelijks maar + 15 man kwamen, werd op autorisatie van den Gouverneur Pompe aan de passargangers toegestaan om, na verkoop hunner waren, in die peperdure toko per man voor een halven gulden, per vrouw voor één gulden te koopen" (At our fortification at Segli a Chinese had, during the closure, a pepper-expensive toko. To encourage the pasar-visit in Sigli, where daily only ± 15 men came, on the authorisation of Governor Pompe the pasar-goers were allowed, after the sale of their wares, to buy in that pepper-expensive toko — per man for half a guilder, per woman for one guilder).

The *Gouverneur Pompe* autorisatie — the authorisation of the eleventh Governor of Atjeh, [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Governor Pompe]] — allowed pasar-goers at Sigli to buy, after the sale of their wares, up to half a guilder (per man) or one guilder (per woman) of merchandise from the Chinese *toko* inside the Dutch *versterking te Segli*. The licensing was tiny — *± 15 man* came daily to the Sigli pasar, and the per-head purchase ceiling was half a guilder — yet the scarcity-multiplied effect was disproportionate: under the closure, even a half-guilder purchase at the *peperdure toko* became a coveted privilege. The experiment was, in Van Heutsz's argument, the empirical proof of the closure's fiscal capacity: a tiny *vergunning* at a single *toko* could be made, by the closure's scarcity, into a powerful instrument of fiscal and political control — the operational proof that the *scheepvaartregeling* could place the Dutch *in den volsten zin des woords op de geldkist van Atjeh* (in the fullest sense of the word on the cash-box of Atjeh).

### The 1893 prescription: Segli as one of four Noordkust ports

In Van Heutsz's own [[1893]] prescription, Segli was retained as one of the open ports of the *scheepvaartregeling*. Scherer's [[20 January]] [[1891]] *lezing* (lecture) before the *Indisch Genootschap* had named Segli as one of four Noordkust ports — *Op de Noordkust: 1 Edi. 2 Telok Semawé 3 Samalanga 4 Segli* (On the Noordkust: 1. Edi, 2. Telok Semawé, 3. Samalanga, 4. Segli) — and Van Teijn had endorsed the reduced list of three: *De Generaal van Teijn achtte op de Oost- en Noordkust: Edi, Telok Semawé en Segli, althans vooreerst, voldoende* (General van Teijn considered Edi, Telok Semawé and Segli sufficient, at least for the present, on the East- and North Coast). Van Heutsz concurred, and the prescription that closed the [[1893]] tract retained Segli as one of the open call-ports of the *scheepvaartregeling* Van Heutsz would himself implement as Governor of Atjeh from [[1898]] onward.
