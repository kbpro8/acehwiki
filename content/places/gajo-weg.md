**The Gajo-weg** (the Gayo road) was the road connecting the [[N.O.-kust van Atjeh|north-east coast of Atjeh]] with the interior [[Gajoland|Gayo]] highlands of central [[Acheh|Atjeh]], mentioned in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s [[Kreemer's De Karbouw (1956)|*De Karbouw* ([[1956]])]] (p. 102) as the route along which, in [[1917]], Kreemer personally observed buffalo-cart (*buffelkar*) drivers using a kind of wooden brake-shoe (*remschoen*) on the steep descents — an observation that overturned the then-current Dutch ethnographic assumption that such braking technology was unknown in the archipelago. The Gajo-weg is the only piece of Atjeh infrastructure explicitly named in *De Karbouw*'s chapter on the buffalo as transport animal, and the [[1917]] observation is one of the few personally dated field observations that Kreemer records in the book.

## The [[1917]] brake-shoe observation
The Gajo-weg passage in *De Karbouw* (p. 102) reads:

> *"Dat zij elders wèl voorkomen, bleek ons bij het passeren van de Gajo-weg (verbinding van de N.O.-kust van Atjeh met het binnenland) in [[1917]], toen wij de karrevoerders op de steile hellingen wel degelijk van een soort houten remschoenen zagen gebruik maken."*
The observation is significant because it corrected a widespread Dutch ethnographic generalisation, derived from earlier writers, that the use of brake-shoes on buffalo-carts was unknown in the archipelago. Kreemer's personal observation on the Gajo-weg in [[1917]] showed that, on the contrary, the drivers of buffelkars descending the steep gradients between the Atjeh coast and the Gayo highlands did make use of wooden brake-shoes — a small but valuable correction to the comparative ethnographic literature on transport technology.

## The road's historical context
The Gajo-weg, as a road connecting the Atjeh coast with the Gayo interior, belongs to the infrastructure of the late-colonial period — that is, to the period after the Dutch military conquest of the [[Gajoland|Gayo]] country in the early years of the twentieth century (the Gayo lands were brought under Dutch control in [[1904]], during the closing phases of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]]). The road thus post-dates the military pacification of the interior and represents the civilian-administrative integration of the Atjeh coastal and highland zones. Kreemer's traverse of the road in [[1917]] — a date within the late-colonial peace — fits this chronological frame.


## Significance
The Gajo-weg is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history as a documented piece of Atjeh infrastructure, as the type-locality of Kreemer's [[1917]] observation on buffalo-cart brake-shoes, and as a material symbol of the late-colonial integration of the Atjeh coastal and Gayo highland zones that followed the Dutch military conquest of the interior. The [[1917]] observation also serves as a fixed chronological point in Kreemer's own field-experience in northern Sumatra, alongside his earlier "verblijf in Atjeh" (stay in Atjeh) and his visit to the [[Gajoland|Gajoland]] recorded elsewhere in *De Karbouw*.
