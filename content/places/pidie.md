The major North Coast territory and kingdom. 'The Achehnese give the name of Pidie to the whole of the territory which formerly belonged to the kingdom of that name, i.e. almost the whole of the North Coast with its hinterland.' Pidie is historically known as a seat of religious learning: 'Thus most of the learned in Great [[Acheh]] have spent the greater part of their student life in Pidie, while vice versa the studiously inclined in Pidie and on the East Coast amass their capital of knowledge in Great [[Acheh]].' The centres of learning in Pidie before the Dutch included [[Langga and Langgo]], [[Langga and Langgo]], [[Sriweue]], [[Simpang]], and [[Ie Leubeue]] (= Ayer Labu). [[Tiro]] in Pidie was 'sanctified by the presence of so many living ulamas and the holy tombs of their predecessors.' Heretical mysticism (eleumee sale) also flourished in Pidie. In the [[Hikayat Malem Dagang]], 'The first place touched at is Pidie (vulg. Pedir), the panglima of which is handed down to fame as the bravest and most distinguished of generals.' The [[Hikayat Pochut Muhamat]] describes [[Pochut Muhamat]]'s tour through Pidie gathering allies: he visited [[Padang Teuji]], [[Reubee]], and eventually won over [[Pangulee Beunaroe]], the powerful uleebalang of the IX Mukims. The sadati kisah describes Pidie as forming a square with four uleebalangs holding power: 'The X Mukims are subject to Bentara Keumangan; Teungku Sama Indra rules the VIII Mukims; The La'seumana is a fatherless child; he rules the XXII Mukims; The V Mukims are under the control of Teungku Ujong

**Pidie** (also *Pedir*, or *Pidié* in Acehnese orthography) was a significant coastal territory immediately to the east of [[Great Acheh]], lying beyond the boundary formed by the footpaths through [[Reueng-reueng]], [[Pancha]], and [[Janthoe]] and continuing to [[Teumieng]] on the East Coast.

Pidie was one of the outer dependencies of the [[Aceh Sultanate]], bound to the sultanate in a more distant relationship than the three sagis of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]] itself. The territory fell within the broader limits of the kingdom — the Acehnese placing those limits at [[Teumieng]] (Tamiang) on the East Coast — but was distinct from "Acheh proper."

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes that the Pidië dialect was employed in certain senses within the Acehnese language, for example using the word *penghulu* (Malay equivalent of *imeum*) as chief or lord — a title otherwise confined in Acheh to the prophet and his disciples.

Some members of Sayyid families from [[Mecca]] were settled in Pidie and made occasional journeys to [[Keumala]], where the young sultan ([[Muhammad Dawot Shah]]) treated them with the highest honour.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pidie

**Pidie**, vulgarly known to Europeans as **Pedir**, is the region of [[Acheh]] lying on the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]] immediately to the south of Great-Acheh. It is one of the oldest and most politically important of the Achehnese dependencies, and throughout *The Achehnese* Vol. I [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] treats it as a region second only to the heartland in significance. Its people, speech and customs differ in many particulars from those of [[Great Acheh]], and the *kawom* (tribal) organisation survived here with greater force than in the lowlands near the [[Dalam (Sultan's Court)|Dalam]].

The *kawoms* have maintained most force in those parts of the country where the political development is most backward: in the Tunong (the [[XXII Mukims]]), in Pidie, and in the [[VII Mukims Bueng]] (the part of the [[XXVI Mukims]] that has most in common with the highlands in language and manners). In the lowlands, on the other hand, and especially in the neighbourhood of the Dalam, blood-feuds (*bila*) and political dissension have tended to weaken tribal ties. The title *teungku* is applied in Pidie and on the East Coast not only to religious officials and learned men — as it is in Great-Acheh — but also to the holders of worldly offices and worldly distinctions; in Great-Acheh such persons are distinguished by the title *teuku*. Pidie also has its federations of mukims on the model of the Three Sagis: just as Acheh has its XXII Mukims of Panglima Polem, so Pidie has its XXII Mukims of [[Bentara Keumangan]], and so forth.

The district is closely associated with [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]] ([[1607]]–[[1636]]). In Pidie, just as in Great-Acheh, there were a number of mosques which enjoyed the reputation of having been erected by that Sultan and which were thus also called *meuseugit raya* ("great mosques"). The famous story of the humble man of [[Lam Panaih|Lampanaih]] who gave milk to a thirsty Sultan on the Pidie campaign, and was honoured by his descendants becoming the [[Ja Sandang|Teuku Kali]] (court judges), shows how closely Pidie was bound up with the royal mythology. Snouck Hurgronje also notes that, while the Teungku (religious teacher) holds his rank by personal qualities in Great-Acheh, in Pidie it is possible to be born into the title.

In modern times Pidie acquired fresh political importance as the seat of the fugitive Sultan's court. After the loss of the Dalam and the death of [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]], the pretender [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] established himself at [[Keumala]] in Pidie territory; thither uleebalangs who had tendered their submission to the Dutch Government made pilgrimage in order to obtain sealed deeds of recognition, and thither too the influential Imeum of [[Chadé|Chade']] went to support the defeated candidate in the last pre-war royal conflict. Pidie was also the theatre of the murder of Teuku Lamreueng, who had re-established himself in his territory and tendered his submission to the Dutch Government and was killed there by the adherents of Teuku Lampaseh as he went to fetch his family. In agriculture, the planting-out (*pula*) of rice in Pidie is left to the women, who work for a small daily wage — a custom shared with [[Daya]] and some parts of the highlands.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pidie

**Pidie** (vulgarly known to Europeans as **Pedir**) was, in the political system of [[Acheh]] described by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]], one of the most important **dependencies** of the sultanate, lying between [[Great Acheh]] and the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]] of Sumatra. Though outside the territory of the [[Three Sagis (XXVI, XXV, XXII Mukims)|three sagis]] that formed the kernel of the kingdom, Pidie was closely linked to it by religion, by race, and by a long history of political dependence on the sultans of Acheh. Its reputation in the country was enhanced by the number of distinguished *ulamas* it produced — most famously the celebrated [[Teungku Tiro (Muhamat Amin)|Teungku Tiro]], whose gampong of [[Tiro]] in Pidie enjoyed a privileged position as a place of sanctuary and religious teaching.

Like Great Acheh, Pidie possessed confederations of mukims analogous to the sagis. As Snouck Hurgronje notes, "just as Acheh has its XXII Mukims of [[Panglima Polem]], so we find in Pidie the XXII Mukims of [[Bentara Keumangan]] and so on." Several of the great mosques (*meuseugit raya*) of the dependency were reputed to have been erected by [[Sultan Meukuta Alam]] (1607–1636), the great royal builder, alongside those of [[Indrapuri]], [[Indrapeurua]] and [[Indrapatra]] in Great Acheh itself. Some of these remained objects of veneration down to the time of the Dutch war, although their original supervision over the surrounding mukims had long since become a dead letter. As in [[Daya]] and other dependencies, there survived in Pidie certain relics of the *wakeueh*-districts — territories whose revenues had once been reserved for the sultans but whose chiefs had long been free of the court's control.

The political usages of Pidie differed in several particulars from those of Great Acheh. In Great Acheh the holders of worldly offices and distinctions — the *keuchi'*, the *panglima*, the *imeum* and the *uleebalang* — enjoyed the title **teuku**, while **teungku** was reserved for those connected with religion. In **Pidie and on the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]]** the holders of worldly offices or worldly distinctions also enjoyed the title *teungku*, a divergence which points to an older usage preserved in the dependencies after the capital had introduced a sharper distinction. Pidie was also subject, at least in districts where ancient rulers had constructed irrigation canals, to a rice tax called *jasa pade*, levied for the Sultan — an amount of padi equal to that used as seed on the area from which it was collected. This tax was no longer collected in Great Acheh proper.

In religious custom, too, Pidie followed its own local usages. In the matter of the [[Maulud (Mo'lot)|Kanduri Mo'lot]] the guests, contrary to the practice of the capital, took to their homes the remnants of the feast, which they called *ayapan*. The determination of the commencement of the [[Puasa|fasting month]] also gave rise, in Pidie, to longstanding quarrels between the various gampongs and to actual discrepancies in their calendars — disputes which the three sagis escaped by following the usage of the capital. In matters of padi-cultivation the people of Pidie and Daya also differed from the lowlanders of Great Acheh, performing certain rites that were elsewhere conducted only by men.

Pidie acquired fresh political importance during the [[Achehnese War]]. When the [[Dalam (Sultan's Court)|Dalam]] was lost and [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]] died, the fugitive court of the pretender [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] found refuge in Pidie territory — at [[Keumala]] — "far removed from the theatre of the conflict," where the sultanate continued "very much the same as it had been before the war, the scene alone being changed." It was in Pidie, too, that [[Teuku Chut Lamreueng]] of the [[XXVI Mukims]] was murdered by the adherents of [[Teuku Muda Lampaseh|Teuku Lampaseh]] after he had gone there to fetch his family. Pidie was also the seat of a semi-independent branch of the royal family, settled at [[Kuala Batee]] and given to piracy, to which belonged the [[Tuanku Usen]] and [[Tuanku Abdomajet]] who eventually submitted to the Dutch Government.
