In the [[Hikayat Nubuet]]: 'Abu Jhay (Abu Jahl) is represented as king of Mecca' and Muhammad 'in his tender youth... with the help of forty companions, waged a long war against Abu Jhay who is represented as king of Mecca and who deemed himself slighted by the young lad.'

**Mecca** (also *Mekka*, Arabic *Makkah*) was the holiest city of Islam and a site of major importance to [[Great Acheh|Acehnese]] society and religion. The performance of the *haj* (pilgrimage to Mecca) was one of the Five Pillars of Islam, and those who had completed it bore the title *haji* — a mark of great religious distinction in Acehnese society.

## Role in Acehnese Islam

The title *haji* was one of several applied to the [[Teungku|teungku]] class of religious leaders in [[Great Acheh|Acheh]], alongside *leubë* (one who observes his religious obligations faithfully), *malém* (one with some knowledge of the holy books), *além* (one who has brought his studies to perfection), *ulama* (an authority on religious law and doctrine), and *sayyid* (a descendant of Mohammed). The pilgrimage to Mecca was thus the most outward mark of Islamic devotion.

## Snouck Hurgronje's Time in Mecca

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] spent time in Mecca during [[1884]]–[[1885]], where he was in a position — especially at the holy city — to obtain intimate knowledge of the influence of Mohammedan fanaticism upon the obstinate resistance of the [[Acehnese People|Acehnese]] to Dutch rule. This residence gave him the foundational insight for his study of Acehnese religion and politics.

Some members of Sayyid families from Mecca settled in [[Pidie]] and made occasional journeys to [[Keumala]], where the young sultan [[Muhammad Dawot Shah]] treated them with the highest honour.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Mecca

**Mecca** (also written **Mekka** or **Makkah**) is the holy city of Islam in Arabia, the direction of which is indicated by the *mihrab* in every mosque and the destination of the *hajj* pilgrimage. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I Mecca is one of the most frequently cited places outside the East Indian Archipelago, both as the spiritual centre towards which Achehnese life is oriented and as the active source of the reforming influence that has progressively modified the indigenous adats of [[Acheh]].

The *kiblat* — the direction of Mecca, to which attention has to be paid in the performance of prayers — is repeatedly noticed by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]. The *mahrab* or *merab*, the masonry niche of the Achehnese prayer-house (*deah*), is built solely to indicate it. The Achehnese believe that on the *qadar* night the very trees bow to the ground in awe in the direction of the *kiblat*, that is to say toward the Sacred Mosque at Mecca; the young folk make expeditions on the night of the 27th "to seek trees doing obeisance." A line joining the three stars of Orion's belt is also believed exactly to indicate the *kiblat*. The valley of Muna (the ancient Mina), which lies to the east of Mecca, is the scene of the great sacrificial feast connected with the Hajj on the 10th day of the month Dul-hidjah.

Mecca is the great centre through which the more strict and orthodox doctrine of Islam is brought to bear on the Archipelago. The *tariqahs* so popular in other parts of [[Sumatra]] do not flourish in Acheh, though sundry Achehnese *hajis* have enrolled themselves as members of a *tariqah* in Mecca; the *zar* spirits that "possess" the women of Mecca are compared with the Achehnese *burong* spirits that attack women in childbed. The doctrine that the sacrifice of a goat for a child (*hakikah*) is an extremely meritorious act to be offered in the holy city has been fostered by the people of Mecca, who of course reap the profits on the sale of the goats. So too the influence of Mecca has caused the celebration of the anniversaries of the departed so long as pious children or grandchildren survive to cherish their memory. The growing practice of dictating the words of the marriage-formula in the first person is said to have lately become customary in most localities "owing to the increasing influence of Mekka in such matters." Achehnese who have lived in Mecca enjoy enhanced religious prestige: [[Cheh Marahaban]], long a resident in Mecca, became *ulama* and *kali* to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the Sultanate; and some members of a sayyid family from Mecca settled in [[Pidie]] and made occasional journeys to [[Keumala]], where the young sultan treated them with the highest honour.

The marriage-officials of Mecca are called *mumlik* (a term whose "absolute" identity of function with the district *pangulus* of [[Java]] Snouck Hurgronje had already discussed in his *Mekka*, Vol. II). Together with [[Medina]] and [[Cairo]], Mecca is cited as a centre of Mohammedan civilisation where the legal equality of the children of slave-concubines with those of free wives is actually observed in practice — a custom only partially observed in Acheh and in inland Arabia. In the matter of the calendar, the doctrine of the *ruya* (observation of the new moon) has more recently been accepted throughout the East Indies under the combined influence of Mecca and [[Hadramaut]], displacing the older method of *hisab* (calculation). Here, as in so much else, the holy city sets the standard to which the outlying Muslim lands tend, sometimes slowly and sometimes with great rapidity, to conform.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Mecca

**Mecca** (also written **Mekka**, Arabic **Makkah**) is the holy city of Islam in [[Arabia]], and the destination of the pilgrimage (*hajj*) which every pious Achehnese was expected, if able, to accomplish at least once in his lifetime. Throughout *The Achehnese* it appears both as the supreme spiritual centre towards which Achehnese religious life was oriented — the *kiblat* or direction of prayer, the goal of the Hajj, and the source of much of the doctrinal and devotional literature in use in [[Acheh]] — and as the place where [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] himself, during a residence in [[Arabia]] in [[1884]]–[[1885]], had gained that intimate knowledge of Mohammedan fanaticism which he afterwards applied to the analysis of Achehnese resistance to Dutch rule.

The most ordinary outward token of the connexion of Acheh with Mecca was the **Mekka cap**, the knitted head-covering brought back by returning *hajjis* and sold in the markets of Acheh. The Achehnese *kupiah* — a cylindrical cap of close-pressed tree-cotton, ornamented with silk or cotton stuffs of various colours and crowned with a knot of gold or silver thread — "greatly resembles the Mekka cap in colour," but is higher in the crown; the local craftsmen of Acheh, Snouck notes, "make a finer quality than those of Mekka." Even the *meunasah* — the village chapel and club-house of the Achehnese gampong — contained a *mihrab* or niche indicating the direction of Mecca, towards which all prayers were addressed; and during the [[Puasa|fasting month]] the very trees, it was believed, bowed to the ground in awe in the direction of the Sacred Mosque at Mekka.

The Sultan's edicts of appointment to Achehnese chiefs expressly enjoin that "where any is able, [he should] accomplish the journey to Mekka to undertake the *haj*." Returning pilgrims bore the honorific title of **haji** and enjoyed, with the *ulamas* and *alems*, the respect of the entire community. Some Achehnese *hajis* went further and, while at Mecca, enrolled themselves as members of a *tariqah* or mystic order; the *tariqahs* so popular in other parts of [[Sumatra]] did not, however, flourish in Acheh itself. Mecca was also the seat of learning sought out by ambitious scholars. The pandit [[Pandit Raniri|Raniri]], the great 17th-century theologian of Acheh, had his treatise on the death of [[Sultan Meukuta Alam]] printed at Mekka in 131 Heg.; and [[Cheh Marahaban]], son-in-law of Teungku Lam Gut and *ulama* to the sovereign of the port during the last years of the sultanate, was "long a resident in Mecca." [[Sayyid Abu Bakar bin Husain Bil-Faqih|Teungku Anjong]] of [[Gampong Jawa]] — the most venerated tomb in Acheh — was a sayyid from the Hadramaut family of the same name that had its members settled in Mecca, and pilgrims from the East Indies maintained in the holy city a constant intercourse with their homeland.

Mecca exerted a continuous influence on the development of Mohammedan practice in the Indian Archipelago. The recent spread of the **ru'ya** doctrine — the determination of the commencement of the fasting month by actual observation of the new moon, instead of by astronomical calculation — was, Snouck Hurgronje notes, due to the influence of Mecca and [[Hadramaut]]. The *mumliks* of Mecca — the official marriage-registrars of the holy city — supplied the model which Snouck invoked to explain the function of the *pangulus* and *naibs* of Java and the *teungku meunasah* of Acheh. The muftis of Mecca pronounced, in answer to an application by [[Sayyid 'Uthman]] of [[Batavia]], the *fatwa* which proved the existence of the *ta'liq of Batavia* against the denial of [[L. W. C. van den Berg|Van den Berg]]. The people of Mecca, in particular, "have done their best to foster the doctrine that it is an extremely meritorious act to offer the *haqiqah* sacrifice for the child in the holy city" — reaping the profit on the sale of goats and the share of the meat.
