**Agam** was the landstreek (district) in the Padangsche Bovenlanden — the uplands behind [[Padang|Padang]] — around the site of the later [[Fort de Kock|Fort de Kock]] (modern Bukittinggi), which was brought into the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Compagnie]]'s Westkust treaty system by the [[1682]] expedition of commandeur [[Jan van Leene|Jan van Leene]]. In Dr. F. W. Stapel's *Geschiedkundige Atlas van Nederland* Agam is the northernmost of the Padangsche Bovenlanden districts and the one whose submission in [[1682]] opened the Compagnie's long involvement with the interior of west Sumatra.

## The 1682 submission

Agam appears in Stapel's text only in the closing phase of the [[1682]] expedition. After the defeat of [[Bajang]] and the submission of [[Priaman]] (on [[17 March]] [[1682]]), "those of Agam, the land-strip around the present Fort de Kock, which had undertaken an incursion into the Priamansche, were defeated, and likewise concluded a bond with the Compagnie" (*Die van Agam, de landstreek om het tegenwoordige Fort de Kock, welke een inval in het Priamansche hadden ondernomen, werden verslagen, en sloten eveneens een verbond met de Compagnie*).

The Agam submission is the first formal Compagnie treaty with a Bovenlanden district. The Padangsche Bovenlanden — the highlands behind Padang — had been the nominal suzerain territory of the king of [[Menangkabau]] throughout the [[Painansch Contract]] era, but the [[1682]] Agam treaty is the first direct Compagnie political relationship with a Bovenlanden polity.

## Significance

Agam is the first formal Compagnie treaty with the future heartland of the Padangsche Bovenlanden. The country around present-day Bukittinggi would become the political and military heart of the Padangsche Bovenlanden in the nineteenth century, and the [[1682]] treaty is its first formal contact with the Compagnie. The district would be the centre of the [[Padri War]] in the early nineteenth century, and the site of the principal Dutch military stations in west Sumatra through the entire colonial period.

The [[1682]] Agam submission is also the operational proof that the Compagnie's Westkust authority, however complete on the coastal Painansch Contract system, could not be extended to the Bovenlanden without military force. The Agamers' "incursion into the Priamansche" — the occasion for Van Leene's response — is the operational proof that the coastal Painansch system was porous to the inland powers, and that the Compagnie's Westkust authority required Bovenlanden co-operation to be effective.
