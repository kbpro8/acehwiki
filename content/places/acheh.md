Acheh (modern Aceh) is the region at the northern tip of Sumatra that was home to the Sultanate of Acheh, one of the most powerful Islamic port-kingdoms in Southeast Asia from the 16th to the 19th century. It became a major stopping point for Muslim pilgrims traveling between the Indonesian archipelago and Mecca, and a center of Islamic scholarship, mysticism, and literature in the Malay world. Acheh fought a prolonged war of resistance against Dutch colonial conquest beginning in [[1873]], a conflict that produced much of the religious-political and literary material recorded in Hurgronje's study of the Achehnese.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Acheh

**Acheh** (also written **Atjeh**, **Achin**, or in modern spelling **Aceh**) is the country forming the subject of *The Achehnese* of Dr. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]. It occupies the northern extremity of [[Sumatra]], and the Achehnese themselves placed the limits of their kingdom at [[Tamiang]] on the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]] and at [[Baros]] on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]]. Far more restricted, however, was the territory they described as **Acheh proper**, or what the Dutch were wont to term **[[Great Acheh]]** — the kernel of the kingdom, which has supplied the outlying districts with much of their population and has constantly striven to exercise dominion over them.

In [[1891]] Snouck Hurgronje proceeded to Acheh in pursuance of instructions from the Netherlands-India Government to make a special study of the religious element in the political conditions of that country. The materials he gathered, supplemented by further observations during the years in which the Dutch conquest was actually in progress, formed the basis of the treatise that first appeared at [[Batavia]] in [[1893]]–[[1894]] and was issued in English translation in [[1906]]. His account treats in turn the distribution of the people, the forms of government and the administration of justice, the social and domestic institutions, the adats of marriage, divorce, pregnancy, birth and childhood, and the popular religious life.

The form of the true Acheh the Achehnese delight to compare to that of a winnowing-basket (*jeu'èë*), with the débouchement of the [[Acheh River]] (*kuala Acheh*) suggesting the somewhat sharply pointed mouth of the winnower. Proceeding down stream one finds the territory of the [[XXV Mukims]] on the left, that of the [[XXVI Mukims]] on the right, and the intervening space up to the broad belt of outlying country occupied by the [[XXII Mukims]]. These three confederations or congeries of mukims are called the **three sagis** (*tiga sagi*, "three angles") — the three angles of Acheh — and the three uleebalangs or chieftains who stand, or are supposed to stand, at their head are called *panglima sagi èè*, "heads of sagi or angles." The three sagis together form the political heart of the country, and their chiefs long enjoyed the traditional function of "appointing and dethroning princes."

Acheh is distinguished in the comparative ethnography of the [[East Indian Archipelago]] by a number of striking features. The Achehnese lacked the *ta'liq* marriage contract familiar to the [[Minangkabau|Menangkabau]] and other Muslim peoples; divorce was comparatively rare; the social position of women was unusually high; and the country was ruled, between [[1641]] and [[1699]], by four successive female sovereigns. The Dutch war, which began with the [[First Dutch attack on Acheh]] in [[1873]], revealed at once the disunity of the political system and the extraordinary stubbornness of popular resistance, fed by the religious enthusiasm that gave the war its *sabil* (holy war) character. After the loss of the [[Dalam (Sultan's Court)|Dalam]] and the death of [[Sultan Mahmut Shah]], the fugitive court of the pretender [[Tuanku Muhamat Dawot]] established itself at [[Keumala]] in [[Pidie]] territory, but the sultanate continued to exercise no perceptible influence on the conduct of affairs.

From the seventeenth century onward the Sultanate of Acheh had lost effective control over the great chiefs of the State and over their dependents; even at the height of royal power under such princes as [[Alauddin al-Qahar]] (reigned [[1540]]–[[1567]]), [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam / Iskandar Muda]] ([[1607]]–[[1636]]), or his successor [[Iskandar Thani]], the show of grandeur consisted chiefly in enlargements of territory, increased authority over the ports and a consequent growth of revenue and court splendour — not in solid institutions capable of surviving dynastic overthrow. Throughout, Snouck Hurgronje insists that the institutions of Acheh are in large measure genuinely indigenous and of very great antiquity, and that the Sultan's authority — even at its strongest — never seriously displaced the ancient autonomy of the uleebalangs, the *kawoms* and the *mukims*.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Acheh

**Acheh** (also written **Atjeh**, **Achin** or, in modern spelling, **Aceh**) is the country forming the subject of *The Achehnese* of Dr. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]. It occupies the northern extremity of [[Sumatra]], and the Achehnese themselves placed the limits of their kingdom at [[Tamiang|Teumiang]] on the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]] and at [[Baros]] on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]]. Far more restricted, however, was the territory they described as **Acheh proper**, or what the Dutch were wont to term **[[Great Acheh]]** — the kernel of the kingdom, which has supplied the outlying districts with much of their population and has constantly striven to exercise dominion over them. According to the Achehnese idea, this core territory was bounded by a line extending from [[Kluang]] on the west to [[Krueng Raya]] on the [[North Coast|north coast]], and passing through [[Reueng-reueng]], [[Pancha]] and [[Janthoe]].

The form of the true Acheh the Achehnese delight to compare to that of a winnowing-basket (*jeu'èë*), with the débouchement of the [[Acheh River]] (*kuala Acheh*) suggesting the somewhat sharply pointed mouth of the winnower. Proceeding down stream one finds the territory of the [[XXV Mukims]] on the left, that of the [[XXVI Mukims]] on the right, and the intervening space up to the broad belt of outlying country occupied by the [[XXII Mukims]]. These three confederations or congeries of mukims are called the **[[Three Sagis (XXVI, XXV, XXII Mukims)|three sagis]]** (Malay *tiga sagi*, "three angles") — the three angles of Acheh — and the three uleebalangs or chieftains who stand, or are supposed to stand, at their head are called *panglima sagi èè*, "heads of sagi or angles." The three sagis together form the political heart of the country, and their chiefs long enjoyed the traditional function of "appointing and dethroning princes."

The history of this triangular kingdom and of the coast-states and islands that constituted its dependencies remained, in Snouck Hurgronje's judgement, yet to be written. European sources — accounts of travels and extracts from old archives — furnished only fragmentary materials, while the Malayan chronicles and native oral tradition, though full of interest as regards the methods of thought of their writers, could not be relied on as the groundwork of history, being rather collections of fabulous genealogies, legends and tales. It is clear from all sources that there has never been an opportunity in Acheh for a regulated and normal development of forms of government. Even under princes such as [[Alauddin al-Qahar|Alaudin al-Qahar]] (also called Sidi Mukamil, reigned [[1540]]–[[1567]]), [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam / Iskandar Muda]] ([[1607]]–[[1636]]), or his successor [[Iskandar Thani]], the show of royal grandeur consisted chiefly in enlargements of territory, increased authority over the ports and a consequent growth of revenue and court splendour — not in solid institutions capable of surviving dynastic overthrow.

Acheh is distinguished in the comparative ethnography of the [[East Indian Archipelago]] by a number of striking features. The Achehnese lacked the *ta'liq* marriage contract familiar to the [[Minangkabau|Menangkabau]] and other Muslim peoples; divorce was comparatively rare; the social position of women was unusually high; and the country was ruled, between [[1641]] and [[1699]], by four successive female sovereigns — the so-called [[Female rule of Acheh (1641–1699)|female rule of Acheh]]. The Dutch war, which began with the [[First Dutch attack on Acheh]] in [[1873]], revealed at once the disunity of the political system and the extraordinary stubbornness of popular resistance, fed by the religious enthusiasm that gave the [[Achehnese War|war]] its *[[Holy War (Jihad) against the Dutch|sabil]]* character.

Snouck Hurgronje's account of Acheh in Vol. I treats in turn the distribution of the people, the forms of government and the administration of justice, the social and domestic institutions, the adats of marriage, divorce, pregnancy, birth and childhood, and the popular religious life.
