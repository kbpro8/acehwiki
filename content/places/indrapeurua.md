**Indrapeurua** is the locality in the [[VI Mukims of the XXV|VI Mukims of the XXV]] which gave its name to the great mosque (*meuseugit raya*) founded, by tradition, by [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Sultan Meukuta Alam]] ([[Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]], reigned [[1607|1607]]–[[1636|1636]]) as the head mosque of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]] sagi of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]].

The foundation is described by Snouck Hurgronje in his account of the Achehnese mosque system. The erection of certain great mosques was, in the popular tradition, among the institutions ascribed to Sultan Meukuta Alam; such mosques were recognised as *meuseugit raya* both on account of their size and of the fact of their being erected by a sovereign prince, and also because they were regarded as the head mosques of a great number of mukims. Besides the [[Meuseugit Raya of the capital|Meuseugit Raya of the capital]] — the great mosque par excellence — there was one for each of the three sagis of Great Acheh: that of [[Indrapuri|Indrapuri]], which still existed in Snouck's day, for the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]]; that of Indrapeurua for the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]]; and that of [[Indrapatra|Indrapatra]], almost in the place where [[Ladong|Ladong]] now stands, for the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]]. Of the last two, Snouck notes, "scarcely any trace remains."

The three related toponyms — *Indrapatra*, *Indrapuri*, *Indrapeurua* — bear witness to a common layer of Hindu or Hindu-Buddhist nomenclature anterior to the Mohammedan settlement of the country. The first element *Indra-* is the name of the Vedic king of the gods, found in countless place-names of the Indianised archipelago; the second element *-peurua* appears to be an Achehnese phonological development of one of the Sanskrit place-name suffixes — possibly of *-pura* ("city") under the influence of Achehnese vowel harmony. The occurrence of these three names together in the topography of Great Acheh is one of the principal evidences of the Hindu influence on the language and civilisation of the country, of which Snouck remarks that "it is past all doubt that Hinduism exercised for a considerable time a direct or indirect influence on the language and civilization of Acheh, though there is but little trace of such influence remaining in her present popular traditions and institutions."

The mosque at Indrapeurua, like its sister foundations, owed its special reputation to its size and its royal founder, and its supervision was originally committed to *imeums* of superior rank. In the course of time, however, the office of imeum degenerated, the great mosques fell into disrepair, and at Indrapeurua, as at Indrapatra, scarcely a trace survived into the late nineteenth century. The disappearance of the building has left the name attached principally to the tradition of the [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]] foundations and to the topographical identity of the site with one of the mukims of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]].

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Indrapeurua

**Indrapeurua** is the site of the great mosque (*meuseugit raya*) of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]] of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje as one of the three sagi-mosques of the Achehnese kingdom. The mosque stood, in Snouck's account, "in the [[VI Mukims of the XXV|VI Mukims of the XXV]]" — that is, in the lowland sagi of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]] — and scarcely any trace of it remained at the time of his writing.

### The Three Sagi-Mosques

In the chapter on the Achehnese *imeum* and the administration of the mosques, Snouck describes the constitution of the great mosques of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]]. Such is the great mosque par excellence, the [[Meuseugit Raya of the Capital|Meuseugit Raya of the capital]], from which the whole district surrounding the ancient seat of royalty takes its name. "Besides this there was one for each of the three sagis of Great Acheh viz. that of [[Indrapuri|Indrapuri]], which still exists, for the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]], that of **Indrapeurua** (in the [[VI Mukims of the XXV|VI Mukims of the XXV]]) for the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]], and that of [[Indrapatra|Indrapatra]], almost in the very place where [[Ladong|Ladong]] now stands, for the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]]. Of the last two scarcely any trace remains."

The three sagi-mosques — Indrapuri, Indrapeurua, and Indrapatra — were the head mosques of the three great territorial divisions of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]]. Their erection was, by tradition, attributed to a sovereign prince, and they were regarded as the head mosques of a great number of mukims. The same chapter observes that there were also, in [[Pidie|Pidie]], a number of mosques which enjoyed the reputation of having been erected by **Meukuta Alam** and which were thus also called *meuseugit raya*; but the three sagi-mosques of Great Acheh held, in the constitution of the kingdom, a place apart.

### The VI Mukims of the XXV

The site of Indrapeurua, in the [[VI Mukims of the XXV|VI Mukims of the XXV]], places the sagi-mosque of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]] in the lowland sagi of Great Acheh. The [[VI Mukims of the XXV|VI Mukims of the XXV]] is one of the smaller wakeueh-districts of the sagi, and the choice of the district as the seat of the sagi-mosque is consistent with the political and religious importance of the lowland in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The same district is named elsewhere by Snouck in connection with the cultivation of sugar-cane, the *pula* method of padi-cultivation, and the rain-making superstitions of the Achehnese coast; it is one of the most richly documented districts of the lowland sagi.

### The Loss of the Sagi-Mosque

The disappearance of the mosque of Indrapeurua, like that of [[Indrapatra|Indrapatra]], is itself a fact of Achehnese history. The sagi-mosques were, by their nature, dependent on the patronage of the sultans and on the cohesion of the sagi; with the decline of the sultanate and the increasing independence of the uleebalangs, the great mosques fell into decay. Only [[Indrapuri|Indrapuri]], the sagi-mosque of the [[XXII Mukims|XXII]], survived in any form into Snouck's time, and its survival is in itself a mark of the relatively greater cohesion of the XXII Mukims throughout the period of the kingdom's decline.

### The Name Indra- in Achehnese Toponymy

The name **Indrapeurua** is one of a small class of *Indra*- toponyms on the Achehnese coast. The element *Indra-* — the name of the Hindu god of the atmosphere — is here combined with a second element (*peurua* or *poerua*) that is probably an Achehnese or Malay formation rather than a Sanskrit one, suggesting that the toponym is a hybrid: a Hindu element attached to a local place-name by way of elevating it to royal or sacred rank. Such hybrid toponyms are characteristic of the transition from the Hindu to the Mohammedan period, and they survive, in the toponymy of the mosques and the markets of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], as evidence of the Hindu substratum of Achehnese civilisation.
