The **Netherlands** (Dutch: *Nederland*; also *Holland* in common English and contemporary Dutch usage) is the European state whose colonial possessions in the East Indian Archipelago form the principal subject of Van der Kemp's monograph. As the metropolitan power behind the [[Netherlands India|Netherlands Indies]] administration at [[Batavia]], and as co-signatory with Great Britain of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 17 March 1824]], the Netherlands is one of the two central political actors in Van der Kemp's account, mentioned on more than forty of its pages.

Throughout the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries the Netherlands alternated between great-power status and the role of a satellite of stronger European states. The Napoleonic occupation had dissolved the old Dutch East India Company (VOC) and led to the British interregnum in [[Java]] (1811–1816). The restoration of Dutch sovereignty, formalised at the [[Convention of 24 January 1817]] in [[London]], reconstituted the Netherlands as a colonial power, but left it in a markedly weaker position than Britain in the Eastern Seas. Van der Kemp repeatedly emphasises the asymmetry: the Dutch plenipotentiaries in [[1824]] — [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] and [[Captain Elout|Elout]] — were conscious that they could not press their claims too hard without risking the collapse of the negotiations, and they accordingly yielded on points (the [[Acheh]] reservation, the surrender of [[Bencoolen]], the abandonment of Indian factories) that would, in retrospect, prove fateful.

A recurring motif in Van der Kemp's treatment is the contrast between the bold assertions of Dutch supremacy over [[Sumatra]] made in the Netherlands itself and the actual conduct of Dutch officials in the Archipelago. The "solemn denial" of Sumatran supremacy, he notes, was a Dutch domestic position; but it rested on a clause — the Acheh reservation in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty]] — that the Netherlands' own plenipotentiaries had at the time treated as a mere bagatelle. Half a century later the bill came due: the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871]] required the Netherlands to cede the [[Cape of Guinea|Dutch Guinea Coast]] in West Africa and to recognise British rights in northern Borneo, in exchange for Britain's release of the Acheh reservation.

Van der Kemp also discusses the institutional framework within which the Netherlands conducted its colonial policy. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs was at this period temporarily without a leading head, and the Department of Colonies was likewise headless because its minister, Falck, was serving as plenipotentiary in London. The day-to-day drafting of advice thus fell to [[Elout|Minister Elout of Finance]] and to the diplomat [[Ridder Reinhold]]. [[J. C. Baud|J. C. Baud]], in his letter from [[The Hague]] of [[28 November 1822]], warned that the Netherlands must people the Archipelago with Dutch colonists as a counterweight to possible foreign encroachment — a strategic foresight that Van der Kemp credits as anticipating the later American preoccupation with the Philippines.

The Netherlands of Van der Kemp's own day — the kingdom of Queen Wilhelmina, governing the East Indies through Governors-General like [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and advised by scholars such as [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje]] and [[Pieter Johannes Veth|P. J. Veth]] — is the silent backdrop against which the entire monograph is written. Van der Kemp, himself a Dutch colonial historian, writes with an evident sense that the missteps of [[1819]] and [[1824]] were bearing their fruit in the Acheh War of his own time.
