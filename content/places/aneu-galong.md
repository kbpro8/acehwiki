**Aneu Galong** — also written *Aneu' Galong*, *Aneu'Galong*, *Aneu-Galong*, or *Aneu' Galoom* — was a fortress in the interior of [[Great Acheh|Great-Acheh]] which figured in the closing phase of the [[Acheh War]] as one of the strongholds held by the family and following of the celebrated [[Teungku Tiro|Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri]]. It was carried by Netherlands troops in [[1896]], an episode memorable in the chronicle of the *sabil* (holy-war) warriors both for the death in action of members of the patriarchal generation of the Tiro family and for the inheritance it transferred to the infant grandson who would lead the last phase of the resistance.

## The Engagement of 1896

The Dutch capture of Aneu Galong in [[1896]] is one of the few dated engagements with which the chroniclers of the Tiro family connect a known casualty. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s footnote in *The Achehnese* Vol. I records the event laconically: **Mat Amin** — *Tgk. Mat Amin* — fell when the Netherlands troops carried the fortress of Aneu' Galong. In Snouck's account Mat Amin is identified as "the son of [[Teungku Tiro]]", and his death marks the moment at which the family of Teungku Tiro lost its most prominent military heir.

The later Dutch account by [[G. W. T. Dames]] in *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]* (1954) records two distinct casualties at the same action. In Dames's words: "Van de vier zoons van *[[Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri]]* was de oudste, tevens de opvolger, in [[1896]] gesneuveld als commandant bij de verdediging Aneu Galong" — "Of the four sons of Teungkoe Sjech Saman di Tiri, the eldest, who was also the successor, fell in 1896 as commandant at the defence of Aneu Galong." In addition to this eldest son and successor, *[[Tgk. Mat Amin]]* also fell at the same engagement as a *geloofsgetuige* (faith-witness, martyr). In Dames's account, therefore, Tgk. Mat Amin was not the eldest son himself but a separate fighter whose infant son *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]* was at the breast when his father died at Aneu Galong and would inherit the holy war. The two accounts thus differ on whether Mat Amin was himself the eldest son and successor of Teungku Tiro, or a separate *geloofsgetuige* killed in the same engagement — a discrepancy which the two source chronicles do not resolve.

The capture of Aneu Galong belongs to the broader turn of [[1896]], the moment at which, in Dames's phrase, "het lijdelijk afwachten was afgelopen en de troepen zelf tot actie overgingen" — the passive waiting was over and the troops themselves went over to the offensive. It thus stands at the head of the Dutch offensive of the late 1890s that would culminate in the [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]-era *colonnes* of the early 1900s.

## Aftermath: The Brothers of Teungku Tiro

After the fall of Aneu Galong the work of collecting the *sabil* (holy-war) contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]] devolved on the slain man's surviving brothers. Of these, **Teungku Beb** (who died in [[1900]]) and **Teungku Mahidin** — also called **Ma' Et** — together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died [[1901]]), a man much respected for his piety and learning, "continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the sabil contributions in the dependencies on the North Coast." Aneu Galong may thus be read as the geographical symbol of a transition: from the personal leadership of Teungku Tiro's house to the more diffuse and increasingly defensive ulama-resistance of the late 1890s.

## Significance

Aneu Galong is significant both in the chronicle of the [[Teungku Tiro|Tiro family]] and in the narrative structure of *[[Oom Ambon van het K.N.I.L.]]*. In the latter work it is the chronological anchor of the [[colonne Schmidt campaign against the Tiriō Teungkoes (1910-1911)|colonne Schmidt]] narrative: the moment at which the inheritance of the resistance was transferred to the surviving sons and the infant grandson *[[Tgk. Tjhi Ma'at]]*. The boy would grow up to refuse submission on the grounds that "Zijn Vader was als *geloofsgetuige* gevallen, hij zou in diens voetstappen treden" — "His father had fallen as a faith-witness; he would follow in his footsteps" — and would himself be killed at [[Tangsé]] in [[1911]] as the last leader of the *bende* (band). The place thus marks the generational arc of the Tiro resistance: the patriarchal generation killed at Aneu Galong in 1896; the last leader killed at Tangsé in 1911.

With the fall of Aneu Galong the effective military arm of the Tiro tradition was broken, although the religious authority of the line persisted long after the Dutch had consolidated their hold on Great Acheh under General [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]].
