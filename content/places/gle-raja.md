**Glé Raja** — the Raja mountain, the Raja-gebergte — was the steep mountain mass near [[Indrapoeri]] whose bare outrunners narrowed the valley of the [[Acheh River|Atjeh-rivier]] from the west and marked, in the observation of [[W. Cool]] and [[G. B. Hooijer]], the natural boundary between the river's upper and lower courses, as described in their *[[Eene schoone bladzijde uit Atjeh's geschiedenis (Cool & Hooijer, 1889)|Eene schoone bladzijde uit Atjeh's geschiedenis]]* ([[1889]]).

## In the topography of the upper valley

From the berg-plateau of [[14 August]] [[1879]] the bare outrunners of the Glé Raja were seen pressing in from the west, behind [[Indrapoeri]], to constrict the Atjeh valley; to the west of them lay faintly the alluvial plains of [[Groot-Atjeh]] and the rocky curved ridge of the Glé Datar. It was equally imposing by contrast of height: the range stood with the 6000-foot [[Glé Mentelah]] and the 7500-foot, heavily wooded [[Glé Agam]] — to the west of which the eye could only follow, through the forest-filled narrow gap where the Kroeng Djerir ran, between the mountain of that name and the Glé Raja — as one of the giants holding the inner valley.

## Strategic significance

In the final strategic chapter of the book the Glé Raja is one of the two "opstaande zijden" of the defensive triangle of Groot-Atjeh's conquest: the steep Raja-gebergte formed, together with the uninhabited bare hill-ridges east of the [[XXVI Moekims]], the standing side by which the Dutch line of [[1879]] resisted hostile irruption from the east — while the apex of the triangle at Indrapoeri held the natural citadel of the whole system (see [[Indrapoeri defensive triangle (1879)]]). Over the [[XXII Moekims]] the mountain spoke with the [[Goudberg]]: "Wanneer de donder weerkaatst van den Goudberg naar den Glé Raja" — when the thunder re-echoed from the Goudberg to the Glé Raja — is the image under which Hooijer elsewhere described the storms of the highland war.
