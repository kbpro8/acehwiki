The **Gayo** are a Mohammedan people of the central highlands of North Sumatra, inhabiting the mountainous interior behind [[Acheh]] proper and tributary to the Achehnese sultanate. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, the Gayos appear chiefly as the highland allies and refuge-givers of the lowland Achehnese in time of war.

## The Gayos and Alassers as Subjects of Acheh

The Gayos and the [[Alas|Alassers]] are characterised as "Mohammedan subjects of Acheh." They inhabit the [[Central highlands of North Sumatra|central highlands of North Sumatra]], a region separated from the populated districts of lowland Acheh by "a huge ring of forest." This ring of forest, Snouck Hurgronje explains, is what gave the Achehnese an advantage in guerilla warfare: between the populated districts the Achehnese finds sometimes jungles and sometimes swamps in which he can conceal himself, and beyond those jungles lies the highland country of the Gayos and Alassers, an almost inaccessible refuge.

## A Refuge for Achehnese Bands

During the [[Achehnese War|war with the Dutch]], the Gayo country became a strategic sanctuary for Achehnese bands. Whenever lowland gampong-dwellers had anything to their discredit, they knew where they could safely retire to. Furthermore, "bands could count on safe hiding-places when they wished among the Gayos and Alassers, Mohammedan subjects of Acheh." Achehnese bands had to gather together and act for short periods only, wherever through enquiries or the reports of their spies they had reason to believe that they stood a good chance of winning some advantage or another. They were rarely exposed to attack on any large scale, for the almost inaccessible country that they understood in every detail helped them as an ally.

## Dutch Expeditions into the Gayo Highlands

The strategic importance of the Gayo highlands imposed itself on Dutch operations. Several military expeditions lasting weeks, "yes, and months," were necessary towards the central highlands of the Gayos in order to track down the enemy. From [[1898]] the rule was enforced that all houses and settlements should be spared in the case of enemies as well as friends, a measure that illustrates the difficulty of distinguishing combatants from non-combatants in this theatre of guerilla warfare. The Gayo country, in short, served as the strategic depth of Achehnese resistance, and its subjugation was a necessary prelude to the pacification of the lowlands.
