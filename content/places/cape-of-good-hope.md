The **Cape of Good Hope** (Dutch: *Kaap de Goede Hoop*, commonly *De Kaap*) is the southern promontory of Africa and, by extension, the British colonial possession — the Cape Colony — that grew up around it. In Van der Kemp's monograph the Cape figures only in the closing pages, as the place where [[D. P. Faure]] edited the *Volksblad*, a Dutch-language newspaper in which he published his recollections of the [[1884]] Transvaal negotiations.

The Cape had been a Dutch East India Company station until its capture by Britain in [[1795]] (restored in [[1803]] and retaken in [[1806]]), and was formally ceded to Britain by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London 1824|London treaty of 1824]] and subsequent conventions. By the late nineteenth century it was a self-governing British colony with a substantial Dutch-speaking settler population, and its press was a natural venue for discussion of South African affairs. Faure, having returned from accompanying the Transvaal deputation to [[London]], wrote an article in the *Volksblad* — Van der Kemp quotes from the issue of [[17 April 1884]] — declaring that "the suzerainty is abolished. This, as we understand, was the opinion of the Transvaal republicans; and this, the most painful of their grievances, has now been removed" (Van der Kemp, 1900, p. 239).

Van der Kemp introduces the Cape reference to corroborate Faure's later testimony to [[Joseph Chamberlain]]. The fact that Faure's account had been published immediately on his return, before the suzerainty question had become a "burning question of the day" (Van der Kemp, 1900, p. 214), is presented as evidence that his memory had not been reshaped by later controversy. The Cape, in this respect, serves less as a place of action than as a documentary vantage point from which the historian can triangulate the truth of the [[Transvaal]] negotiations.
