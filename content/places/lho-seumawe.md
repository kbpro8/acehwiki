## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Lho' Seumawe

**Lho' Seumawe** is a place on the north coast of [[Acheh]] whose chief bore the title **Mahraja** or **Mbahraja**. In *The Achehnese* it is mentioned in connection with the group of honorific titles — *ne'* ("grandfather"), *apòë* ("elder brother"), *Mahraja* — by which the relations between the Sultans of Acheh and certain uleebalangs were traditionally expressed.

### The Title of Mahraja / Mbahraja

Snouck Hurgronje observes that the Achehnese explained the title of the chief of Lho' Seumawe, *Mahraja* or *Mbahraja*, as expressing a relationship of "father of the king." The explanation was part of a wider pattern of honorary kinship between the Sultan and his more important territorial chiefs. Just as the powerful chief of the highlands, the [[Panglima Polem]], bore the title *apòë* ("elder brother") of the Sultan, so two uleebalangs bore the official title of *ne'* ("grandfather") of the Sultan: namely, the chief of the Mukim Meura'sa (and, in earlier times, of the [[VI Mukims of the XXV]]), and the chief of the IX Mukims of the [[XXV Mukims]], known as [[Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia]] and [[Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa]] respectively.

### The Problem of the Title

Snouck Hurgronje treats the popular explanation of *Mahraja* with some scepticism. It would indeed, he remarks, be very strange that a chief should obtain the title of Maharaja — a Sanskritic royal title of the highest rank — while his liege lord was simply called Raja of Acheh. The Achehnese gloss "father of the king" (*mbah* = grandfather or father; *raja* = king) appears to be a popular etymology superimposed on a borrowed Indian title whose original meaning had been forgotten. The case is parallel to that of *Panglima Polem* itself: the tradition which made the first Polem the son of a Sultan, spared in infancy by the highlanders, is dismissed by Snouck Hurgronje as containing "not a single grain of historic truth." The title, in his view, "probably typified the original relation between the powerful sagi-chief and the Sultan" — that is, an alliance of near-equals rather than a literal kinship.

### Historical Significance

Whatever its etymology, the title borne by the chief of Lho' Seumawe marks the place as one of the chiefdoms whose relations with the Sultanate dated from a remote period, and whose hereditary prestige exceeded that of ordinary uleebalangs. Lho' Seumawe is also well known from later colonial history as the site of important industrial and military development under the Dutch; but in Snouck Hurgronje's account its interest lies chiefly in the light its titular nomenclature throws on the political constitution of the older Sultanate.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Lho Seumawe

**Lho Seumawe** is a coastal place in the north-east of Acheh mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the seat of an uleebalangship whose chief bore the title **Mahraja** (or **Mbahraja**) — a title popularly explained by the Achehnese as expressing a relationship of fatherhood to the king, and so testifying to the high standing of this chief among the hereditary office-bearers of the sultanate.

### The Mahraja of Lho Seumawe

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Lho Seumawe in his discussion of the hereditary uleebalangships whose titles had come down from the early centuries of the sultanate. He explains how the great hereditary chiefs liked to figure as relations of the Sultan — "elder brothers" or "grandfathers" — and goes on: "Just in the same way we find two uleebalangs with the official title of 'grand-fathers' (*ne') of the sultan viz. the chief of the Mukim [[Meura'sa]] (and also in earlier times of the VI Mukims) and the chief of the IX Mukims of the XXV, [[Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia|Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia]] and [[Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa|Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa]]. The title of the chief of Lho' Seumawe, **Mahraja** or **Mbahraja**, is also explained by the Achehnese as expressing such a relationship ('father of the king'); it would indeed be very strange that such a chief should obtain the title of Maharaja while his liege lord was simply called Raja of Acheh."

### Significance of the Title

Snouck's point is characteristically sceptical. The Achehnese explanation — that the Mahraja of Lho Seumawe was so called because he was the "father of the king" — he treats as a popular etymology of the kind that the Achehnese loved to invent for the titles of their great hereditary chiefs. The very strangeness of the title (a Maharaja in vassalage to a mere Raja) was itself testimony to the great antiquity of the office, and to its having survived from a time when the relative ranks of the sultan and his outlying vassals had been different.

### Significance

Although Lho Seumawe itself is mentioned in Vol. I only in this discussion of titles, its situation on the north-eastern coast made it one of the principal ports of the sultanate. The [[Mahraja of Lho' Seumawe|Mahraja]] who ruled it was one of the most venerable hereditary chiefs of Acheh, and the survival of his Indianised title at so late a date is itself an important fact in the political anthropology of the sultanate. Vol. II of *The Achehnese* treats Lho Seumawe and its dependencies in greater detail.

## Van Daalen's governorship and its consequences

During the governorship of [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] ([[1905]]–[[1907]]), the local civil administrator at Lho' Seumawé proved totally unsuited to his task. When [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] visited the district in [[1907]] as part of the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]], he was shocked to find that the chiefs openly refused to cooperate, complaining of unjust treatment and loss of income; the interior above Keureu'toë had not seen a military patrol or civil administrator for months; local religious leaders (*teungkoe's*) had used the vacuum to turn the population against the Dutch; and the chiefs warned Van Heutsz that "if you go there now, you will get fire everywhere," whereas under the previous administrator ([[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]]), they had come without any military escort. Van Heutsz immediately replaced the incompetent official and ordered a restoration of regular patrols and dialogue. After Van Daalen's removal in [[1908]], Lho' Seumawé was gradually brought back under effective control, and the policy of supporting the chiefs was resumed. The district later became a symbol of how poor local leadership could undo the gains of pacification.

## Account from *De Buitenbezittingen* (1917) — Lhôseumawè

**Lhôseumawè** is the harbour town and *onderafdeelingshoofdplaats* on the boundary between the *afdeeling Noordkust* and the *afdeeling Oostkust* of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]], whose position at the natural junction of the two coast-cultivation zones made it, in the colonial period, one of the three great trade centres of the Gouvernement alongside [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]] and [[Langsa|Langsa]]. *De Buitenbezittingen* records that, in the years before the handbook's compilation, Lhôseumawè had become "het handels-emporium van een groot deel der Noord- en Oostkust" — the trade emporium of a great part of the North and East Coasts — though the rise of Langsa had already begun to draw off some of its trade.

## The onderafdeeling

The *onderafdeeling Lhôseumawè*, like the other *onderafdeelingen* of the Noordkust, was composed of various *landschappen* whose internal administration combined the Groot-Atjehsche system with local variations produced by the expansion of pepper cultivation and the rise of *oelèëbalang-tjoet*-schappen. The town of Lhôseumawè itself was, like [[Sigli|Sigli]], [[Bireuën|Bireuën]], [[Langsa|Langsa]], and [[Koeala Simpang|Koeala Simpang]], *onttrokken aan de landschapsbesturen* — withdrawn from the *landschap* administrations — and placed under direct Dutch rule as the *onderafdeelingshoofdplaats*.

## Trade and shipping

Lhôseumawè's emergence as a trade emporium is documented in the harbour statistics of *De Buitenbezittingen*. In [[1913]] the harbour recorded 193 steamship arrivals totalling 311,000 cubic metres; in [[1914]], 179 arrivals totalling 354,000 cubic metres; in [[1915]], 157 arrivals totalling 379,000 cubic metres. The opbrengst der in- en uitvoerrechten (revenue from import and export duties) rose from f 56,000 (in) and f 17,000 (out) in [[1910]] to f 90,000 and f 25,000 in [[1911]], settling at f 87,000–f 90,000 (in) and f 28,000–f 32,000 (out) in the years [[1912]]–[[1914]]. The Koninklijke Paketvaart Maatschappij's Route 1 (Java-Padang-Atjeh, 14-day cycle) and Route 3a (Penang-Atjeh, 9-day cycle) both called at Lhôseumawè.

## The Volkscredietwezen

Lhôseumawè was also the seat of the *Afdeelingsbank "Lhôseumawè"*, one of the three principal *Volkscredietwezen* institutions of the Gouvernement (alongside the [[Groot-Atjehsche Afdeelingsbank|Groot-Atjehsche Afdeelingsbank]] and the *Credietbank "Langsa"*). The bank, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, suffered from the same *achterstand* — arrears — and *kwade practijken* — abuses — that afflicted the Groot-Atjehsche bank, though to a less catastrophic degree. Its financial overview, influence, and the causes of its arrears are recorded in the handbook's chapter on the *Volkscredietwezen*.

## Account from *Verkenningen in Atjeh* (1923)

**Lho Seumawe** — modern *Lhokseumawe* — is the chief place (*hoofdplaats*) of the *onderafdeeling* of the same name in the *afdeeling* [[Noordkust van Atjeh|Noordkust van Atjeh]], and one of the principal harbours of the north-eastern coast of Atjeh. The town lies on an island with lagoons, connected with Sumatra by a bridge of 120 metres. It was formerly the chief harbour on the north-eastern coast, and is still one of the foremost.

### The Town and Its Discomforts

Favourably situated on the bay, in which ships can come close to the pier, Lho' Seumawé was formerly the chief harbour on the north-eastern coast, and is still one of the foremost. The town lies on an island with lagoons, connected with Sumatra by a bridge of 120 metres. The town bears the burdens of its situation on alluvial ground, and when a Dutch settlement had to come, nobody thought of building himself a villa there. The military dwellings arose in the style and colour of necessity, and private offices came beside, before, behind them in hasty wooden erection, and so Lho' Seumawé became a gloomy dwelling-place without a smile. Langsa with its dull room, Idi with its small quiet, are far preferable to the harbour-town of the ancient kingdom of [[Samoedra|Samoedra]]!

But the present government wishes to make a virtue of necessity and asks cooperation for plans for improvement, first of all of health. The lagoon-coast sends flights of mosquitoes into the town evening after evening, and Broersma has seen walls in a large house that were black with mosquitoes in the corners. The drainage of terrain on the island is fortunately not a work that only great entrepreneurs can do or that costs years of labour, so that a better future is in sight. Where the town itself has so little charm, it is the more to be praised that the *gemeentefonds* is active there in compensating the townsmen, as far as possible, for what they must bear on account of their situation. The fund manages a water-supply from the sand-flat to the town, so that suitable water can be had in the houses. The wells, here and there, yield little and brackish water. The fund also maintains a sea-bath, in which an enclosure removes the danger from animals. The Europeans pay one guilder a month for it; the population also uses it. The fund maintains the market, a slaughterhouse, a cinema with band.

### Trade

The trade has in recent years lost some of its importance, wherefore for example the Atjeh-Mij. has reduced its establishment there. Chinese handle the known export-products mostly to Penang and settle them against the import of manufactures, and also against money and bills. They would gladly have credit on the consignment of goods shipped. Lho' Seumawé handles the import and export for the whole *afdeeling* Noordkust van Atjeh, except however for [[Samalanga|Samalanga]], which uses [[Sigli|Sigli]] for that movement. In that lies an indication that Sigli is on the rising line and Lho' Seumawé on the falling one. In [[1921]] 120 ships were cleared; in [[1922]] 108, almost all of the [[Kon. Paketvaart|Kon. Paketvaart]]. The export was, in 1918 and 1921: copra 734.120 and 1.425.694 kg; pinang-nut 6.186.492 and 7.088.652 kg; pepper 558.266 and 793.115 kg. The receipts of the toll-office were, in 1920, 1921 and 1922: import-duty f 117.062 / f 164.520 / f 159.338; petroleum-cijns f 3.101 / f 4.807 / f 10.177; match-cijns f 36.931 / f 34.226 / f 4.038; match-cijns f 13.783 / f 12.347 / f 29.165. In comparing these figures one must bear in mind that the import-duties were raised by 100 percent in 1921 and again by 100 percent in 1922. Of increasing import-trade there is therefore no question.

### The Maharadja

The *onderafdeeling* comprises many *landschappen*, of which one of the small is called Lho' Seumawé, but the head was of old a respected prince, bearing among other titles that of *[[Maharadja|maharadja]]*, which points to his former standing with the sultan and in the whole of Atjeh. When about fifty years before Broersma's visit — that is, in the years around [[1873]] — a representative of the Dutch government wished to meet the *maharadja* and came ashore from his ship in the bay to do so, the *maharadja* awaited him, leaning against a tree, *trotsch* — proud. He and many of the chiefs stood with loaded revolvers in their belts; the signs of submissiveness were far off. A still-living witness, quoted by [[J. A. Kruyt|J. A. Kruyt]], described him as *'een ruw woesteling'* — *a rough savage* — *'hij was groot en zwaar, liep met lange loshangende haren en was gekleed in een zwart jasje met gouden sovereigns als knoopen.'* The *maharadja* of Broersma's visit was [[Sri Tengkoe Abdoel Hamid Orangkaja Sri Maharadja Mangkoe Boemi|Sri Tengkoe Abdoel Hamid Orangkaja Sri Maharadja Mangkoe Boemi]], who signed the *[[Korte Verklaring (1899)|Korte Verklaring]]* in [[1904]].

### The Sarikat Islam

In the whole *afdeeling* Noordkust van Atjeh with its three *onderafdeelingen* — Lho' Seumawé, [[Bireuen|Bireuën]] and [[Lho Soekon|Lho Soekon]] — the [[Sarikat Islam|Sarikat Islam]] showed a vigorous life after [[1918]]. Traders, school-teachers — *'laat ons zeggen de intellectueelen'* — but also several chiefs were members and took part. A chief of Samalanga had joined by way of a move on the political chessboard on which he was playing against the influential *Maharadja*, who had also become a member of the Sarikat Islam in order to strengthen his chances of retaining a *landschap Plimbang*. The movement in Lho' Seumawe took the direction of bringing the distinction between believers and unbelievers to the fore, of undermining the Dutch government, and of working towards a union of *oeléebalangs*. Three chiefs of Lho' Seumawe were banished in [[1922]], an intervention Broersma describes as *'geen dag te vroeg gekomen'* — *not a day too soon.*

### The HVA at Boeloeh Blang Ara

Not far westward of Lho' Seumawé one reaches, by a narrow bumpy road, the estate [[Boeloeh Blang Ara|Boeloeh Blang Ara]], which the [[Handelsvereeniging Amsterdam|Handelsvereeniging Amsterdam]] opened in [[1920]] and gave up as failed in [[1922]] after spending eight tons of gold on it. The failure of that estate is one of the great disappointments of the post-war planting-period in Atjeh.

## Swart's administration, 1903–1905

On [[12 October]] [[1903]] Swart was relieved of the government of the sub-division [[Poelò Raja]] and charged with the government of the sub-division Lho' Seumawè in the *Afdeeling Onderhoorigheden van Atjeh*, the assignment being described as temporary; on [[24 October]] [[1903]] he was extraordinarily promoted *Majoor*. He was honourably relieved of the post on [[3 April]] [[1905]]. It was during his period at Lho' Seumawè that the patrol of sergeant [[Vollaers]] was destroyed at [[Meurandéh Paja]] on [[26 January]] [[1905]], leading to the arrest of [[T. Tjhi' Toenòng]].

## The meeting of 1908

In [[1908]], immediately after assuming the governorship, Swart held a meeting at Lho' Seumawè with all the *oelèëbalangs* of the eastern part of the province. Discussing the situation, he pointed out that in various *landschappen* there were again *[[djahat]]s*, whereupon one of the chiefs answered: "not only are there evildoers everywhere, but we ourselves are evildoers too!" There followed the discussion of the disastrous [[Boetenstelsel|system of fines]] and the withholding of the chiefs' incomes, of which Swart promised the immediate return of what had been wrongly withheld. At the same meeting he demanded an end to the attacks on the [[Atjehtram]]: "they still knew me there from the time when I was a major at Lho' Seumawè and so they knew that it would come to blows. Then they said: 'you can rely on it'. And from that time the tramway line there was never destroyed again."

## In the resistance years

[[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] noted in his letter of July [[1908]] that in the Lho' Seumawè country, where Swart had formerly travelled alone even from [[Lho' Soekon]] through [[Matang Koeli]] to [[Paja Bakong]], it was now unsafe even with military escort. In the resistance of [[1908]]–[[1912]] Lho' Seumawè nevertheless remained the most favourable district; the administration there had for a considerable time been in the hands of a *controleur*, though the garrison of the bivouac [[Boeloh Blang Ara]] under [[M. Schouten|Schouten]] repeatedly had its hands full. By [[1910]] the *[[Koloniaal Verslag]]* could report that the European administrators could visit a great part of their *ressorten* without military escort — "in Lho' Seumawè the whole sub-division."

It was to Lho' Seumawè that the woman found wandering near the raided *ladangs* of [[Tgk. di Mata Ië]] in October [[1911]] was sent for care. On [[13 March]] [[1919]] [[T. Radja Sabi]] was recognised at Lho' Seumawè by one of his former *kawans* and brought to his uncle [[T. Tjhi' Béntara]], ending nine years of wandering.

## The letter of 1933

[[T. Maharadja Mangkoeboemi]], *zelfbestuurder* of the *landschap* Lho' Seumawè, was the first signatory and one of the two drafters of the [[Letter of the Acehnese Chiefs to General Swart (26 September 1933)|letter of the Acehnese chiefs]] to Swart on his seventieth birthday.

## Account from *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* ([[1923]])

**Lho Seumawè** (modern Lhokseumawe) was the deep harbour of the North Coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] which in the last years of the old order served as sea-outlet for the smuggled trade of the closed [[Pidie|Pidië]] tract, and which under Dutch rule developed into a garrison and commercial town — giving its name to the petroleum establishments of [[1899]], to a credit bank of [[1913]], and to the [[Handelsvereeniging Amsterdam|H.V.A.]]'s ill-fated estate [[Boeloe Belang Ara]] in its neighbourhood.

### The open harbour

Under the resolution of [[29 May]] [[1883]] Lho Seumawè belonged to the few harbours remaining open for import and export; and when the [[Pidie|Pidië]] tract was closed for the coasting trade, the population resorted to clandestine transport over land to [[Meureudoe]] and thence by sea to Lho Seumawè, until the licence system of July [[1894]] at [[Sigli]] relieved them. In [[1899]] the [[Bataafsche Petroleum Maatschappij|Koninklijke Petroleum Maatschappij]] founded establishments at Lho Seumawè and [[Peureula]]. From [[1903]] to [[1907]] the [[Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij]] kept a temporary agency there (at a level equal to that of Koeta Radja) while the coastal tramway was being built.

### The tramway and later development

Funds for the line [[Sigli]]–Lho Seumawè were granted in the budget of [[1900]]; construction proceeded from both ends — the middle section in [[Samalanga]] being politically inaccessible — and was completed in April [[1904]] after three iron bridges had been washed away by *bandjirs*. From Lho Seumawè the route was continued under the budget of [[1901]] toward [[Idi]], after a proposal for a line from the [[Aroebai|Aroe-baai]] to Lho Seumawè had been rejected by the Government. The town received a Hollandsch-Indlandsche school in [[1916]]; in [[1913]] a credit bank for the North Coast arose there (with that at [[Langsar]] for the East Coast), and a government post- and telegraph-office, and agencies of the [[Atjehsche Handelmaatschappij]] and of Medan houses. In [[1922]] N. [[N. van Zalinge|van Zalinge]] reported that the shipping-off of pinang-nuts and copra from Lho Seumawè, though it showed no increase over that year, would grow in the future "since ever more coconut- and pinang-trees are planted", while trade centred ever more on [[Langsar]], the rising estate district in its vicinity.
