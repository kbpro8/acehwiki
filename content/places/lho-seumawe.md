An important port and centre on the North Coast. The Mahraja (chief) of Lho' Seumawe is mentioned: 'I will not live at Sawang Keupula, for I fear lest the Mahraja (of Lho' Seumawe) may carry me off.' (sadati dialogue). Lho' Seumawe appears in the list of North Coast places referenced in the hikayats and sadati performances.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Lho' Seumawe

**Lho' Seumawe** is a place on the north coast of [[Acheh]] whose chief bore the title **Mahraja** or **Mbahraja**. In *The Achehnese* it is mentioned in connection with the group of honorific titles — *ne'* ("grandfather"), *apòë* ("elder brother"), *Mahraja* — by which the relations between the Sultans of Acheh and certain uleebalangs were traditionally expressed.

### The Title of Mahraja / Mbahraja

Snouck Hurgronje observes that the Achehnese explained the title of the chief of Lho' Seumawe, *Mahraja* or *Mbahraja*, as expressing a relationship of "father of the king." The explanation was part of a wider pattern of honorary kinship between the Sultan and his more important territorial chiefs. Just as the powerful chief of the highlands, the [[Panglima Polem]], bore the title *apòë* ("elder brother") of the Sultan, so two uleebalangs bore the official title of *ne'* ("grandfather") of the Sultan: namely, the chief of the Mukim Meura'sa (and, in earlier times, of the [[VI Mukims of the XXV]]), and the chief of the IX Mukims of the [[XXV Mukims]], known as [[Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia]] and [[Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa]] respectively.

### The Problem of the Title

Snouck Hurgronje treats the popular explanation of *Mahraja* with some scepticism. It would indeed, he remarks, be very strange that a chief should obtain the title of Maharaja — a Sanskritic royal title of the highest rank — while his liege lord was simply called Raja of Acheh. The Achehnese gloss "father of the king" (*mbah* = grandfather or father; *raja* = king) appears to be a popular etymology superimposed on a borrowed Indian title whose original meaning had been forgotten. The case is parallel to that of *Panglima Polem* itself: the tradition which made the first Polem the son of a Sultan, spared in infancy by the highlanders, is dismissed by Snouck Hurgronje as containing "not a single grain of historic truth." The title, in his view, "probably typified the original relation between the powerful sagi-chief and the Sultan" — that is, an alliance of near-equals rather than a literal kinship.

### Historical Significance

Whatever its etymology, the title borne by the chief of Lho' Seumawe marks the place as one of the chiefdoms whose relations with the Sultanate dated from a remote period, and whose hereditary prestige exceeded that of ordinary uleebalangs. Lho' Seumawe is also well known from later colonial history as the site of important industrial and military development under the Dutch; but in Snouck Hurgronje's account its interest lies chiefly in the light its titular nomenclature throws on the political constitution of the older Sultanate.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Lho Seumawe

**Lho Seumawe** is a coastal place in the north-east of Acheh mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the seat of an uleebalangship whose chief bore the title **Mahraja** (or **Mbahraja**) — a title popularly explained by the Achehnese as expressing a relationship of fatherhood to the king, and so testifying to the high standing of this chief among the hereditary office-bearers of the sultanate.

### The Mahraja of Lho Seumawe

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Lho Seumawe in his discussion of the hereditary uleebalangships whose titles had come down from the early centuries of the sultanate. He explains how the great hereditary chiefs liked to figure as relations of the Sultan — "elder brothers" or "grandfathers" — and goes on: "Just in the same way we find two uleebalangs with the official title of 'grand-fathers' (*ne') of the sultan viz. the chief of the Mukim [[Meura'sa]] (and also in earlier times of the VI Mukims) and the chief of the IX Mukims of the XXV, [[Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia|Teuku Ne' Raja Muda Seutia]] and [[Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa|Teuku Ne' Peureuba Wangsa]]. The title of the chief of Lho' Seumawe, **Mahraja** or **Mbahraja**, is also explained by the Achehnese as expressing such a relationship ('father of the king'); it would indeed be very strange that such a chief should obtain the title of Maharaja while his liege lord was simply called Raja of Acheh."

### Significance of the Title

Snouck's point is characteristically sceptical. The Achehnese explanation — that the Mahraja of Lho Seumawe was so called because he was the "father of the king" — he treats as a popular etymology of the kind that the Achehnese loved to invent for the titles of their great hereditary chiefs. The very strangeness of the title (a Maharaja in vassalage to a mere Raja) was itself testimony to the great antiquity of the office, and to its having survived from a time when the relative ranks of the sultan and his outlying vassals had been different.

### Significance

Although Lho Seumawe itself is mentioned in Vol. I only in this discussion of titles, its situation on the north-eastern coast made it one of the principal ports of the sultanate. The [[Mahraja of Lho' Seumawe|Mahraja]] who ruled it was one of the most venerable hereditary chiefs of Acheh, and the survival of his Indianised title at so late a date is itself an important fact in the political anthropology of the sultanate. Vol. II of *The Achehnese* treats Lho Seumawe and its dependencies in greater detail.
