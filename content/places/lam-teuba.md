**Lam Teuba** is a locality in [[Acheh]] associated in the ethnographic record of *The Achehnese* with the manufacture of poisonous charms drawn from a species of toadstool (*kulat*). The poison-makers (*ureueng meukulat*) of Lam Teuba were regarded as the best in the country, and their reputation placed the village at the centre of one of the darker branches of Achehnese popular magic.

## The Kulat and Its Practitioners

Snouck Hurgronje, in his chapter on Achehnese magic, describes how certain sorts of toadstool — *kulat* — were very much dreaded by the Achehnese. The preparation of these for malicious uses formed a special branch of mystic craft, and the scoundrels who practised the art were called *ureueng meukulat*. The most powerful *kulat* of this description was known as *kulat Lam Teuha*, from the name of the locality where it was to be found in the greatest abundance; while the poison-makers of Lam Teuba were "also regarded as the best." Much skill was required to seek out the right kinds, to "charm" (*rajah*) them in the desired sense, and to work the charm when prepared. Thus, to cause an enemy a wound on the leg, the practitioner transfixed a toadstool with a needle and then secretly mixed a portion of it with his food.

## The Logic of Sympathetic Magic

Other manipulations of the *kulat* were employed to engender passion for a lover, hatred against a spouse, madness, or even death. As with all Achehnese magical practice, the virtue of the preparation lay not so much in the physical properties of the substance as in the incantations pronounced over it. A poison owed its activity chiefly to the magic incantations pronounced over it, or to the witchcraft required for its preparation; and its force might in like manner be broken by an expert with the aid of certain definite prayers (*tangkay*). As an antidote to the ills resulting from *kulat*, there was no better remedy than *kulat* itself — but it was a remedy most difficult to procure.

## Lam Teuba in Achehnese Popular Geography

The reputation of Lam Teuba thus placed the village alongside *kulat Lam Teuha* as a recognised source of dangerous magical expertise. Such local specialisations were characteristic of the Achehnese countryside, in which individual gampongs were habitually identified with particular crafts, charms, or forms of knowledge — from the cloth of [[Lam Gut|Lam Gugob]] to the epidemic-averting *lanchang* launched from the [[Kuala (of the Aceh River)|kuala]] of the Aceh river. Snouck Hurgronje's notice of Lam Teuba is brief, but it suffices to show that the village enjoyed, in the late nineteenth century, a sinister pre-eminence in the arts of sympathetic poisoning.

## Lam-Teuba in the eye survey of [[1937]]

In Dr. [[C. E. Gomperts]]'s [[Oogheelkundig kampongonderzoek in Groot-Atjeh (Maart-December 1937)|eye survey of 1937]], Lam-Teuba appears as one of only two *moekims* of [[Groot-Atjeh]] possessing irrigation from mountain streams, and consequently as the strongest test of the theory that *[[Xerophthalmie|xerophthalmie]]* was simply a disease of food shortage.

### Irrigation and the double harvest

Since there was no artificial irrigation of the *[[Sawah|sawah]]* fields in Groot-Atjeh and cultivators depended on rainfall, the long drought of [[1936]] and [[1937]] caused the *paddi* planting to fail — "met uitzondering van den aanplant in [[VI Moekims Lhong]] en Moekim Lam-Teuba, alwaar de sawahbevloeiing plaats vindt door middel van bergstroomen die ook in de droge tijden voldoende water leveren." In Lam-Teuba the planting and harvest took place twice a year; in the rest of Groot-Atjeh only once. The *moekim* accordingly had "ruim voldoende voedsel (rijst) voor de bevolking."

### The test of the dietary theory

Gomperts found that the percentage of xerophthalmia cases in Lam-Teuba was "vrijwel gelijk" to that in the valley *moekims* [[Indrapoeri]], [[Seulimeum]] and [[Lam-Kabeuë]], where the rice harvest had been less favourable — one of three comparisons on which he rested his conclusion that nutrition alone could not solve the xerophthalmia question.

Against [[J. Tijssen|Tijssen]]'s contrary proposition that xerophthalmia occurred especially "in rijk voedselgebied, rijstovervloed in Atjeh," Gomperts set the Lam-Teuba figures directly: 4 cases among 290 children examined, or 1½ per cent.
