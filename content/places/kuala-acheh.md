The mouth of the [[Acheh]] river (Krueng [[Acheh]]), where the great saint [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel]] (known as Teungku di Kuala) is buried. His tomb is described as the most sacred in the whole country after that of [[Teungku Anjong]]. The saint took his name from the fact that he is buried near the Kuala or mouth of the [[Acheh]] river. In the [[Hikajat Malém Dagang|Hikayat Malem Dagang]], the magic tree destined by [[Si Ujut]] for a warship comes drifting from the opposite coast to Kuala [[Acheh]] and remains there quietly. In the war with the Dutch, a severe conflict took place near the tomb of Teungku di Kuala. A crocodile of [[Teungku Anjong]], marked by his master with a band of aren-rope (taloe jo') around its neck, still keeps watch in Kuala [[Acheh]], taking care that no noxious crocodiles enter the river. The [[Acheh]] River (Krueng [[Acheh]]) The principal river of [[Acheh]] Proper, running through the capital. The tomb of Teungku di Kuala ([[Abdurra'uf of Singkel]]) is situated near its mouth (Kuala [[Acheh]]). Near the tomb, the waters are said to be haunted by a gigantic skate (parbe) which wrecks the vessels of the wicked, especially those who have failed to honour the great saint of [[Acheh]]. The river also marks an important geographic boundary between different territorial units.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Kuala (of the Aceh River)

The **Kuala** — literally the "river-mouth" — of the [[Acheh River|Aceh River]] is the estuary through which the principal watercourse of [[Great Acheh]] discharges into the Strait of Malacca to the north of [[Banda Acheh]]. In *The Achehnese* it appears not as a settlement but as a hydrographic and ritual feature: the threshold between the inland political heart of the kingdom and the open sea, and the place at which one of the most picturesque of the old preventive rites against epidemics was concluded.

### The Epidemic Lanchang

Snouck Hurgronje describes how, in earlier times, an Achehnese sultan would command the construction of a great *lanchang* (a raft or small craft) of wood or bamboo, furnished with a quantity of rice and meat, and accompanied by a pretended human sacrifice. Some poor man, willing to play the part of victim for a few dollars, was placed on board and set adrift to drift down the Aceh river and out of the *kuala*. At the river's mouth he would contrive to regain the shore, leaving the rice and meat to their strife with the waves. The ceremony, known as [[Both lanchang (setting afloat of a lanchang)|both lanchang]] ("setting afloat of a lanchang"), was employed "so to speak officially" in Acheh to combat threatened outbreaks of pestilence such as cholera (*ta'un*) and small-pox (*gluén*).

The ritual logic was continuous with the wider Achehnese belief that epidemic disease originated in the battles of the *jins* (jinn), and that propitiatory offerings could deflect their malign influence. Smaller *lanchangs* on a domestic scale were also set afloat in cases of individual illness, but only the Sultan possessed the authority to launch the great state ceremony that drifted out past the *kuala* and into the open sea.

### Topographical Setting

The Achehnese themselves liken the shape of their country to that of a *jeungki* (winnowing basket): the debouchment of the Aceh river — the *kuala Achèh* — suggesting to their fancy the somewhat sharply pointed mouth of the winnower, "whence all unclean particles adhering to the husked rice are shaken out." Proceeding downstream from the interior, one passed on the left the territory of the [[XXV Mukims]] and on the right that of the [[XXVI Mukims]], the intervening belt widening towards the coast. The *kuala* itself thus lay at the seaward end of a richly populated lowland corridor that had at its apex the [[Dalam]] — the Sultan's residence — and the cluster of prosperous gampongs collectively known as [[Banda Acheh]].

### Religious Geography

The mouth of the Aceh river also figured in the spiritual topography of the country. The most venerated tomb in pre-war Acheh, that of [[Teungku Anjong]] in [[Gampong Jawa]], had in earlier times been rivalled in popular devotion by that of [[Teungku di Kuala]] — better known as Abdora'oh, or [[Abdurra'uf of Singkel]] — whose very name perpetuated the sacred association of the *kuala* with the saintly tradition of the coast.
