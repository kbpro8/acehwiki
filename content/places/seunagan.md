The area on the West Coast where Habib Seunagan carried out his teaching of unorthodox mysticism. He derived his name from 'the scene of his labours on the West Coast to the South of [[Meulaboh]].' Before attaining celebrity he was known as Teungku [[Peunado]] after the gampong in [[Pidie]] where he was born. His teaching included disseminating the ideas of [[Hamzah Pansuri]]. 'In Seunagan one Teungku di Krueng (ob. [[1902]]) may be considered as the spiritual successor of Habib Seunagan.'

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — West Coast (of Acheh)

The **West Coast** of [[Acheh|Acheh]] is one of the two great coast-dependencies of the sultanate, the other being the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]]. It is separated from the rest of Acheh by the wild *rantos* districts — inhospitable tracts in which the larger settlements lie embedded — and was historically the principal field of labour of the Achehnese pepper-planters, who established colonies there and remained sometimes for years at a time in a society where there were no women.

### The rantos and the planters

The rantos are described by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as "the wild and inhospitable districts on the West Coast which separate the larger settlements from one another and are the field of the pepper-planters' labours." In these rantos nothing could be obtained during the south-west monsoon; only the interval between monsoons — the so-called *keunong* 5–17 — made it safe to sail from the capital to the West Coast, and during the rest of the year the sea was closed to Achehnese vessels. The West Coast was thus, in respect of communication, almost a separate country from [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]]; its trade and its population were shaped by the alternation of the monsoons.

The pepper-planters who colonized the West Coast came, as a rule, from the interior of Acheh. Their kanduri-cycle — the *kanduri bungong lada*, the annual feast held when the pepper blossoms — was celebrated separately in the house of each pepper-planter, since the pepper-plants do not all blossom at the same time; the period of these kanduris lasted as long as three months. The feast consisted of glutinous rice and its accessories, and was consecrated with a prayer by a *teungku*. Wednesday was *pantang*, or forbidden by the adat, for pepper-planting as well as for rice-cultivation on the West Coast. The sixth day was especially lucky for agricultural labour, unless it happened to fall on a Friday.

### The viciousness of the colonies

The Achehnese colonists on the East and West Coasts who lived there sometimes for years at a time in a society where there were no women developed, in Snouck's account, every vice of the nation to its highest pitch. The true highlanders are reputed — not indeed more virtuous, for with them theft and robbery are the order of the day — to be less weak and effeminate than the lowlanders; but among the coast colonists opium, drink and unnatural crime exercised an influence far greater than in the upper country. On the West Coast especially, the practice of smoking opium in company still prevailed in Snouck's day, marked by some characteristic customs: the votaries of the habit sat together in a prescribed position, the pipe passed round, and each in his turn had to take two pulls so strong that he could not refrain from a deep groan.

### Foreign admixture

The West Coast has received, more than any other part of Acheh, the impress of foreign admixture. The contemptuous appellation *aneu' Jamee* ("descendants of strangers or guests") and *aneu' Rawa* ("people from the province of Rawa"), to which the epithet "tailed" (*meukeutèë*) is also added, is applied to the people of mixed descent on the West Coast. The term *Mante* — the name of an aboriginal people imagined by the Achehnese — is also used in the lowlands as a nickname for the less-civilized highlanders, and is applied in the same sense to the people of mixed descent on the West Coast.

### Place-names and political chiefs

The word *tandil* — a Tamil word originally meaning a collector of moneys, but among the Malays denoting the headman of a gang of coolies or of the crew of a boat — survives as an hereditary title of certain chiefs in the highland districts of the West Coast. The coastal towns of the West Coast — among which [[Daya|Daya]], [[Trumon|Trumon]], [[Susoh|Susoh]], [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]], [[Tapa Tuan|Tapa Tuan]] and others are named in *The Achehnese* — were the seats of uleebalangs whose authority was, in the eyes of the chiefs of [[Acheh proper|Acheh proper]], only that of *keuchis* or *mantris* magnified by royal favour or by their own arrogance. The West Coast was, however, the theatre of important events in the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]]: the Hasan-Husain feast, for instance, is still celebrated to this day in Trumon in the manner customary in the Deccan.
