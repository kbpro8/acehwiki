**Aru** — the historic port-kingdom on the north-east coast of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], in the region of modern **Deli** (around modern Medan) — was the **wingewest** (tributary province) whose possession was contested between [[Acheh|Atjeh]] and [[Djohor|Djohor]] throughout the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, and whose transfer from Atjeh to Djohor by the [[1587]] peace between Atjeh and the Portuguese defined the strategic framework of the Atjeh-Djohor rivalry that frames Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The 1587 transfer

The [[1587]] peace between Atjeh and the Portuguese transferred Aru from Atjeh to Djohor. The transfer was the principal diplomatic outcome of the peace and the proximate cause of the renewed Atjeh-Djohor hostility that would frame the rest of the sixteenth century and the early seventeenth. Van der Woude notes that "de vijandschap tusschen Atjeh, een van de machtigste Staten op Sumatra, en Djohor, dat bij den vrede tusschen de Portugeezen en Atjeh in 1587 het wingewest Aru had ontvangen, was niet geluwd". The sultan of Atjeh, in [[1598]], sent envoys to [[Goa|Goa]] asking Portuguese help against Djohor; the Portuguese received the envoys with honours at Malaka in May [[1599]] and at Goa, but the help was not forthcoming.

## The 1539 Atjeh-Aru war

The 1587 transfer was itself the consequence of an earlier war. In [[1539]], Atjeh recaptured Aru from Djohor — "drie jaar later heroverde Atjeh het wingewest Aru op den sultan van Djohor" — and developed itself into a formidable naval power. The Atjeh-Aru war of [[1539]] was the principal sixteenth-century instance of the Atjeh-Djohor contest for the north-east Sumatran coast, and the proximate cause of the Atjeh naval rise that would make the sultanate the chief Muslim rival of the Portuguese in the Strait of Malaka.

## The strategic position

Aru's strategic position — on the north-east coast of Sumatra, commanding the approaches to the Strait of Malaka from the west and the approaches to the pepper ports of the Atjeh coast from the east — made it a natural object of contest between the two chief Muslim powers of the region. The kingdom's possession gave its holder a forward base against the other's pepper ports and a strategic depth against Portuguese attack from Malaka. The Atjeh sultans sought Aru as a buffer against Djohor and the Portuguese; the Djohor sultans sought Aru as a base for the recovery of Malaka. The 1587 transfer thus defined the strategic framework within which the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s European policy — the playing-off of the Dutch, English, French and Portuguese against each other — would unfold.

## Significance

Aru is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal **wingewest** of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries and the principal object of contest between Atjeh and [[Djohor|Djohor]] throughout the period. The 1587 transfer of Aru from Atjeh to Djohor — by the peace between Atjeh and the Portuguese — defined the strategic framework of the Atjeh-Djohor rivalry that frames Van der Woude's Atjeh chapter, and the 1539 Atjeh-Aru war was the principal sixteenth-century instance of the Atjeh-Djohor contest for the north-east Sumatran coast. Aru's strategic position — on the north-east coast of Sumatra, commanding the approaches to the Strait of Malaka — made its possession a strategic necessity for both Atjeh and Djohor, and the contest for Aru defined the strategic framework within which the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s European policy would unfold.
