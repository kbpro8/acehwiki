**Kota Podjoet** was the high Dutch observation post on the western side of the [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] perimeter, whose lookouts spotted enemy *benteng* (fortification) construction for the *zuider-face* battery and signalled range and direction by horn signals to correct the artillery fire. It was also the site of the principal Dutch military cemetery of the occupation, to which the dead of K. Radja were daily carried on "ominous little wagons" drawn by chain-gang convicts through the interior of the Kraton — a practice Borel singles out as injurious beyond measure to the morale of the living.

## The observatory

The height of Kota Podjoet gave the Dutch a wide view over the kampongs to the south and west — [[Soerian]], [[Poe Oe]], [[Setee]], [[Mandarsa Poeti]] and the *sawah* (irrigated rice fields) stretching toward [[Bital]] and [[Ketapang Doewa]]. On [[29 August]] [[1874]] the post discovered the enemy building a *benteng* at short distance to the south, already nearly complete; range and direction were signalled to the new *zuider-face* battery, the first shots were corrected by horn signals, and the shells soon fell inside the work — the Atjehneezen were seen fleeing "head over heels," abandoning guns and tools. The post was also the look-out that on [[16 May]] [[1874]] was "ernstig bedreigd" (seriously threatened) during a heavy enemy attack on the eastern face of the Kraton, when the entire *ooster-face* was "voortdurend hevig beschoten."

## The military cemetery

Kota Podjoet became the burial-ground of the Dutch garrison from [[1874]] onward. Borel's description of the daily passage of the dead through the Kraton is one of the most striking passages of the book. The dead, "simply wrapped in sheet," were carried on a wheeled stretcher drawn by two *kettinggangers* (chain-gang convicts) from the hospital barracks inside the Kraton to the cemetery at Kota Podjoet; "these ominous little wagons had to go inside the parapet, past barracks." Borel insists that the constant visible passage of the dead, especially during the [[Crisis at Kota Radja (May–June 1874)|May–June 1874 crisis]] and the [[Cholera catastrophe at Kota Radja (February 1875)|February 1875 cholera catastrophe]] — when the dead averaged 15–18 per day, with peaks of 25 or 30 — was a moral injury to the garrison beyond the bare statistics of mortality.
