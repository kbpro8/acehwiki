**Netherlands India** (also written **Netherlands-India**, in Dutch *Nederlandsch-Indië*, and known colloquially as the Dutch East Indies) was the colonial territory governed from Batavia by the [[Governor Van Heutsz|Governor-General]] of the Netherlands. In *The Achehnese*, Vol. I, it appears both as the administrative framework within which [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] wrote — the title-page of the original Dutch work styles him "Adviser for Native Affairs, Netherlands India" — and as a comparative field against which Achehnese institutions, customs and religious life are measured.

The Introduction narrates that in [[1891]] Snouck Hurgronje "proceeded to Acheh in pursuance of instructions from the Netherlands-India Government to make a special study of the religious element in the political conditions of that country." The same Introduction notes the practical difficulties of completing the English translation: the translator [[A.W.S. O'Sullivan|O'Sullivan]] "in the Straits Settlements" and the author "in Netherlands India" were both "wrapped up in official duties." After the abolition of the Achehnese royal authority and the death of the last [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan]], the Netherlands India Government came forward as his successor and claimed the allegiance of the chiefs of the dependent kingdoms.

As a comparative field, Netherlands India is invoked throughout Vol. I when the author wishes to show that an Achehnese usage is matched, modified or contradicted elsewhere in the Archipelago. The Hasan-Husain festival, for example, "in different parts of Netherlands-India and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra (Padang, Bencoolen etc.) ... is celebrated on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the Qanoon-e-islam" of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Ja'far Shurreef]]. The Mohammedans of the Deccan, whose manners are portrayed in that work, are Shafi'ites like those of the Malay Archipelago; and a comparison of Shurreef's book with what is "actually found in Netherlands-India" shows that Shi'ite-influenced adventitious additions to the creed were adopted by Malay and Javanese converts with the same readiness as the fundamental truths of Shafi'ite law.

The country is also the locus of administrative practices against which Achehnese conditions are contrasted — for instance, the extension of the word *mukim* in Penang and Province Wellesley, where "the English Government has adopted the word to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes," and where in British territory it has a stricter sense than the corresponding Achehnese usage.
