**Netherlands India** (also written **Netherlands-India**, in Dutch *Nederlandsch-Indië*, and known colloquially as the Dutch East Indies) was the colonial territory governed from Batavia by the [[Governor Van Heutsz|Governor-General]] of the Netherlands. In *The Achehnese*, Vol. I, it appears both as the administrative framework within which [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] wrote — the title-page of the original Dutch work styles him "Adviser for Native Affairs, Netherlands India" — and as a comparative field against which Achehnese institutions, customs and religious life are measured.

The Introduction narrates that in [[1891]] Snouck Hurgronje "proceeded to Acheh in pursuance of instructions from the Netherlands-India Government to make a special study of the religious element in the political conditions of that country." The same Introduction notes the practical difficulties of completing the English translation: the translator [[A.W.S. O'Sullivan|O'Sullivan]] "in the Straits Settlements" and the author "in Netherlands India" were both "wrapped up in official duties." After the abolition of the Achehnese royal authority and the death of the last [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan]], the Netherlands India Government came forward as his successor and claimed the allegiance of the chiefs of the dependent kingdoms.

As a comparative field, Netherlands India is invoked throughout Vol. I when the author wishes to show that an Achehnese usage is matched, modified or contradicted elsewhere in the Archipelago. The Hasan-Husain festival, for example, "in different parts of Netherlands-India and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra (Padang, Bencoolen etc.) ... is celebrated on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the Qanoon-e-islam" of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Ja'far Shurreef]]. The Mohammedans of the Deccan, whose manners are portrayed in that work, are Shafi'ites like those of the Malay Archipelago; and a comparison of Shurreef's book with what is "actually found in Netherlands-India" shows that Shi'ite-influenced adventitious additions to the creed were adopted by Malay and Javanese converts with the same readiness as the fundamental truths of Shafi'ite law.

The country is also the locus of administrative practices against which Achehnese conditions are contrasted — for instance, the extension of the word *mukim* in Penang and Province Wellesley, where "the English Government has adopted the word to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes," and where in British territory it has a stricter sense than the corresponding Achehnese usage.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Netherlands India

**Netherlands India** (Dutch **Nederlandsch-Indië**; the **Dutch East Indies**) was the colonial state through which the Kingdom of the Netherlands administered its possessions in the East Indian Archipelago after their restoration in [[1816]]. In P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819*, the entire narrative turns upon the friction between Netherlands India and [[British India]] in the years from the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|treaty of 1814]] to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 17 March 1824]] and beyond.

## Restoration and friction with the British

Van der Kemp dates the modern troubles from "the restoration of the Dutch authority in the East Indian archipelago," which he elsewhere calls "het herstel van het Nederlandsch gezag." The British "saw us return to [[Hindostan]] with displeasure"; from [[Bencoolen|Benkoelen]], [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] intrigued against Dutch authority on [[Sumatra]] and farther in the Archipelago. The British-Indian officials of [[Bengal|Bengalen]] urged their Dutch counterparts to give up the Hindostan factories in exchange for compensation in the Archipelago; and since these factories "appeared to us no longer worth anything," and "an undivided authority on Sumatra offered the opportunity to make of this island a second [[Java]]," the Dutch government was amenable. [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] and [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] had agreed already in 1814 on the worthlessness of the Hindostan possessions, and [[Elout|Commissioner-General Elout]] in February [[1817]] recommended taking Bencoolen in exchange — the foundation of the revision of the treaty of 1814 that culminated in the 1824 instrument.

## Sphere-of-influence diplomacy

The central object of Dutch policy was, in Van der Kemp's phrase, "to obtain the abandonment of all English influence on Sumatra." The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 17 March 1824]] accordingly placed Sumatra (called by the British "Java minor") under exclusive Dutch influence and assigned the [[Malay Peninsula]] to the British sphere (article 9). It also stipulated (article 12) that the British would establish no office on the [[Karimon Islands|Carimons]], [[Batam|Battam]], [[Bintan|Bintang]], [[Lingga|Lingen]] or any of the islands south of [[Singapore]]. In exchange the Dutch surrendered "Nederlandsch Hindostan, [[Malacca]], Singapore, and one million guilders" — Van der Kemp's lament for "kostbaar Malakka." The Dutch plenipotentiaries' solemn disclaimer of any design upon "political supremacy or commercial monopoly in the Eastern Archipelago" was, Van der Kemp notes acidly, an empty formula repeated from the earlier Castlereagh-Canning notes of 4 August [[1820]].

## The "dead letter" of 1819 and the paper war of the 1820s

The [[Acheh treaty of 1819|Acheh treaty]] of April [[1819]], concluded by Raffles with Sultan [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]], was for Netherlands India a [[dead letter]] — yet it was "revived" in the 1824 treaty as an obstacle to Dutch supremacy over Acheh. Van der Kemp traces the long [[Paper war|paper war]] of the 1820s, in which the [[Pinang Nota (1825)|Pinang Nota of 1825]] and [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minutes pressed the British case, until the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra treaty of 1871]] finally gave the Dutch a free hand on the island in exchange for the [[Cape of Guinea cession (1871)|cession of the Dutch Gold Coast]]. [[Lord Palmerston|Palmerston]], as E. de Waal recalled, "attached himself simply to the letter of the treaty of 1824"; the differential duties and coasting regulations of Netherlands India furnished the steady grievance. By the late 1820s the salt-price reduction and the [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] had transformed the rivalry into a contest for the trade of [[Sumatra's East Coast|Sumatra's East Coast]] and the [[Siak]] dependencies, while the [[Java War (1825-1830)|Java War]] of 1825-1830 — [[Dipo Negoro|Dipo Negoro]]'s revolt — preoccupied the Batavia government's military resources.

## The lessons of history

Van der Kemp's concluding pages draw the wider moral that "the political complications with England or its servants since the restoration of the Dutch authority in the East Indian archipelago brought to mind many a time how the same questions, the same manners, the same ways of speaking on the English side recur in the study of history." He draws explicit comparison between [[Raffles' raid on Palembang (1819)|Raffles's raid on Palembang]] in 1819 and the [[Jameson's raid comparison|Jameson raid]] on the [[Transvaal]] a century later — an analogy that frames Netherlands India's experience as one chapter in a recurring pattern of British frontier encroachment against colonial spheres of influence.


## Account from Keuchenius's 1905 letter — Nederlandsch-Indië and colonial economic reform

**Nederlandsch-Indië** is also the overarching context for the [[Submission of Aceh|submission of Aceh]] and the broader economic and administrative reforms proposed in the [[1905]] letter by [[Keuchenius|W. H. J. Keuchenius]] to Governor-General [[J. B. van Heutsz]]. Aceh was a part of the Dutch East Indies, though it retained a distinct political and cultural identity. The successful campaign to pacify Aceh, as championed by Van Heutsz, was a significant event in the history of the colony. Keuchenius, in his letter, argues that the economic health of the entire colony of Nederlandsch-Indië depends on reforms in trade representation, the private sector, and the Chinese question, placing Aceh's pacification as a precedent for a broader colonial revival — see [[Kracht van wapenen]].
