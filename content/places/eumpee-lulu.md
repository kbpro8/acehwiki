A mountain that rises from the sea close to the coast of Lam Pu'ue' in the IV Mukims of the XXV. It is personified by popular superstition. The legend holds that an old woman named Grandmother Lulu, attacked by a raging thirst, caused her husband and neighbours to drain every spring and well for miles around. Still unable to slake her thirst, she prayed to God; rain fell in torrents, she drank it all, walked into the sea, and was transformed into this mountain. A noise like growling thunder is always heard there; at times the Grandmother shakes the mountain so it trembles. Her descendants still survive, and among them is always a woman who commands the art of making rain. In times of drought she goes to the sea, falls into a frenzy, tries to rush into the sea, is held back by force, and gives commands that produce rain.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — IV Mukims (of XXV)

The **IV Mukims of the XXV** — the "Four Mukims of the Twenty-Five" — is a territorial division of the lowland sagi of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]] of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]], repeatedly named by Snouck Hurgronje in connection with the cultivation of sugar-cane and the rain-making superstition of the Achehnese coast.

### The IV Mukims as a Wakeueh-District

The IV Mukims of the XXV is reckoned among the wakeueh-districts of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]] — that is, among the smaller territories severed by the earlier sultans from the control of the great uleebalangs and placed directly under the sultan's own rule. The institution of wakeueh, Snouck explains, was a means adopted by the earlier sultans for strengthening their internal authority. The sultans succeeded in getting the ruling chiefs both in [[Acheh Proper|Acheh proper]] and the various dependencies to consent to a certain portion of their territories — usually three mukims — being severed from their control. The chief or chiefs of these smaller districts were thus brought directly under the sultan's rule and withdrawn from that of the uleebalangs. In the XXV Mukims, the IV Mukims is a slightly larger wakeueh-district of four mukims, and its chiefs enjoyed a corresponding independence of the [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem of the XXV]].

### Sugar-Cane and Molasses

The IV Mukims is named by Snouck as one of the three districts of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]] in which the cultivation of sugar-cane for molasses was most extensively carried on. "Molasses is chiefly manufactured in the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]], the [[VII Mukims Bueng|VII Mukims Bu'ëng]] and the IV Mukims, since in these places the cane is of inferior quality." The cane-gardens of these districts were manured with cow-dung, the same preventive being adopted against disease in the cane as against disease in the padi — viz. the spreading of burnt cow-dung (*abee*) over the field. The inferior quality of the cane in the IV Mukims, which made it unsuitable for direct consumption but adequate for boiling down into molasses, is the kind of detail that fixes the IV Mukims as a lowland, well-watered, and relatively densely settled district of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV]].

### The Pula Method of Padi-Cultivation

The same IV Mukims appears in the agricultural chapter as one of the districts in which the *pula* (transplanting) method of padi-cultivation is practised, with certain modifications in swampy districts such as the [[VII Mukims Bueng|VII Mukims Bu'ëng]]. The *pula* method, in which the padi is first sown in a seed-bed and then transplanted to the flooded field, was characteristic of the more careful husbandry of the lowland sagis, and its practice in the IV Mukims is consistent with the picture of a well-cultivated and densely settled lowland district.

### The Mountain on the Coast

A footnote in the chapter on the kawoms names **Gunong Lhee** (Eumpèe Luluh) as "a mountain on the coast of the IV Mukims which plays a part in the rain-making superstition." The mountain is thus the principal topographical feature of the IV Mukims, and the rain-making observance attached to it is one of the survivals of the older local cults of the lowland coast. The IV Mukims is accordingly a district in which the political and agricultural importance of the wakeueh-land is combined with a marked topographical character and a surviving body of popular superstition.

### Relation to Other "Four Mukims"

The generic name "IV Mukims" is also borne by the [[IV Mukims Ateue|IV Mukims Ateue]] of the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI]] and (in a wider confederation) by the [[IV Mukims of the VII of the XXVI|IV Mukims of the VII of the XXVI]]. The fact that three different districts of Great Acheh could each be called simply "the IV Mukims" reflects the Achehnese convention by which the smaller wakeueh-districts and territorial fractions were identified by their numerical designation alone.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — IV Mukims

The **IV Mukims** — the "Four Mukims" — is the name borne by more than one uleebalangship in [[Great Acheh]]. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje mentions the IV Mukims chiefly in two contexts: the [[IV Mukims (of XXV)|IV Mukims of the XXV Mukims]] and the [[IV Mukims Ateue|IV Mukims Ateue]] of the [[XXVI Mukims]]. Both were known simply as "the IV Mukims" in Achehnese parlance, in contrast to three-mukim districts such as [[Lhee Mukim Lam Rabo]] or [[Lhee Mukim Kayee Adang]].

### The IV Mukims of the XXV

The IV Mukims of the [[XXV Mukims]] is mentioned as one of the principal districts where sugar-cane is cultivated for molasses. "Molasses is chiefly manufactured in the [[XXII Mukims]], the [[VII Mukims Bueng|VII Mukims Bu'ëng]] and the IV Mukims, since in these places the cane is of inferior quality." Cane-gardens in these districts are manured with cow-dung, the same preventive being adopted against disease in the cane as against disease in the padi, viz. the spreading of burnt cow-dung (*abee*) over the field. The same IV Mukims also appears in the agricultural chapter as one of the districts where the *pula* (transplanting) method of padi-cultivation is practised, "with certain modifications in swampy districts such as the [[VII Mukims Bueng]]."

### Eumpèe Luluh and the Rain-Making Superstition

A footnote in the chapter on the kawoms records that "a number of smaller kawoms" have left traces in local superstitions. "Compare [[Ja Karieng|Ja Kariéng]], a dreaded tree on [[Pulo Lam Puyang]], the subject of many vows; **Eumpèe Luluh**, a mountain on the coast of the IV Mukims which plays a part in the rain-making superstition; [[Eumpèe Blieng]], a holy well in the [[IX Mukims]], which also helps to cause rain, etc." This mountain is one of the natural features that the Achehnese consulted as omens for the monsoon and the rains, alongside the Pleiades and the *keunong* cycle.

### Relation to Other "Four Mukims"

The generic name "IV Mukims" is also borne by the [[IV Mukims Ateue]] of the [[XXVI Mukims]] and (in a wider confederation) by the [[IV Mukims of the VII of the XXVI]], the latter being a subdivision of the seven-mukim uleebalangship of the [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI]]. The fact that three different districts of Great Acheh could each be called simply "the IV Mukims" reflects the Achehnese convention that the smaller wakeuëh-districts and territorial fractions could be identified by their numerical designation alone, without further qualification.
