**Gajo** — also spelled *Gayo* — refers both to the ethnic group and to the highland region they inhabit in the interior of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]], a mountainous country separated from the populated lowland districts of the residency by a vast ring of primeval forest (Dutch: *oerbos*) that, in the early twentieth century, was opened to colonial administration by the construction of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]]. The Gajo are noted in the colonial sources for their dark-skinned inhabitants, distinctive clothing, and stilt-house villages; in the early twentieth century the Gajo population accounted for about 5 % of the *gewest*'s inhabitants, and by the [[1920s]] they numbered roughly 50,000. Their country — the [[Gajoland|Gajolanden]] — was the strategic depth of the Acehnese resistance in the closing phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and, after the [[1904]] campaigns of [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[e-c-van-daalen|Van Daalen]], a frontier of the colonial plantation economy.

## Demography

In the [[1920s]] the Gajo of the central highlands numbered approximately 50,000, representing about 5 % of the population of [[Aceh|Aceh]]. The figure, recorded in the source *DLP-84-Atjeh* and in the wider colonial demographic literature, places the Gajo as the principal highland people of the residency (Dutch: *gewest*) and as the demographic core of the [[Gajoland|Gajolanden]] — the highland region that, together with the [[Alasland|Alaslanden]], formed the central massif of northern Sumatra. The Gajo population was concentrated in the river valleys and upland plains around [[Takengen|Takengen]] and [[Blang Kedjeren|Blang Kedjeren]], where the cooler climate and the fertile volcanic soils supported a denser settlement than the lowland fringes. The demographic weight of the Gajo, though modest in absolute terms, was significant in the context of the residency's population, which was concentrated in the coastal lowlands and which had been thinned by the protracted warfare of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]].

## Settlement and Architecture

Gajo villages, such as [[Gajodorp|Gajodorp]], were characterised by tightly packed houses built on stilts, each with a gallery (Dutch: *galerie*) and a thatched roof. The houses, constructed on piles and roofed with straw, reflected the communal living patterns typical of the Gajo highlands, and the layout of the village — with its houses clustered around a central open space — embodied the social organisation of a people whose political structure was less hierarchical than that of the lowland Acehnese. The stilt construction kept the houses above the damp ground of the highland valleys, and the gallery served as the principal social space of the household — the place where visitors were received, where the family gathered, and where the daily work of weaving and food preparation was carried on. The thatched roof, of alang-alang grass (Dutch: *alang-alang*) or of attap, was the standard roofing of the highland house and survived, in many villages, well into the late colonial period.

## Cultural Characteristics and Economy

The Gajo wore dark, self-woven garments and silver ornaments — *silvery necklaces* and *handbags* — that distinguished them from the lowland Acehnese, whose dress was more varied and more subject to the influence of European textiles. Imported Western clothing was, in the early twentieth century, gradually replacing the traditional dress, a process accelerated by the opening of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] and the consequent intensification of contact with the colonial economy. The Gajo economy, in the pre-colonial period, was based on subsistence agriculture and shifting cultivation (Dutch: *ladang*); after the construction of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]], the Gajo increasingly participated in plantation labour for the rubber and oil-palm estates introduced into the highland fringes, and in the smallholder cultivation of coffee and copra that the colonial market rewarded. The integration of the Gajo into the colonial economy, however, was slower and less complete than that of the lowland population, and the highland country retained, throughout the colonial period, much of its pre-colonial character.
