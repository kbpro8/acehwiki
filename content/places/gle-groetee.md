**Glé Groetèë** — the caves of the Glé Groetèë — is a cave system on the [[Westkust (of Atjeh)|West Coast of Aceh]] in which the resistance fighters of the [[Verzet in Lhong]] — after breaking out of the surrounded house at [[Lhong]] — entrenched themselves to fight their last battle there. In *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]), the Glé Groetèë is the place of the annihilation: the place where the colonial army decided on annihilation and carried out the decision, leaving "eight holy graves, on which the white pennants would soon rise as erected signs."

## The entrenched in the caves

After a sharp pursuit by the marechaussée, the band fortified itself in one of the caves of the Glé Groetèë to fight its last battle there. The entrenchment in the caves — the resistance fighters' choice of the cave as their last defensive position — is the book's marker of their paradise-death: the men who had broken out of the Lhong house and escaped the klewang-fight chose the cave as the place where they would fight to the utmost.

## The annihilation

Annihilation was decided upon, and the decision was carried out. When everything was over, the population was given the opportunity — in accordance with the regulations — to bury its fallen. Eight holy graves, on which the white pennants would soon rise as erected signs.

The eight holy graves — the eight holy graves of the annihilated resistance fighters — are the book's marker of the paradise-death: the resistance fighters who were annihilated in the Glé Groetèë achieved the paradise-death they sought, and their graves became holy graves with white pennants. The white pennants — the white pennants on the graves — are the book's marker of the resistance fighters' martyr status: the annihilated men were martyrs of the faith, and their graves were holy graves.

## The book's critique

Afterwards, this outcome was regretted from higher up, because — as it was put — the opportunity for new hatred had thereby been opened. But it was all considered inevitable. But was that so? I think not.

The book's — and [[Musket]]'s — argument is that the annihilation in the Glé Groetèë was not inevitable: the colonial army had failed to use modern methods (tear-gas bombs and gas-masks) that would have taken the opponent alive — and would have prevented the resistance fighters' paradise-death. The Glé Groetèë is therefore the book's marker of the colonial army's failure: the place where the resistance fighters were annihilated — and where, in Musket's argument, they should have been taken alive.

## Significance

The Glé Groetèë is significant in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* as the place of the annihilation of the [[Verzet in Lhong]] resistance fighters. The book's argument — that the annihilation was not inevitable, and that the colonial army had failed to use modern methods that would have prevented the resistance fighters' paradise-death — is the book's clearest example of the inter-war colonial critique of the old-time methods. The eight holy graves with the white pennants are the book's marker of the resistance fighters' martyr status: the annihilated men achieved the paradise-death they sought. The Glé Groetèë is therefore the book's marker of the colonial army's failure and of the resistance fighters' paradise-death.
