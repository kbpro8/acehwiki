A port near the capital of [[Acheh]], the most important harbour. Teuku Nya' Muhamat (the loyal '[[Raja Muda]]' chief) used his efforts to advance the prosperity of the capital and of Ulee Lheue (Olehleh). The death of [[Teuku Asan]] occurred at Ulee Lheue under peculiar circumstances. The soldiers occupying the mosque of Ulee Lheue actually fired the fatal shot, though most Achehnese attributed it to Teuku Ne's followers. [[Teuku Uma]]'s dramatic submission to the Gompeuni included a journey to Ulee Lheue and [[Kutaraja]]. Near Ulee Lheue (the keude or bazaar) is the tomb of [[Tuan di Bunot]] (named because it lies beneath a huge bunot-tree). The tomb of Teungku [[Lam Guha]] ('saint of the cave'), a Javanese from [[Demak]], lies behind the mosque at Ulee Lheue and is now visited by many pilgrims. Also [[Teungku Pante Cheureumen]]'s tomb lies near Kuala Doe on the shore of Ulee Lheue.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Ulee Iheue

**Ulee Iheue** — vulgarly known to Europeans as **Olehleh** — is a coastal place in [[Acheh]] mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the site of the *kanduri la'ot*, the "sea-kanduri" or religious feast of the sea, which was held annually in the month of the *keunong tujoh blaih* (the 7th *keunong* of the Achehnese calendar). It marks, together with the beginning of the *musem barat* or South-West Monsoon, one of the great seasonal turning-points of the Achehnese year.

### The Kanduri La'ot

Snouck Hurgronje introduces Ulee Iheue in his account of the *keunong* cycle — the twenty-eight periods into which the Achehnese agricultural year was divided by the rising of the lunar mansions. Under *keunong* IV, the *keunong tujoh blaih* (17th of Ramadan 1310 = 4th April), he records: "A *kanduri la'ot* (sea-kanduri) takes place in this month at Ulee Iheue (vulg. Olehleh, cf. X below). The beginning of the *musem barat* or S. W. Monsoon also falls within this month, and so does the maximum altitude of the sun at noon (*seunang mata uröë*); it actually occurs on the 5th April."

The sea-kanduri was the ritual counterpart of the agricultural *kanduri blang* ("religious feast of the field") held at the harvest. Whereas the *kanduri blang* marked the close of one agricultural cycle, the *kanduri la'ot* at Ulee Iheue opened the season of the South-West Monsoon — the season in which the Achehnese sailor could most safely venture out upon the sea.

### The Two Sea-Kanduris

Snouck distinguishes the sea-kanduri at Ulee Iheue from a second sea-kanduri held later in the year. Under *keunong* X, the *keunong limböng* (5th of Rabī' al-awwal 1311 = 16th September), he notes: "In this *keunong* the *musem timu* (N. E. Monsoon) commences, and the second division of the seine-fishers (v. sub IV above) celebrate their *kanduri la'ot* or sea kanduri." The seine-fishers of Acheh were thus divided into two bodies, each of which held its own sea-kanduri at the beginning of its working season. Ulee Iheue was the site of the first of these, that of the South-West Monsoon.

### The Coastal Setting

Ulee Iheue's situation on the coast of the [[XXVI Mukims]] made it a natural meeting-place for the seine-fishers of the lowlands of [[Great Acheh]]. Snouck's footnote reference "cf. X below" shows that the place was well known enough to require no further topographical description. The vulgar form **Olehleh** was the name by which the place was known to the Dutch and other Europeans, and it was at Olehleh that the Dutch expedition of [[1873]] first attempted to land in [[Acheh]] — a fact which Snouck does not mention here, but which the reader of *The Achehnese* would have known.

### Significance

The single reference to Ulee Iheue in Vol. I places it among the small set of Achehnese coastal localities whose ritual life was tied to the cycle of the monsoons. Its sea-kanduri, like the *kanduri blang* of the agricultural districts, was one of the ways in which the Achehnese sought to secure by religious observance the bounty of the season that was about to begin.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Ulee Lheue

**Ulee Lheue** (also rendered **Olehheh** or **Olehleh**) is a coastal place in [[Acheh]] in the neighbourhood of the capital, [[Banda Acheh]]. It faces the small island of Pulo Batee ("Stone Island"), from which a porous and easily workable kind of stone was formerly quarried for the chiselled tombstones of the wealthier Achehnese folk. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I Ulee Lheue is principally cited for two features: the oppressive chief-control to which its *pukat*-fishermen were subjected, and the annual *kanduri la'ot* (sea feast) with which the fishermen celebrated the changes of the monsoon.

The stone of Pulo Batee, opposite Ulee Lheue, was employed in former times for chiselled tombstones and for the *pungeu* (pediment) of the posts of Achehnese houses. It is easy to work but not durable. Till quite recently the people of [[Meura'sa]] possessed great skill in cutting these stones, so that the *batée Meura'sa* were in great demand throughout all Acheh. Hewn tombstones are now largely imported from [[Penang]] instead. The place is also the site of the cave of the Daroy, where the Javanese Teungku Lam Guha used to do *l'apa* (religious seclusion) for successive years; his tomb at Ulee Lheue is now an object of veneration.

The *pukat*-fishermen of Ulee Lheue present a characteristic example of the oppressive taxation to which the Achehnese fisheries were subject. They have to deal with at least three chiefs: the uleebalang [[Teuku Ne']], his *banta* [[Teuku Sandang]], and Raja Itam, a son of a deceased Teuku Ne'. These three always enforce their demands for fish and punish defaulters with severity. The fishermen are thus in a state of dependence that leaves them little freedom, and the case is cited as a typical instance of the way in which the *pawang* (fishing-master) may be ground down by his chiefs.

The *kanduri la'ot* — the religious feast of the sea, which is to the *pukat*-fishers what the *kanduri blang* is to the planters — is held when the fishery enjoys a compulsory holiday owing to the rough weather of the changes of the N. E. and S. W. monsoon. The foreshore at Ulee Lheue is divided for the *pukat*-fishermen into two *lho's* (bays): one of which gives its religious feast in *keunong* 17, at the beginning of the S. W. Monsoon (about April), and the other in *keunong* 5, at the beginning of the N. E. Monsoon (about September). This partition of the foreshore into two *lho's*, each holding its own annual *kanduri la'ot*, is one of the regular institutions of the Achehnese fishing-villages, and Ulee Lheue is given as the standard example.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Ulee Lheue

**Ulee Lheue** (vulgarly known to Europeans as **Olehleh**, also written **Ulee Iheue**) was a coastal village or gampong of [[Banda Acheh]], the capital of [[Acheh]]. It lay on the sea-shore to the west of the mouth of the [[Acheh River]], and was one of the chief landing-places and fishing-stations of the capital. As a coastal settlement exposed to the trade of the [[Straits of Malacca|Straits]] and to the alternate monsoons, it figures in *The Achehnese* chiefly in connexion with the ***pukat***-fishery — the great seine fishery of Acheh — and with the annual **kanduri laot** or sea-feast celebrated by its fishermen.

The foreshore at Ulee Lheue was divided for the *pukat*-fishermen into two ***lho'*** (literally "bays"), each marked off by boundary-marks from the other. Each *lho'* held its own annual [[Kanduri la'ot (Sea-kanduri)|kanduri laot]] to invoke God's blessing on the labours of its *pawangs* (fishermen): one in *keunong* 17, at the beginning of the [[S.W. Monsoon|Musem barat]] (about April), and the other in *keunong* 5, at the beginning of the [[N.E. Monsoon|Musem tiniu]] (about September). The feast — at which a buffalo was always slaughtered — was given by the *pawangs* of the *lho'*, who could claim a contribution of about four dollars from each of their contract buyers, and was attended by the *panglima* of the guild, the *uleebalang*, and the gampong authorities (*keuchi's*, *teungkus* and *ureueng tuha*). For seven days after the *kanduri*, no fish might be taken in the waters fronting the *lho'* — a *pantang* prohibition corresponding to that which closes the rice-fields during the growing season.

The *pawangs* of Ulee Lheue were, however, peculiarly burdened by the multiplicity of chiefs who claimed their tribute. They "had to deal with at least three chiefs — the *uleebalang* [[Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa|Teuku Ne']], his *banta* [[Teuku Sandang]], and [[Raja Itam]], a son of a deceased Teuku Ne' — [who] always enforced their demands for fish and punished defaulters with the ban." A *pawang* who failed to set apart a gift suitable to the rank of these chiefs might find his *sampan* and *pukat* "placed under the ban for a month or sometimes even for an unlimited period"; release from the ban required a money present "which may in fact be called a fine." Where the offence was trifling, two *rakans* (followers of the chief) would go down to the market and take from the buyer such quantity of the defaulter's fish as they considered "fair."

Ulee Lheue was also a place of some religious and antiquarian note. Off its shore lay [[Pulo Batee]] ("Stone Island"), whose porous and easily workable stone supplied, in former times, the material for the chiselled tombstones of the wealthier Achehnese. The people of neighbouring [[Meura'sa]] "possessed great skill in cutting these stones, so that the *batie Meura'sa* were in great demand throughout all Acheh." A Javanese whose tomb at Ulee Lheue was, at the time of writing, an object of veneration, was known by the name of [[Teungku Lam Guha]] — "the Teungku of the Cave" — having, it was said, performed *tapa* (religious seclusion) for successive years in the cave at the source of the [[Krueng Daroy|river Daroy]]. A freed Circassian slave, formerly the property of [[Habib Abdurrahman]] (who also imported Hindus as slaves into Acheh), still lived at Ulee Lheue in Snouck Hurgronje's time — a curious relic of the cosmopolitan slave-trade which had once made the capital a meeting-point of races from [[Circassia]] to [[Nias]].
