The **VOC trading post at Aceh** (in Dutch: *factorij te Atjeh*) was a commercial outpost established by the [[Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) in the Sultanate of [[Aceh]] (Atjeh) in the early 17th century. It was intermittently maintained and repeatedly closed due to the "tyranny and bad treatment" by the sultans, reflecting the turbulent relationship between the Company and Aceh.

## Establishment and early years

The first Dutch traders visited Aceh shortly after 1600, but a permanent factorij was not established immediately. By [[1616]], the Company had a factorij there, but it was withdrawn in that year because of the "tyranny and bad treatment" of Sultan [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskander Moeda]] towards Dutch merchants, as recorded in the Company's documents: "overmits de tyrannye ende het quaet tractement" of the sultan.

## Re-establishment and final closure

In [[1620]], the VOC re-established the factorij, but it was lifted again in [[1623]]. After that, the Company maintained only a single assistant (assistent) in Aceh to keep an eye on affairs, rather than a full trading post. The factorij was not revived thereafter, as the VOC preferred to conduct trade through occasional visits and treaties — see [[Dutch–Aceh Treaty of 1641]] and [[Dutch–Aceh Treaty of 1649]] — rather than a permanent presence.

## Trade and competition

The main commodity was pepper, but Aceh also controlled the West Coast of Sumatra, including ports like [[Tiku]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]], and [[Indrapoera]]. The Company's pepper trade was often hindered by Acehnese interference, especially when the sultans claimed sovereignty over areas like [[Indragiri]], where the VOC obtained pepper through its post at [[Djambi]]. The factorij's history illustrates the difficulty the Company faced in maintaining a foothold in a powerful, independent sultanate.

## Significance

The on-again, off-again history of the VOC's factorij at Aceh highlights the strategic importance of Aceh as a source of pepper and as a gatekeeper to the West Coast of Sumatra. It also shows that the Company was unable to impose its will on Aceh through force and had to rely on treaties and concessions to secure its trade. The eventual pattern of maintaining only a representative rather than a full factorij set the stage for Aceh's relative autonomy until the 19th century.
