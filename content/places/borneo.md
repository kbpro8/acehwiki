**Borneo** — the great island known in modern Indonesian as **Kalimantan** — appears in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* only in a passing comparative reference, where it serves [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] as an illustration of the way in which legends of strange peoples are propagated and believed.

In the chapter on the racial composition of the [[Acheh|Achehnese]], Snouck compares the Achehnese traditions concerning the **Mante** (or Mantras) — a legendary earlier population of the country — with what he himself once heard said of the tailed **Uyaks** reputed to exist in Borneo:

> They remind me of what I once heard said of the tailed Uyaks reputed to exist in Borneo; the existence of these people, my informant remarked, appeared quite probable from what one heard of them all over Borneo, but they always seem to live one day further inland from the point reached by the traveller.

The Mante are described in the same passage as a people supposed to go naked and to have the whole of their bodies thickly covered with hair, and believed to inhabit the mountains of the [[XXII Mukims]]. All that the Achehnese know of them is by hearsay: "one here and there will tell you that in his grandfather's time a pair of Mantes, man and wife, were brought captive." Borneo is brought into the comparison because, like the highlands of the XXII Mukims, it is a country supposed to harbour a hidden hairy people whose existence is always affirmed by rumour and never confirmed by sight.

The mention of Borneo in Vol. I is thus incidental — a geographical parallel to a single Achehnese legend. It does, however, place the Achehnese traditions of the Mante within a wider field of Indonesian folk-belief about hairy wild men of the interior, of which the tailed Uyaks of Borneo and the Orang Pendek of the [[Padang|Padang highlands]] are the better-known examples.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Borneo

**Borneo** — the third-largest island in the world, known in modern Indonesian as **Kalimantan** — was, in the early nineteenth century, a vast and only superficially mapped landmass whose coastal sultanates formed part of the wider Malay-Muslim commercial network of the [[Malay Archipelago|Malay archipelago]]. The Dutch had long maintained a presence on its southern and western coasts, while the British, operating from [[Bengal|Bengal]] and [[Penang]], periodically probed for openings. In P. H. van der Kemp's narrative of the [[Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819|Aceh agreement of 1819]] and its long aftermath, Borneo — and in particular its **west coast** — appears as a theatre of Anglo-Dutch rivalry, the site of failed British initiatives and of Dutch commercial countermeasures.

The Borneo episode is introduced through the figure of **Major Farquhar**, Resident of [[Malacca]]. In 1818, Van der Kemp records, Farquhar "was destijds van zijn tocht naar West-Borneo en Lingga teruggekomen" — returned from a voyage to West-Borneo and [[Lingga]]. His expedition had been met with mixed fortunes: at [[Pontianak]] the Dutch commissioner **Van Boekholz** showed him the door, although he succeeded in concluding a contract with the sultan of Lingga. The failure at Pontianak was significant: it demonstrated that the Dutch had returned in force to Borneo's west coast after the Napoleonic interlude and were prepared to defend what they regarded as their sphere. Farquhar's subsequent conviction that the [[Karimon Islands]] offered a viable alternative for British commerce contributed directly to the founding of [[Singapore]] in January 1819.

Van der Kemp returns to Borneo later in the article, in connection with the long-running question of Dutch policy on the island. He notes pointedly that he had argued elsewhere "dat wij er niet naar gestreefd hebben" — that the Dutch had not pursued Borneo with sufficient vigour. The remark is part of his broader critique of Dutch colonial statesmanship in the years between 1816 and 1824: while the diplomats in Europe were busy exchanging [[Hindostan|Nederlandsch Hindostan]] for [[Malacca]] and renouncing British claims on [[Sumatra]], the on-the-ground initiative in the archipelago was passing to the British. Borneo's west coast, with its sultanates of [[Pontianak]] and [[Sukadana]] and its complex ties to [[Siak]] and the east coast of Sumatra, was precisely the kind of indeterminate frontier where British enterprise could flourish if Dutch vigilance relaxed.

The figure who best embodies these entanglements is **Raja Akil** of [[Siak]], the Siak crown-pretender whose career Van der Kemp sketches with reference to **Veth**'s study of Borneo's western afdeeling. In 1828, Van der Kemp notes, Raja Akil "werd vorst van Soekadana op Borneo" — became prince of [[Sukadana]] on Borneo. The career of a single individual moving between Sumatra and Borneo illustrates the deep interconnection of the two islands' political networks, and the porosity of the colonial boundaries that the [[Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 1824]] was meant to formalise. Borneo, in short, was never simply a backdrop; it was an active participant in the diplomacy that produced the Aceh agreement and shaped the colonial order of the nineteenth-century Indies.
