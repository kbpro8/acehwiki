The island of Nias off the West Coast of [[Sumatra]] is noted for its people, who were often enslaved and brought to [[Acheh]]: 'Nias slaves, while others are the offspring of poor Achehnese in the highlands.' The girls trained as sadati performers 'are some of them the best-looking children of Nias slaves.' Achehnese contempt for the Niasese is noted. Also, 'the Achehnese derive their knowledge of the healing properties of the peundang from Nias; it is supposed to have been discovered by the leprous princess banished thither, from whom the whole population of the island is said to be derived.' ## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Nias

**Nias** (Achehnese **Nieh**) is the island off the west coast of [[Sumatra]] from which most of the slaves of [[Acheh]] were drawn, and the chief external reference of the Achehnese slave-system as described in *The Achehnese* Vol. I. The Achehnese slave-traders were until quite recently the terror of Nias and the adjacent islands; the inhabitants were kidnapped in hundreds up to a few years before Snouck Hurgronje wrote, and were still surreptitiously purchased in smaller numbers.

Slaves are a factor of importance in the development of the Achehnese race, and most of these slaves came from Nias. The trade was carried on by slave-traders whose reputation long survived the actual traffic; the people of the island were the victims of an organized kidnapping that continued, in less open form, into the early years of Dutch occupation. The figure of the Niasese slave, male and female, became a familiar one in Achehnese households, and the descendants of such slaves formed a recognised element of the population.

The Achehnese story current as to the origin of the Niasese resembles the Javanese myth of the Kalangs, which Snouck Hurgronje traces in Veth's *Java*, III, p. 580 et seq. The same story, in a modified form, is popular in [[Banten]], where in the absence of Kalangs it is applied to the Dutch. The Achehnese version runs as follows. A princess who suffered from a horrible skin-disease was for this cause banished to Nias, with only a dog to bear her company. On that island she found many *peundang* plants, and gradually became acquainted with the curative properties of the *peundang* root. It is not clearly stated what induced her to marry her dog; but from this wedlock a son was born. When he grew up he wished to marry, but Nias was uninhabited. His mother gave him a ring to guide him in his search for a bride; the first woman he met whom the ring fitted was to be his destined wife. After many wanderings he at last met his mother again, the ring fitted her, they wedded, and from this incestuous union is descended the whole population of Nias.

In this legend, typifying the vileness of the origin of the Niasese in Achehnese eyes, there is wanting one feature which characterizes the Javanese myth of the Kalangs. Both have in common the dog and the incestuous marriage; but the Javanese version adds the figure of the wild sow that becomes the mother of a heroine in a miraculous manner, while the Achehnese version is content with the simpler machinery of mother, dog and son. The story was clearly an importation from Java, modified in transmission: the Achehnese tell it of the people of Nias, the Javanese of the people of the wilderness who became the Kalangs, and the people of Banten of the Dutch themselves.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Nias

**Nias** (Achehnese **Nii'h**, also written **Nieh**) is the island off the west coast of [[Sumatra]] which supplied, in historic times, the great bulk of the slave population of [[Acheh]]. Throughout *The Achehnese* it figures as the principal recruiting-ground of Achehnese slavery and as the country of origin of a substantial element in the Achehnese race. The Achehnese slave-traders were, in Snouck Hurgronje's phrase, "until quite recently the terror of Nias and the adjacent islands": the Niasese "were kidnapped in hundreds up to a few years ago, and are still surreptitiously purchased in smaller numbers." This traffic in human beings, and the constant absorption of Niasese blood into the Achehnese population through concubinage, made Nias an integral part of the social system of Acheh.

The Achehnese valued the Niasese highly as slaves. They were described as "tractable, obedient, zealous and trustworthy," and the women were "more highly prized for their beauty than those of the dominant race." Many of the boys who as *sadati* (dancers) or otherwise were made to minister to what Snouck calls "the unnatural lusts of the Achehnese" were of Niasese origin. Nias slaves were employed not only for ordinary tillage and pepper-cultivation but also as soldiers in the endless little wars of the country; during the [[Civil war of 1854-58 (Raja Suloyman vs Raja Ibrahim)|civil war of 1854–1858]] between [[Raja Suloyman]] and his guardian [[Raja Ibrahim]], the supporters of the latter in particular "usually employed Niasese to carry out all operations against the enemy." From Nias, too, the Achehnese derived their knowledge of the highly prized **peundang** treatment, a curative root-treatment whose properties the legendary banished princess was said to have discovered on the island.

Despite the high value set upon them, the Niasese were held in contempt by the Achehnese, who regarded them as descended from a vile and incestuous origin. The legend, told both in Acheh and (with variations) in Java, relates that a princess suffering from a horrible skin-disease was banished to Nias with only a dog for companion; she married the dog, bore him a son, and from the incestuous union of mother and son sprang the whole population of the island. The Achehnese summed up this contempt in a doggerel verse: *"Nii'h kumudée urbe bee buy, malum bee asee"* — "Niasese, that eats bengkudu fruits, smells like a pig in the daytime and like a dog at night." In spite of these sayings, and of the prevalence of *kurab* (ringworm) among the Niasese, the Achehnese set a high value on them as slaves and frequently married female slaves of Niasese origin.

The absorption of Niasese blood into the Achehnese population took place through several channels. Although the Achehnese, who laid great stress on descent from the mother's side, was reluctant to father children by his female slaves, such unions did occur, and recourse was had to various methods to avert or nullify their natural consequences. The Mohammedan law made the children of such unions follow the condition of the mother — that is, slavery — but the **Achehnese adat**, on the other hand, treated them as free; their origin was indicated by the name ***aneu' inong*** ("children of gold," i.e. of proprietorship), and after a generation or two the name was dropped and their descendants became Achehnese. Free Niasese did not, except in rare cases, intermarry with Achehnese women, but their children might take wives of mixed descent; "in the third generation they too are Achehnese, though with a slight Nias taint." The same process of absorption characterised the more limited trade in Batak, Chinese, and African ("Abeusi") slaves brought back by *hajis* from [[Mecca]].
