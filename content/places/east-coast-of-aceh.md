**The East Coast of Aceh** — the *Oostkust van Atjeh* — was the low-lying coastal strip along the eastern side of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] facing the [[Strait of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]], characterised in the source *DLP-84-Atjeh* by extensive mangrove swamps whose stilt roots were clearly visible from passing vessels, and whose mangrove bark (Dutch: *bakaubast*) had been historically considered worthless but by the early twentieth century had become a commercial product exported through the harbours of the residency (Dutch: *gewest*). The coastline was later traversed by the [[Atjehtram|Atjeh-tram]] and the [[Atjehweg|Great Atjeh Road (Dutch: *Groote Atjehweg*)]], which facilitated the trade and settlement that transformed the Oostkust from a frontier of military operations during the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]] into one of the principal plantation districts of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]].

## Environment and the Mangrove Swamp

The mangrove vegetation of the East Coast dominated the landscape of the littoral, providing a natural barrier against tidal forces and serving as the source of the *bakau* bark (Dutch: *bakaubast*) that became a significant smallholder export of the residency. The mangrove swamps, with their exposed stilt roots and their dense canopy of salt-tolerant trees, were characteristic of the muddy shoreline that extended, in uninterrupted stretches, from the delta of the [[Krueng Raya|Krueng Raya]] in the north to the borders of the [[Sumatra's East Coast|Sumatra's Oostkust]] residency in the south. The contrast with the steep, rugged shoreline of the [[West Coast of Aceh|West Coast of Aceh]] was marked: where the Westkust presented difficult terrain that required extensive engineering for road construction, the Oostkust presented a flat, waterlogged plain that was poorly drained but readily traversed by rail and road. The mangrove swamp was therefore both an environmental feature and a commercial resource of the East Coast, and its exploitation for *bakau* bark contributed to the early colonial economy of the residency even before the plantation boom of the 1910s and 1920s.

## Economic Role and the Plantation District

The exploitation of mangrove bark for *bakau* products — principally as a source of tannin for the leather industry — contributed to the early colonial economy of the East Coast, while the construction of transport infrastructure — the [[Atjehtram|Atjeh-tram]], the [[Atjehweg|Great Atjeh Road]], and the [[Atjehspoorweg|Atjeh railway]] — opened the region to the broader commercial exchange that transformed it into one of the principal plantation districts of the *gewest*. The east-coast lowlands, with their deep alluvial soils and their accessibility by water, were well suited to the plantation crops — rubber, palm oil, sisal, and copra — that the European estates opened under the [[Agrarische Wet 1870|Agrarian Law of 1870 (Dutch: *Agrarische Wet*)]] after the formal close of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] in [[1904]]. The towns of [[Langsa|Langsa]], [[Koeala Simpang|Koeala Simpang]], and [[Besitang|Besitang]] grew up as the commercial centres of the new plantation district, and the [[Atjehspoorweg|Atjeh railway]] was extended to [[Besitang|Besitang]] to connect the Oostkust of Atjeh with the [[Deli Spoorweg Maatschappij|Deli Spoorweg Maatschappij]] network and the port of [[Belawan|Belawan]]. The East Coast thus became, by the 1920s, the most economically developed part of the residency, and the principal point of contact between Atjeh and the wider Sumatran economy.
