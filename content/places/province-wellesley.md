**Province Wellesley** (Malay *Seberang Perai*) is the strip of mainland territory on the Malay Peninsula opposite [[Penang]], acquired by the [[British India|British East India Company]] in [[1800]] and named after Richard Wellesley, Marquess Wellesley, Governor-General of India. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Province Wellesley is invoked chiefly for its adoption of the Achehnese-Malay word *mukim* as an administrative subdivision, and as the locus of certain Malay festivals parallel to those of Acheh.

## The Word Mukim in British Administrative Usage

The Arabic word *mukim* (literally "one who resides"), Snouck Hurgronje observes, has been distorted in Acheh into a territorial designation for a parish-like subdivision. "In [[Penang]] and Province Wellesley," the translator's footnote continues, "the English Government has adopted the word to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes. In its stricter sense it corresponds almost exactly to the English word 'parish'." The same word has "in [[Kedah]] the same modified meaning as in Acheh." The Penang-Kedah-Wellesley axis thus preserves in its colonial and state administrative vocabulary the same territorial conception that underlies the Achehnese *mukim*, a testimony to the diffusion of Achehnese-Malay institutional language across the Straits.

## The Sawah / Ladang Vocabulary

A further translator's note glosses the Malay word *sawah* (irrigated rice-field) as used in the southern parts of the [[Malay Peninsula]] — [[Johor]], [[Malacca]] etc. — and in [[Java]]. "In [[Penang]], Province Wellesley, [[Kedah]] etc. the word is *bladang*." The contrast mirrors that of the Achehnese *blang* (open field) versus *ladang* (swidden), and places Province Wellesley within the same linguistic region as Kedah and Penang on the one hand, and as Acheh on the other.

## The Mandi Safar Festival

The [[Mandi Safar]] or "bathing of Safar" — the popular Malay festival of ritual bathing at the close of the month Safar — is one of the most popular of festivals among the Malays of Penang and Province Wellesley. "The method of its observance is almost identical with that of the Achehnese as here described," the translator notes. The parallel between the two observances is another indication of how closely the calendar-customs of Acheh agreed with those of the opposite coast of the Straits.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Province Wellesley

**Province Wellesley** (Malay: *Seberang Perai*) is the strip of mainland territory on the [[Malay Peninsula]] lying opposite [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]], from which it is separated by the Penang Strait. Acquired by the British East India Company from the [[Raja of Quedah|Sultan of Kedah]] in [[1800]] for an additional annual payment on top of the cession of Penang itself in [[1786]], it formed, together with Penang, [[Malacca]], and (after [[1819]]) [[Singapore]], one of the constituent territories of the Straits Settlements.

Although Province Wellesley is not mentioned by name in Van der Kemp's monograph, it is the necessary geographical complement of [[Penang]] in any account of the British establishment on the [[Malay Peninsula]]. The [[Penang]] government, which figures prominently in Van der Kemp's narrative as the immediate conductor of British relations with [[Acheh]], exercised jurisdiction over both the island and the opposite mainland strip. When Van der Kemp discusses the Pinang administration's interventions in Achehnese affairs — its despatch of [[Captain Phillips|Captain Phillips]], its correspondence with the [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan of Acheh]], its reception of [[Tunku Pakih|Tunku Pakih]] — the political actor in question is the government of Prince of Wales Island together with Province Wellesley.

Province Wellesley's importance in this period lay chiefly in its supplying Penang with food, fresh water, and timber, and in its commanding the mainland side of the harbour. It was also the base from which British influence could be projected into the adjacent Malay states of Kedah and Perak — a fact of which [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and his successors were keenly aware. In Van der Kemp's framing, Penang (with Province Wellesley) together with the other "Straits" — Malacca and Singapore — constituted the British strategic triangle whose protection justified the diplomatic effort devoted to the [[Acheh Treaty of 1819|Acheh treaty]] and the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London 1824|London treaty of 1824]].
