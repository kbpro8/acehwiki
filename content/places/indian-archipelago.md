The **Indian Archipelago** — also called the **East Indian Archipelago**, the **Eastern Archipelago**, or the **Malayan Archipelago** — is the great island world of South-East Asia, of which [[Acheh|Acheh]] forms the westernmost part. The term is used throughout *The Achehnese* as the common geographical frame for the comparative study of Achehnese institutions; it is essentially interchangeable with [[East Indian Archipelago|East Indian Archipelago]] and [[Eastern Archipelago|Eastern Archipelago]], which Snouck Hurgronje uses in the same sense.

## Funeral observances

The funeral observances of the Moslim populations of the Indian Archipelago are, Snouck observes, "practically identical with those of Acheh." The rules prescribed by the Mohammedan law for the treatment of the dead are pretty faithfully observed throughout the Archipelago, though here as everywhere else they are "modified and added to in details by the adat of the country." The special *tahlils* recited in Acheh on the third, seventh, ninth, eleventh, forty-fourth and hundredth days after death have their parallels in the other parts of the E. Indian Archipelago; the Achehnese name *kanduri meueng ubèë jeurat* ("the feast of the cat at the grave") is, however, peculiar to Acheh, and the name *sěndana* given to this feast in other parts of the E. Indian Archipelago is unknown in Acheh.

## Marriage-makers and the wali

The system of official marriage-makers is treated as characteristic of the Indian Archipelago as a whole. In every part of the Archipelago, the *wali* of the bride is almost invariably represented by a *wakil*, even though he be actually present at the making of the contract. The district pangulu, or whatever other name the maker of the marriage contract may be known by, causes one or two of the officers of the mosque to attend marriages, and these obtain a moderate fee for their assistance. The names of these officials vary with the locality: in [[Mecca|Mekka]] they are called *mumlik*; in [[Medina|Medina]], "licentiates for the making of marriages"; in [[Egypt|Egypt]], *fiqhī*; in Java, *naib* or district pangulu, or their subordinates (*ketib*, etc.); in Acheh, *teungku meunasah*.

The Indian Archipelago is also the scene of the universal application of the *ta'liq* — the method, sanctioned by Mohammedan law, by which a husband's failure to maintain his wife, or his prolonged absence from her, entitles her to a judicial declaration dissolving the marriage. Such predeclared and morally compulsory *ta'liqs* are by no means rare in the Indian Archipelago: the husband, for instance, in conformity with an agreement with his parents-in-law, declares immediately after the marriage ceremony that he will be liable to divorce if he ill-treats his wife, fails to maintain her, or absents himself for a specified period.

## The first contact with the child's mother earth

Throughout the whole of the Indian Archipelago it is regarded as a momentous epoch in the life of a child when he first comes into contact with his mother earth — that is, when he is first set down on the ground. The *peutron*, as this ceremony is called in Acheh, has its parallels in every part of the Archipelago; the Achehnese form is described by Snouck as one of the most characteristic of Achehnese domestic ceremonies.
