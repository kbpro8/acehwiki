**Sabang** was the free port and coaling station developed on the northern bay of
[[Poeloe We]], the island off the northern tip of [[Acheh]], by the
[[Netherlands Indies]] Government and the [[N.V. Zeehaven en Kolenstation Sabang|Sabang Company]]
from the 1880s onward. Conceived as the only [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] free port and promoted as a rival
to [[Penang]] and [[Singapore]], it never captured the regional entrepôt trade its founders
predicted, but established itself instead as one of the most important bunkering stations in the
[[East Indies]], commanding the shipping lanes between [[Europe]] and the trading ports of the East.

## The island and its occupation (1884–1887)

The harbour was laid out in the northern bay of [[Poeloe We]], an island that in the seventeenth
and eighteenth centuries had served the [[Sultanate of Acheh]] as a penal colony and that in the
nineteenth century held only a very small permanent population on its southern side. The island
retained importance for [[Acheh]] chiefly because it yielded *sulphur*, which the warlike sultanate
needed for the manufacture of gunpowder. The [[Netherlands Indies]] Government took the island into
possession in [[1884]], building a small coal jetty that year on the southern bay of
[[Balohan|Lhok Balohan]] and appointing a head official for the island. The Balohan bay proved
unsatisfactory for shipping, and a commission sent out in [[1887]] concluded that the bay at Sabang
was the better site.

## Conception as an ocean harbour

In [[1887]] the [[Batavia]]-based firm [[De Lange (firm)|De Lange]] received a concession to
establish a coal station at the Sabang bay; the head of the firm, [[Van Oosterzee]], is remembered
in the source as the "spiritual father of Sabang." The idea of Sabang as an ocean harbour was first
propagated vigorously by the then Major [[Van Heutsz]] in his [[1892]] brochure
[[The Subjugation of Atjeh (1892)|"The Subjugation of Atjeh"]], which urged the colony to exploit
the island's natural advantages by opening it as the only [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] free port, from which
he expected formidable competition with [[Penang]].

That competition did not materialise as predicted. The long-standing trade between [[Acheh]] and
[[Penang]] — which had built up a standing line of credit for the traders of the coastal districts
among their Penang correspondents — together with the limited staying power of the Dutch capital
then interested in Acheh's commerce, kept the import and export trade in its established channels.
The transfer of that trade from [[Penang]] to Sabang foreseen by [[Van Heutsz]] scarcely occurred;
only a portion of the trade of the cultivation district on the [[East Coast of Sumatra]] was drawn
to the new port.

## Opening and early growth (1897–1903)

Sabang was opened as a free port for general trade in [[1897]]. A year later, in [[1898]], the
[[N.V. Zeehaven en Kolenstation Sabang]] was incorporated and took over the business of the firm
[[De Lange (firm)|De Lange]]. The beginnings were modest: in [[1895]] the place still meant little
commercially; two simple coal jetties were built for the concession-holder in [[1896]]; and in
[[1898]] a government dry dock of 3,000 tons gross capacity was stationed there, later passing to
the [[N.V. Zeehaven en Kolenstation Sabang|Sabang Company]] under a servitude guaranteeing free
docking to Government vessels.

The decisive advance came in [[1903]], when the [[Stoomvaart Maatschappij Nederland]] undertook to
route its ships via Sabang rather than [[Padang]], and the [[Packet Service]] likewise brought the
harbour into its contractual services — giving Sabang direct communication with [[Europe]] and the
Indian trading ports. The port had been linked by telegraph cable to [[Oelèë Lheuë]] as early as
[[1897]]; a direct cable to [[Medan]] followed in [[1912]], and in [[1911]] a radio-telegraphic
station — described as among the most powerful in all of East Asia — was established there.

## Harbour works and installations

Growing traffic forced continual investment in the harbour and its equipment: a hotel for the
passenger service from [[Acheh]] and the [[East Coast of Sumatra]], houses and coolie quarters for
the expanding workforce, and for the goods trade a breakwater, quay walls, warehouses, trading
jetties, coal sheds and coal jetties. A small seaworthy passenger boat maintained the connection
between Sabang and [[Oelèë Lheuë]], the starting point of the [[Atjeh tram]], whose landing jetty
had to be lengthened for the ferry. The ferry and the warehouses at [[Oelèë Lheuë]] were taken over
by a daughter company, the [[Atjeh Transport Maatschappij]], which held a privileged position in
Government river transport.

For the coal trade the company built large modern installations, including a self-trimming bunker
lighter with belt conveyor handling about 150 tons an hour, a lighter with a grab crane of about
100 tons an hour, and several transborders. Alongside the 3,000-ton dry dock there stood an ice
factory, a gas factory, an electrically pumped water supply that also served ships, oil tanks,
locomotive cranes, slipways for building lighters and small boats, and a large repair workshop able
to turn out castings as large as bridge parts for [[Deli]]. Some 400 [[Javanese]] contract coolies
were housed in a camp with its own sick bay, alongside dwellings for [[Chinese]] free labourers, the
contract system for the latter having been abandoned.

## The coal and transit trade

The delivery of steam coal to ships remained the most important part of the company's business. Coal
deliveries grew from 19,000 tons in [[1900]] to about 130,000 tons in [[1906]] and a plateau of
210,000 tons in [[1912]] and [[1913]], before the war reduced them to 176,000 tons in [[1914]] and
135,000 in [[1915]]. Until [[1899]] only Ombilin and Cardiff coal were stored; competition with
[[Colombo]], [[Penang]] and [[Singapore]] then made it necessary to import Bengalese coal, and the
standard blend of Bengalese and Ombilin coal in a 2:1 ratio — the "Sabang mixture" — became well
regarded in the steamship service.

Before the war the company held contracts with some 130 steamship lines, and the station was
preferred to [[Penang]] and [[Singapore]]: at Penang ships had to wait for the tide, and at
Singapore night entry was forbidden, whereas at Sabang the harbour and its lighting allowed vessels
to enter at any hour and to bunker day and night with the least loss of time. As a trading harbour
its scale can be gauged from the (admittedly incomplete) trade values: imports rose from about
f 9,820,000 in [[1911]] to f 12,956,000 in [[1914]], while exports climbed more steeply, from about
f 9,013,000 in [[1911]] to f 23,669,000 in [[1914]].

## A transit harbour without a hinterland

By nature Sabang was a transit harbour, lying on a small island with no real hinterland. Its
founders hoped that the flourishing of the [[East Coast of Sumatra]] and the expected development of
[[Acheh]] as a cultivation and mining area might in time supply one. The planned ocean harbour at
[[Belawan]] was not expected to do Sabang much harm — the example of [[Port Swettenham]], whose rise
had not injured [[Penang]] or [[Singapore]], was cited — since in world shipping the decisive
question was which harbour was best placed and served most quickly and cheaply, nationality counting
for little. The special trading conditions expected to follow the war were thought likely to
strengthen Sabang's position against competing foreign ports.

## Significance

Sabang stands as a case of a colonial port that failed in its declared purpose yet succeeded in
another. The "illusion" of supplanting [[Penang]] and [[Singapore]] as the entrepôt of the region
was never realised, and the old credit-bound trade with [[Penang]] held firm; but by exploiting
[[Poeloe We]]'s deep, all-hours anchorage and the new ocean routes of the [[Stoomvaart Maatschappij Nederland]]
and the [[Packet Service]], Sabang made itself indispensable as a coaling and transit station on the
approach to the [[Strait of Malacca]]. It is the clearest expression of how Dutch power, once it had
brought [[Acheh]] under control, sought to convert the conquest into a commercial gateway.
