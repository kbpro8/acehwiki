The **XXII Mukims** (Dutch *XXII Moekims*; "Twenty-two Mukims") was the highland *sagi* — confederacy of territorial districts — of [[Acheh]] proper, one of the [[Three Sagis]] which together controlled the capital [[Banda Acheh]] in the early nineteenth century. The sagi was ruled by the chief whose hereditary title was [[Panglima Polim]] ("Heer Oudere Broeder"), and was regarded as the most important of the three sagi-chiefdoms of Great Acheh.

## Position within the Three Sagis

Van der Kemp identifies the three sagis at the time of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs commission of 1819]] as the [[XXII Mukims]], the [[XXV Mukims]] and the [[XXVI Mukims]]. He notes that the head of the XXII Mukims, who "vele geslachten door" had borne the title of Panglima Polim, "gold als de voornaamste onder de Sagishoofden" — was regarded as the chief among the sagi-heads. The 1893 work of [[Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]] on *De Atjehers*, cited by Van der Kemp, treats the same division.

## Role in 1819

When [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] and [[Captain Coombs]] anchored before [[Banda Acheh]] on [[14 March]] [[1819]] they found no sultan in residence. The commission sent a circular inland; the only reply came from Polim, "from his usual place of residence in his own country" — that is, from the XXII Mukims. The meeting, he informed the commissioners, might take place in eight days; until then the commission was not to land. Van der Kemp cites the report to London of [[7 October]] [[1820]]: "It is evident to us that Panglima Polim was the actual ruler of Acheen; that he had entirely cast off his allegiance to [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jowhar 00l Aulum]] and was not disposed to renounce it; moreover that he treated [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf 00l Aulum]] merely as a tool…". The de facto supremacy of the XXII Mukims within the Three Sagis was thus the constitutional reality with which the British had to deal.

## Aftermath

The sagi continued to refuse Jauhar Alum. In the [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|letter of 4 December 1822]] the Three Sagis swore, on the tomb of [[Teungku Anjong]] at [[Kampong Java]], never to accept the old sultan again. After Jauhar's death in [[1823]] the Three Sagis and the [[Queen-Mother of Acheh|Queen-Mother]] exercised such authority as remained in the capital. [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] confirmed that "the whole power at Acheen was in the hands of Panglima Polim and at the subordinate ports the respective chiefs had established their independence". The XXII Mukims and their chief thus remained the effective rulers of the capital, while the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] proved to be, as the London authorities acknowledged, a "dead letter".

## Account from *Boon, Van Lansberge* (1943) — XXII Mukims

The **XXII Mukims** was a region in [[Aceh]] that was a major center of resistance against Dutch rule during the [[Aceh War]]. It was the focus of several Dutch military campaigns in [[1878]]-[[1880]], and its eventual submission marked a significant turning point in the conflict.

## Strategic Significance

The XXII Mukims region was important for several reasons:
- It was a center of Acehnese resistance, particularly under the leadership of [[Panglima Polim]] and other chiefs.
- It contained key locations including [[Indrapoeri]], a symbolic center of resistance.
- Control of the region was essential for the Dutch to secure their position in [[Great Aceh]].

## Dutch Military Campaigns

### [[1878]] Operations

Following the [[June]] [[1878]] invasion by [[Habib Abdul Rahman]], the Dutch launched operations into the XXII Mukims:
- General [[K. van der Heijden]] captured [[Mon Tasië]] in [[July]] [[1878]].
- The operations were slow, with Van Lansberge expressing frustration: "I do not understand what Van der Heijden is doing with the large military force he now has at his disposal."

### [[1879]] Campaign

A major campaign was launched in [[March]] [[1879]]:
- **Capture of Indrapoeri ([[24 March]])**: The mosque at Indrapoeri was captured.
- **Capture of Glé Jeuëng ([[9 June]])**: This operation was costly but successful.
- **Arrest of T. Moeda Baet ([[9 May]])**: The recalcitrant chief was arrested and exiled to [[Banda]].

## Submission

The submission of the XXII Mukims was achieved through a combination of military pressure and negotiation:
- [[Habib Abdul Rahman]] submitted and agreed to leave Aceh ([[October]] [[1878]]).
- [[T. Moeda Baet]] submitted in [[October]] [[1878]].
- The region was gradually pacified through the establishment of Dutch posts and the appointment of local chiefs.

## Cost and Challenges

The campaigns in the XXII Mukims were costly and controversial:
- Van Lansberge noted that "the war is being fought sluggishly" and that operations were "led by a system of delay."
- General [[H.G. Boumeester]], after visiting Aceh, assessed the situation and recommended limited engagement rather than full occupation.
- The cost of the campaigns contributed to the financial strain on the colony.

## Legacy

The pacification of the XXII Mukims was a key step in the eventual establishment of Dutch control over Aceh. It demonstrated the effectiveness of military pressure combined with political negotiation, a pattern that would be repeated in other regions.
