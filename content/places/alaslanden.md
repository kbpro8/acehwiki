**The Alaslanden** are the long, fertile valley of the *Lao Alas* — the great river that, rising on the slopes of the G. Losèr as the W. ni Agoesen, runs south-eastward through the heart of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] to its confluence with the Lao Renéën — and one of the five *afdeelingen* of the gouvernement, established after the *colonne van Daalen* of [[1904]] brought the Alas valley under Dutch authority. *De Buitenbezittingen* describes the Alaslanden as one of the four *cultuur-centra* of the interior, an "oase te midden van het ongastvrije, woeste landschap" — an oasis amid an inhospitable, savage landscape — and the territory in which, more than in the Gajoland, Dutch administration had already imposed a territorial organisation upon the genealogical order of the native chiefs.

## Geography

The Alas valley forms the northern extension of the Western lengtedal of the Barisan range, beginning as the *Renoen-dal* of the Daïri-landen to the south and continuing northward past the bergreus G. Losèr into the dal of the middle W. ni Tripa. The Lao Alas itself rises on the G. Losèr and flows south-east between the Wilhelmina-keten — "een zwaren muur tusschen de vruchtbare Alasvallei en het woeste heuvelland van boven-Langkat en Tamiang" — and the Western *randgebergte* of the Daïri-landen. Its bevolking, of heterogeneous origin (Batak, Menangkabau, Atjehsch, Pak-Pak), is concentrated in well-cultivated kampongs along the rivier; the sawahs of the Alas valley provide completely in the rijstbehoefte of its inhabitants, and "daarenboven vrij bewoners van het bergland" — feed besides the inhabitants of the mountain country round about.

## The Gajo- en Alaslanden and the 1904–1905 expedition

The Alaslanden and the adjacent [[Gajoland|Gayo]] highlands together formed the **Gajo- en Alaslanden**, the mountainous region in the interior of [[Acheh|Atjèh]] inhabited by the Gayo and Alas peoples, which constituted the last major centre of resistance during the [[Aceh War]]. The Gayo and Alas highlands form a rugged, forested plateau in central Aceh, with peaks exceeding 3,000 metres. Before the 1904 campaign, *De Buitenbezittingen* records, the Dutch "wisten goed als niets" — knew effectively nothing — of the inhabitants of the Alas valley.

In early [[1904]], [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] — then a lieutenant colonel — led a column of 120 *marechaussées* on a four-month expedition through both the Gayo and Alas lands. The operation was designed to break the last organised resistance and bring the interior under Dutch control; Van Daalen's tactics proved effective, and the expedition brought the country "ineens geheel onder onze macht" (at once entirely under our power). The campaign attracted criticism in the Netherlands, particularly after the "Wekker" articles of [[1907]], for the severity of its methods and casualties among the local population, but the subsequent [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] found such allegations to be largely exaggerated. Civil administrators subsequently, including [[H. Colijn]], worked on integrating the region into the broader Aceh governance structure. A permanent administrative post was established, and the [[Korte Verklaring]] system was extended to the region's chiefs.

## The Radja Tjèq and the two landschappen

The original territorial overlords of the Alas valley were the *Radja Tjèq* (Radja Kémala) — the *roi fainéant* who, by the time of the Dutch arrival in [[1904]], "had afstand gedaan van alle directe bestuursmacht" — and his vassals the *Kédjoerön Bambél* and the *Kédjoerön Batoe Mboelan* (whose deputy was the *Kédjoerön Radja Bintang*). The two landschappen — [[Batoe Mboelan|Batoe Mboelan]] in the north (under direct Radja Tjèq rule around the residential kampong) and [[Bambél|Bambél]] in the south (held in leen by the Kédjoerön Bambél) — were each governed by a *Rijksraad* of four *Péngoeloe si-émpat*, of which the Radja Tjèq himself reserved the fourth seat. The legend of the colonisation — the *Bataq* founders Alas, Kroeas, Pagan and Képala Dèsa; the later Menangkabau immigration under Radja Déwa; the Atjehsche *Toenggal Alas* invested with the southern half — is given in the source at length.

## Dutch organisation, 1914

*De Buitenbezittingen* records that the Dutch, "onbekend met de werkelijke verhoudingen" — unfamiliar with the actual relations — initially treated the two Kédjoeröns as wholly independent *landschapshoofden* authorised to sign the [[Korte Verklaring|Korte Verklaring]], reducing the Radja Tjèq to the rank of an ordinary *Péngoeloe si-émpat* — a misstep whose political consequences were, however, not damaging. The reorganisation of [[1914]] (entirely in werking since that year) created two new *landschappen*: the *landschap Batoe Mboelan* was dissolved, its territory transferred to Bambél, and the *Kédjoerön* moved his residence to [[Poeloe Nas|Poeloe Nas]] (where he signed a new Korte Verklaring for that landschap), while the old Batoe Mboelan was reorganised into four *mérga*'s under bezoldigde Péngoeloe's si-émpat. The Alaslanden were now divided into the landschap Poeloe Nas (four mérga's: Klis under Imöm Balei, Képala Dèsa under Batin, Kling under Kérétan, Tjébéro under Béntjirim) and the landschap Bambél (four mérga's: 'Ngkran under 'Ngkran, Béroeh under Biaq Moeli, Sékédang under 'N Djaar, and Sélian under the Radja Tjèq of Batoe Mboelan). The Kédjoerön retained direct rule over the kampongs of Bambél and Téroetoeng; the Radja Tjèq over those of Batoe Mboelan.

## The Alasweg and the ending of the bong-stelsel

The Alasweg — driven from [[Kota Tjane|Kota Tjane]] (the bestuurszetel of the afdeeling) to Kaban Djahe on the Karo-hoogvlakte, and there connected to the wegennet of the Oostkust van Sumatra — opened the isolated valley to the east, "waardoor de handelsbeweging zich voor het grootste deel daarlangs verplaatst," ending the *schadelijke en onderdrukkende werking* of the Singkelsche *bong-bong-stelsel*. The bevolking of the afdeeling was about 10,000 (3,000 men, 3,000 women, 2,000 boys, 2,000 girls) over 6,000 km² — about 1.7 per km², the sparsest of the five afdeelingen. The registered adult Atjehers of the afdeeling numbered 3,170 at the end of [[1915]].

## Account from *Atjèh and the War with Japan* (1949) — Alasvalley

**The Alasvalley** is the long, low-lying valley of the Lawé Alas river in Central Atjèh, the focus of the Dutch defense of Sumatra in March [[1942]] where General [[Major-General Overakker|Overakker]] personally led the final resistance against the Japanese invasion after the fall of Java, and the home territory of the Alas people whose subdistrict, with headquarters at [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]], was merged with the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] in [[1941]] to form the new combined subdistrict Gajó Loeós and Alaslanden. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats the Alasvalley as the keystone of the entire Dutch strategy of prolonged guerrilla warfare in the Atjèh highlands.

## The valley and the subdistrict

The Alasvalley, the principal settlement area of the Alas people, lay in the deep southeast of the Atjèh residency; "in contrast to the low-lying Alas valley, the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] valley lies at 800 to 1000 m. altitude with a surrounding mountain range of 2000 to 3000 m." The subdistrict Alaslanden — with headquarters [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]] — had only been merged with the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] subdistrict — with headquarters [[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang Kédjérèn]] — in [[1941]] "through a merger of the subdistricts Alaslanden … and Gajó Loeós … made possible by the already in the previous chapter … mentioned establishment of the road connection [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]]-[[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang Kédjérèn]]." The new district, "which in total had 43,500 inhabitants, consisted of only three *landschappen*: [[Bambél|Bambél]] (14,000) with the headquarters [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]] and [[Poeloe Nas|Poeloe Nas]] (6,500) in the Alas valley and the *landschap* [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] (23,000)."

## The defense of March 1942

When, "after the fall of Java, namely, General Overakker decided to also withdraw the troops elsewhere on Sumatra via [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] to Central Atjèh, in order to try to hold out in the Atjèh highlands for as long as possible. The focus of the defense thus came to lie in the Alasvalley and the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]], where General Overakker would personally lead the defense." The plan, "according to the official report of the military operations ('De Militaire Spectator', April 1947, p. 220-221)," was that "in a meeting between both military commanders it had been decided, despite the capitulation of the entire army having been ordered, to continue the fight. The area of the Alasvalley was best suited to tie down as many Japanese troops as possible for as long as possible with a small number of troops." General Overakker "hoped to tie down as many enemy troops as possible, so that they would not be available for operations in other areas (Australia!)." The enormous internment camp at [[Lawé Sigalagala|Lawé Sigalagala]], some 20 km. south of [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]] — originally built for German internees, now housing "several thousand women and children of Indonesian military personnel, as well as about a hundred European evacuees" — became a heavy constraint on the troops' freedom of movement.

## The breakthrough and capitulation

"On [[14 March]] general Overakker had moved his headquarters from [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] to the Alasvalley and first established it at [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]], later at the former Japanese undertaking 'Boekit Toesam'." The Japanese troops, after taking Siborongborong, "advanced from Siborongborong Southward to [[Taroetoeng|Taroetoeng]]. … Only at the border between Tapanoeli and Sumatra's West Coast did the Japanese notice their mistake and turned their attention to the Alasvalley." "On [[25 March]] a Japanese breakthrough took place from the valley of the Lawé Rénoen, a left-side tributary of the Lawé Alas, to the road [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]]-[[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]], at a distance of 80 km. before [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]]." This breakthrough made "a further retreat to the last stronghold, the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]], necessary. The headquarters was moved to [[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang Kédjérèn]]. On [[26 March]] our last troops left [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]]." When General Overakker "decided on [[27 March]] to surrender, there was no longer any enemy to be acknowledged in the [[Blang Kédjérèn|Blang]], so that the surrender could only be offered on [[28 March]]. Thus in Atjèh the final phase of the fighting on Sumatra would be fought."
