**Lebong Donok** is the famous gold-bearing quartz outcrop near [[Muara Aman]] in the Lebong district of [[Benkulen|Benkulen]] (Bencoolen), whose [[1895]]/[[1896]] discovery by native gold washers — after European interest had been aroused by samples shown to the manager of the Suban Ajam coffee estate by Hadji Ismael of [[Pasar Tjurup]] — led to the establishment of the "Lebong Goud Syndicaat" (Lebong Gold Syndicate) and, in [[1897]], to the founding of the Mining Company "Redjang Lebong". The Lebong Donok ore body became the most productive gold mine of the Netherlands East Indies: in 42 years of operation ([[1899]]–[[1940]]) it produced about 80 million guilders' worth of bullion (41,535 kg of gold and 228,762 kg of silver from 3,243,000 tons of ore), before being finally exhausted and abandoned in [[1940]].

## The 1895/1896 discovery

Van Bemmelen's account of the Lebong Donok discovery, in the gold-and-silver chapter of [[Van Bemmelen's The Geology of Indonesia (1949)|*The Geology of Indonesia*]] ([[1949]]), reads:

> "In [[1895]] or [[1896]] European interest for gold in this region arose suddenly due to the fact that Mr. [of] the coffee estate Suban Ajam was shown samples of gold ores by Hadji Ismael of [[Pasar Tjurup]]. Then a large outcrop of gold-bearing quartz at Lebong Donok near [[Muara Aman]] was shown by native gold washers and this discovery led to the establishment of the 'Lebong Goud Syndicaat'. The ore deposits proved later to be the famous Lebong Donok ore body, mining of which was undertaken by the Mining Company 'Redjang Lebong', established in [[1897]]."

The discovery is the founding episode of European gold-mining in Sumatra, and it set the chronological and methodological pattern for the subsequent Atjeh gold explorations of [[P. J. J. Jansen|Jansen]], [[B. B. Lindberg|Lindberg]] and [[H. Wolvekamp|Wolvekamp]] ([[1922]]) and [[C. de Groot|De Groot]] ([[1946]]).

## The 1897 founding of Redjang Lebong and the 1899 production start

The Mining Company "Redjang Lebong" was established in [[1897]] and took over the mining rights for the Lebong Donok ore body from the Lebong Goud Syndicaat. A small pilot plant was erected and in December [[1897]] the first bullion was produced. Regular production began in November [[1899]], when the "Lebong Donok" concession was formally granted. Production on a minimum base of about 6 gr of gold per ton was continued until [[1936]], when reserves of workable ore were considered to be exhausted; under another direction, production was continued until the mine was finally closed down in [[1940]].

## Production figures

In 42 years of operation ([[1899]]–[[1940]]), Lebong Donok produced:

- **41,535 kg of gold**
- **228,762 kg of silver**
- from **3,243,000 tons of ore**
- total value: **ƒ79,019,000** (about 80 million guilders)
- average value recovered per ton of ore: **ƒ24** (at an average of 24 g/ton combined Au+Ag)
- average Au:Ag ratio: **1:6½**

The Lebong Donok mine alone accounted for 88 % of the total Sumatran gold production by kg, 71 % by kg of silver, and 82 % by total value — making it by far the dominant gold mine of the Sumatran gold-silver belt, and (with Simau) one of the only two gold mines in the Netherlands East Indies that yielded a financial profit.

## Significance for Atjeh

Lebong Donok is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because its [[1895]]/[[1896]] discovery is the founding episode of European gold-mining in Sumatra, and accordingly the chronological and methodological pattern for the subsequent Atjeh gold explorations. The Lebong Donok ore body's 80-million-guilder production (in 42 years) is the benchmark against which the much more modest Atjeh gold production — the [[M.A.E.M.]] dredges at the [[Geudong Concession]] ([[1940]]–[[1941]]) — must be measured: the entire Atjeh gold economy was, in colonial terms, a small frontier outpost of the great Lebong gold-mining district of Benkulen. The [[P. Hövig|Hövig]] [[1914]] monograph on the Lebong gold ores — the foundational source for the colonial study of the Sumatran gold-silver belt — is also the source for the canonical observation that "Remains of former native gold mining are numerous and have been found in many places from Atjeh in the North to Benkulen in the South", a sentence that fixes the geographical frame within which the Atjeh gold occurrences have subsequently been discussed.
