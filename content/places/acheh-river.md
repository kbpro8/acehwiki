**The Acheh River** (*Krueng Acheh*, debouchement *Kuala Acheh*) was the principal river of [[Great Acheh]] and an important geographic and administrative landmark. The mouth of the Acheh River — the *kuala Acheh* — suggested to the Acehnese fancy the somewhat sharply pointed mouth of a *jeuëë* (winnowing-basket), which they used as a metaphor for the shape of their homeland.

## Administrative Division

Proceeding down the Acheh River, one found on the left the territory of the [[Sagi System|XXV Mukims]] and on the right that of the [[Sagi System|XXVI Mukims]]. The right bank was home to twelve [[Gampong|gampongs]] over which the [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] and [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]] contested control — the Teuku Kali Malikon Adé having wrested twelve gampongs on the right bank from the Panglima Meuseugit Raya in a period of rivalry.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Aceh River

The **Aceh River** (in Achehnese usage generally referred to as the *Krueng* Aceh, and at its mouth as the *kuala* or estuary) is the principal river of [[Banda Acheh|the capital]] of Acheh, which descends from the highlands through the lowlands and discharges into the sea at the *kuala* of Acheh. In *The Achehnese* the river figures chiefly as the channel by which the coastal settlements of the capital communicate with the interior, and as the scene of the great *lanchang* ceremony performed in time of epidemic.

The most concrete notice of the Aceh River in Vol. I is in Snouck Hurgronje's account of the *lanchang* ceremony. "Formerly, when an epidemic prevailed, the Sultan would command a great *lanchang* built of wood or bamboo and furnished with a quantity of rice and meat. A pretended human sacrifice to the sea was also made; some poor man who was willing to play the part of victim for a few dollars was placed on board the boat and set off to drift down the Acheh river and out of the *kuala*. At the river's mouth he would manage to regain the shore, leaving the rice and meat to their strife with the waves." The *lanchang*, in Acheh as in the Malay peninsula, was supposed to carry away the *hantu* or evil spirit that caused the epidemic; the same word is used in the northern part of the Malay peninsula for the analogous boat, while in the south it is called *rakit*. The Achehnese *lanchang* of the Sultan was thus a piece of royal folk-medicine, performed on the river of the capital, by which the sovereign sought to avert epidemic from his kingdom.

The Aceh River is also the scene of one of the most famous episodes of Achehnese folk-legend. The unhappy Rabiah, who after death became [[Burong Srabi|the dreaded Burong Srabi]], fled with her lover down the river in a boat, intending to escape to the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]]. "They dropped down the river in a boat, but before they reached the open sea, the man slew his paramour and casting her body into the water, pursued his journey to the West alone." Her body was found near [[Kaje'e Jat'oe]] — "just about where the hospital at [[Kuta Raja]] now stands" — and was pulled out of the water by the people of that gampong, who carried it to [[Tanjong]] in the mukim of [[Pagaraye]] for burial. The legend gives a vivid picture of the river as the great artery of communication between the capital and the West Coast, and as the natural route of flight for those who wished to leave the country by sea.

The Aceh River, like the *kuala* at its mouth, was thus both the practical channel of the capital's external trade and the symbolic channel by which evils — whether the *hantu* of an epidemic or the unhappy Rabiah — were carried out to sea. Its importance in Vol. I is principally as the geographical backbone of the capital district of [[Banda Acheh]], and as the route by which the lowlands of Great Acheh were connected, through the *rantos* of the West Coast, with the wider Achehnese dominion.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Acheh River

The **Acheh River** (Achehnese *Krueng Acheh*; *Kuala Acheh* at its mouth) is the principal watercourse of the [[Banda Acheh|capital]] plain of [[Acheh]], running past the court and the [[Meuseugit Raya]] or principal mosque before debouching into the sea. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje treats the river as both a geographical and a constitutional feature of the old sultanate.

### Form of the True Acheh

The Achehnese delight to compare the form of "true Acheh" — the kernel of the kingdom that supplied outlying districts with inhabitants — to that of a winnowing-basket (*jeu'ee*). The debouchement of the Acheh river (*kuala Acheh*) suggests to their fancy the somewhat sharply pointed mouth of the winnower, "whence all unclean particles adhering to the husked rice are shaken out." Proceeding downstream, one has on the left the territory of the [[XXV Mukims]] and on the right that of the [[XXVI Mukims]], the intervening space being occupied by the [[XXII Mukims]]. This configuration gave rise to the comparison of the three confederacies with the three angles of a triangle, the *three sagis* of Acheh.

### The Panglima Meuseugit Raya

The [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya]] — literally "Commander of the Great Mosque" — was the head of the twenty-four or more gampongs on either side of the Acheh river, in the neighbourhood of the court and principal mosque. Through a long process of degeneration he succeeded in gaining control over half the jurisdiction of the [[Teuku Kali]], the hereditary chief justice of the kingdom. The river thus marked not merely a topographical axis but the political spine of the port-king's domain.

### The Tanoh Raja Reserve

A notable institution connected with the river was the **tanoh raja** ("king's land"): a strip of ground *seven great fathoms* (*deuëpa meunara*) on each side of the Acheh river, reserved from ancient times to the sultan. Subjects might build or plant within this reserve, but the land never became their property, and the sultans could always withdraw the right of user. This royal privilege no doubt originated in the interest of an unimpeded exercise of sovereignty by the kings of the port. The word *wakeuëh* is more commonly used than *tanoh raja* to describe this reservation, although *wakeuëh* also designates the inhabitants of certain districts relieved from sundry burdens and exempted from the authority of the local chiefs.
