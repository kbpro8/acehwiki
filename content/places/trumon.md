A territory on the southern West Coast. The sadati dialogue references the [[Raja Muda]] of Trumon: 'Our intention was, blessed little brother, to take thee there to the house of the [[Raja Muda]] (of Trumon). We can have naught to do now with the Raja of Trumon, for he is in the pay of the Tuan Beusa.' The author of the Hikayat Teungku di Meuke' is 'Teungku Malem, a native of Trumon, married to a woman of [[Peunaga]].'

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Trumon

**Trumon** is a town or region on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] of [[Acheh]] that appears in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as one of the places in which the anniversary *kanduri* of [[Tuan Meurasab]] — the saint of [[Nagore]] on the [[Coromandel Coast|Coromandel coast]] of British India — was occasionally celebrated by the Kling (Tamil Muslim) residents on the 10th day of the sixth month of the Achehnese calendar. It is thus one of the scattered places in Acheh at which the South-Indian Muslim element has left its mark on the calendar of festivals.

The 9th, 10th and 11th days of the sixth month of the Achehnese calendar are consecrated to Tuan Meurasab, whose tomb is at Nagore. He has many worshippers in Acheh, seemingly through the influence of those inhabitants of Southern India who introduced the creed of Islam there. The 10th day of the month is accepted as the anniversary of his death, and the *kanduri* held thereon is called the *kanduri to' thon Tuan Meurasab*, "the religious feast for the expiration of the year of Tuan Meurasab." This feast is occasionally celebrated in [[Kuta Raja]] and Trumon by a great *kanduri* on the 10th day of this month, but the givers of this feast are always the Klings who reside there, and the Achehnese who partake of it do so only as guests. Tuan Meurasab has thus really no place in the Achehnese calendar of festivals proper; his cult is a foreign introduction maintained by the Kling communities of the West Coast and of the capital.

The same restricted character attaches to the celebration elsewhere. Snouck Hurgronje is careful to point out that the feast of Tuan Meurasab is essentially a Kling feast, in which the Achehnese participate as guests rather than as principals. The presence of Kling traders in Trumon — a pepper-producing region of the West Coast — explains both the celebration and its character: the Klings of Trumon, like those of the capital, maintained the devotional usages of their own country in their new home, and the Achehnese came to look on the 10th of the sixth month as a Kling festival rather than as one of their own.

Trumon is also one of the regions of the West Coast generally associated in the book with the pepper-planting economy that drew to the coast the labour of the up-country Achehnese. The poorer women and children, whose husbands and fathers were sojourning on the East or West Coast as pepper-planters, felt the full bitterness of their position on a feast-day, when their friends were careful to refrain from asking them the question addressed to other women at the feast-fair: "How much meat has your husband brought home?" The West Coast was Wednesday *pantang* (forbidden) for pepper-planting as well as rice-cultivation, and the most favourable time for the voyage from the capital to the West Coast began about *keunong* 11 of the calendar.
