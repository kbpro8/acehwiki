**Nieuw Guinea** — the western half of the island of New Guinea — was, from [[1828]] onwards, a [[Netherlands|Dutch]] territorial claim that, in practice, consisted of no more than "hier en daar langs de kusten vlaggen of wapenborden" — flags or arms-boards here and there along the coast. The [[1897]] [[Tweede Kamer]] debate on credits for a more real administrative establishment on [[Nieuw Guinea]] is, in the [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] retrospective, the type-specimen of the colonial-parliamentary warning against the "first step" that, in the case of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], had led from the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|Sumatra-tractaat]] of [[1871]] to the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|declaration of war]] of [[1873]].

## The 1828 possession

The [[Netherlands|Dutch]] claim to [[Nieuw Guinea]] was formalised in [[1828]] by taking possession of the territory as far as the 141st meridian. The legal basis was the [[17th century|mid-17th-century]] submission of the sultan of [[Tidore]], whose territory the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] had — "gemakshalve" — for convenience taken to include "het land der Papoea's en de omliggende eilanden" — the land of the Papuans and the surrounding islands. The 1828 act consisted, in practice, of placing flags and arms-boards along the coast as tokens of Dutch supremacy. According to [[Van Kol]] (s.d.) these tokens were "tamelijk goed gespaard door de inboorlingen" — fairly well spared by the natives — who entertained a superstitious fear of the unknown animals (the Dutch lions) depicted on them.

## The 1897 budget debate

In [[1897]] the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] government asked the [[Tweede Kamer]] for credits for a more substantial administrative establishment on [[Nieuw Guinea]], arguing that the establishment was purely pacific in intent and was undertaken at the request of the *zending* (missions). The [[Tweede Kamer]] was "huiverig" — hesitant — to "steken in een wespennest" — stick its hand in a wasps' nest. After a contested division the credits were granted by 49 against 23.

## The Van Welderen Rengers maxim

The liberal MP [[W. J. van Welderen Rengers|Van Welderen Rengers]] used the [[1897]] debate to formulate the maxim that became a frequently-quoted touchstone of colonial parliamentary debate:

> "In koloniale zaken [geldt] het allerminst het spreekwoord: *c'est le premier pas qui coûte*. De eerste stap wordt gemakkelijk gedaan, het zijn juist de volgende stappen waartoe men ter wille van het prestige gedwongen wordt, welke ons geld en helaas ook mensenlevens kosten."

[[W. J. van Welderen Rengers|Van Welderen Rengers]] pointed, as the type-specimen, to the [[Sumatra-tractaat ([[1871]])|sluiting van het Sumatra-tractaat]] of [[1871]] — when some chambermembers had feared "moeilijkheden met Atjeh" but had been reassured by minister [[P. P. van Bosse|Van Bosse]] — and to the [[Declaration of war against the Sultan of Atjeh (1873)|1873 declaration of war]] on [[Acheh|Atjeh]] that had followed.

## The "Jee maintiendrai" image

The Dutch claim on [[Nieuw Guinea]] is, in [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]]'s paraphrase, a case in which "ons 'Jee maintiendrai' te bengelen [hing] in een door niemand bezocht bosje risophoren, die met hun slanke luchtwortels een treffend symbool vormden van de 'echte' grondslagen van ons gezag op Nieuw Guinea" — our '*Jee maintiendrai*' dangled in a mangrove thicket visited by no one, which with its slender aerial roots formed a striking symbol of the "genuine" foundations of our authority on New Guinea.

## Significance

[[Nieuw Guinea|Nieuw Guinea]] is, in the [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] retrospective, the geographical mirror of [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. Both were territories of "fictief" Dutch sovereignty; the [[1897]] [[Tweede Kamer]] debate on [[Nieuw Guinea]] is the canonical colonial-parliamentary warning against the diplomatic first step that, in the case of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], had led to a 35-year war.
