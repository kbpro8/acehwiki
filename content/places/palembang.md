## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Palembang

**Palembang** (Dutch and English *Palembang*; also *Palimbang*) was the principal Malay sultanate of south-eastern [[Sumatra]], lying along the river Musi some fifty miles from the coast. Although geographically remote from [[Acheh]] and from the [[Sumatra's East Coast|East Coast states]] with which Van der Kemp's article is chiefly concerned, Palembang enters the argument of *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* at a single but pointed juncture: as the scene of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s aggressive raid of [[1819]], an episode which Van der Kemp, writing in the wake of the Jameson raid of December [[1895]], pointedly sets beside it.

## Raffles's raid of 1819

In the closing pages of his article Van der Kemp turns from the immediate subject of the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] to draw a wider moral. "The political complications with England or its servants", he writes, "since the restoration of the Dutch authority in the East Indian archipelago, brought me repeatedly to mind how the same questions, the same manners, the same ways of speaking on the English side are encountered in the study of history, which otherwise have nothing to do with our history." It will therefore be well, he concludes, that in concluding treaties we bear in mind the lessons of history — and not only of our own. "On the agreement of Jameson's raid with the ordered expedition to Palembang in 1819 by Raffles I had already had occasion to point out." The cross-reference is to Van der Kemp's earlier article *De geschiedenis van een Engelschen raid op Hollandsch grondgebied*, in *De Gids* of February [[1898]].

## The parallel with the Jameson raid

The comparison is, on Van der Kemp's part, deliberately provocative. The Jameson raid — Leander Starr Jameson's armed incursion of December [[1895]]–January [[1896]] into the Transvaal, in support of the Uitlander conspiracy centred on Johannesburg — had been officially disavowed by the British government, while the advantages it yielded were discreetly retained. So too, Van der Kemp implies, Raffles's expedition against Palembang in [[1819]] had been an act of unauthorised aggression by a British official, of which the British government disavowed the form while retaining such advantages as accrued. The parallel is part of Van der Kemp's larger argument — already announced in the subtitle of his article on the 1819 raid — that the Sumatra and Transvaal questions, however remote in time and place, exhibit the same recurrent pattern of British colonial action, the same disavowal of agents whose successes were none the less quietly absorbed.

## Place in the wider argument

Palembang's appearance in *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* is therefore not as a setting of the Achehnese negotiations but as a comparandum. Van der Kemp's argument is that the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] and its later career — the British reservation of Achehnese independence extracted in [[1824]] from the Dutch plenipotentiaries — exhibit the same structural features as the Transvaal [[Suzerainty question|suzerainty question]]: a clause originally "ter wille van de oppositie" introduced as nothing, later wielded as an "oorlogsram". Raffles's raid on Palembang is the example held up to show that this was no isolated phenomenon but a settled pattern of British colonial statecraft — and that the Achehnese and Sumatran business of [[1819]]–[[1824]] should be read in that light.
