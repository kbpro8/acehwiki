**Palembang** was the principal Malay [[sultanate]] of south‑eastern [[Sumatra]] and, from the early [[19th century|1800s]] onward, a major port city on the [[Musi River]] that became the focus of several Dutch military expeditions. Located roughly fifty miles inland from the coast, the settlement lay at the crossroads of Acehnese influence, Dutch colonial ambition, and British commercial interest. Copies of the *wasiat nabi* (the Prophet’s last admonition) were printed and reprinted there, attesting to its religious and cultural significance. In the historiography of the Dutch East Indies, Palembang appears both as a comparative example in [[P. H. van der Kemp]]’s analysis of British aggression (the [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] raid of [[September]] [[1819]]) and as the objective of Dutch naval and marine operations in [[1819]] and [[1821]].

## Historical Background

The area around Palembang formed the core of the [[Sultanate of Palembang]], a Malay polity that owed its wealth to the fertile Musi basin and its position as a gateway to the interior of Sumatra. Although geographically remote from the [[Acheh]] sultanate and the [[Sumatra's East Coast|East Coast states]], Palembang remained within the sphere of Acehnese suzerainty until the early nineteenth century, when Dutch and British powers intensified their rivalry over the region.

## Raffles’s Raid of [[September]] [[1819]]

In the closing pages of his article on the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]], [[P. H. van der Kemp]] draws a parallel between the British‐led raid on Palembang in [[1819]] and the later [[Jameson raid]] in South Africa. Van der Kemp argues that both operations were unauthorized aggressions by British officials—[[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles]] in the case of Palembang and [[Leander Starr Jameson]] in the Transvaal—later disavowed by the British government while the strategic gains were quietly retained. He situates the Palembang raid as a “comparandum” to illustrate a recurring pattern of British colonial statecraft, linking it to the broader discourse on the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]] and the subsequent [[Anglo‑Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]].

## Dutch Military Expeditions ([[1819]]–[[1821]])

### 1819 Expedition

In [[September]] [[1819]] a Dutch squadron under [[Schout‑bij‑nacht Wolterbeek]] attempted to subdue the forces of [[Mahmoed Badar Oedien]] after his surprise attack on the Dutch fort at Palembang. The operation targeted fortified river positions along the Musi, including the [[Soensang]] River and the islands of [[Peladjoe]] and [[Gombora]], but achieved only limited success.

### 1821 Expedition

A larger Dutch campaign was launched in [[1821]] to secure full control of Palembang and its hinterland. On land the expedition was commanded by [[General‑majoor De Kock]]; at sea it was led by [[Captain Lewe van Aduard]]. The fleet of frigates and corvettes forced the riverine approaches and culminated in two decisive actions:

- **[[20 June]] [[1821]]** – Dutch marines landed near Palembang, engaging local forces in a brief but intense clash that opened the city’s harbour to Dutch vessels.  
- **[[24 June]] [[1821]]** – A second action secured the harbour fully, allowing Dutch ships to anchor and supply their troops for the occupation of the city.

These operations were recorded in the regimental orders of the [[Dutch Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]] (see [[1699]] for earlier regulations). The conduct of the Dutch marines during the battles was later recognised with the awarding of the [[Militaire Willemsorde|Military Order of William]] (see [[1822]]).

## Fortifications and Riverine Positions

The Dutch forces had to contend with a network of defensive works along the Musi River. The [[Soensang]] River, together with the islands of [[Peladjoe]] and [[Gombora]], formed natural obstacles that the Dutch navy sought to neutralise in order to secure a safe line of communication to Palembang. Control of these positions was essential for the movement of troops and supplies inland.

## Significance

The capture of Palembang marked a turning point in the Dutch campaign to dominate the Sumatran coastline. It directly challenged the residual authority of the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] over southern Sumatra and contributed to the gradual erosion of Acehnese suzerainty in the region. The success of the 1821 operations set a precedent for subsequent Dutch expeditions, such as the [[Celebes expedition|Celebes expedition]] of the [[1820s]], and illustrated the pivotal role of the [[Dutch Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]] in extending colonial control through combined naval and riverine warfare. Moreover, the Palembang raid of [[1819]] remains a key comparative case in the historiography of British colonial aggression, as highlighted by [[P. H. van der Kemp]]’s analysis of the parallels between British actions in Southeast Asia and Southern Africa.
