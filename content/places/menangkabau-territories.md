The **Menangkabau territories** comprise the highland region of west-central [[Sumatra]] traditionally ruled by the princes of [[Menangkabau|Minangkabau]]. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje invokes the Menangkabau territories as one of the two regions in which the *ta'liq*-*adat* — the conditional divorce formula attached to the marriage contract — developed into the characteristic institution of the wife's practical independence of her husband.

## The Ta'liq-Adat and the Wife's Independence

The author groups together "Acheh and the Menangkabau territories" as the two regions in which the *ta'liq*-*adat* has produced the same effect upon the position of the wife. In both, the author writes, "the woman is, so far as lodging and maintenance are concerned, practically independent of her husband, since she continues to form an integral part of the family wherein she was born." An Achehnese woman whose husband has gone as a pepper-planter to the East or West Coast and gives no sign of his existence for years, may indeed, by the operation of the *ta'liq*, free herself from the bond of marriage and contract another.

## The Common Source of the Two Adats

The parallel between Acheh and the Menangkabau territories is the more striking in that the *ta'liq* is, in its origin, an institution of the Mohammedan law, developed especially by the jurists of [[Arabia]] and propagated through the archipelago by teachers of that law. Yet its practical consequences — the wife's continuing membership of her natal family, her economic independence, and her power to dissolve the marriage by her own motion — coincide in the two Sumatran regions in a manner that points to a common substratum of pre-Islamic custom. In [[Java]], by contrast, where the *ta'liq* was ordained in olden times by the rulers of [[Mataram]], [[Chirbon]] and [[Banten]], its effects were different, and the wife's position remained distinct from that enjoyed by her Achehnese or Menangkabau sister.
