The largest island off the coast of [[Acheh]]. On Pulo Breueh is a sacred tree called Ya Karieng ('Grandfather Karieng'), propitiated by those who seek lost buffaloes. Those starting on such a quest promise votive offerings to Ja Karieng; if the buffalo is found, artificial flowers are fixed in crevices of the holy tree. There is also an annual feast to counteract epidemics. The island has multiple saints' tombs: Tuan di Payo on Pulo Ulee

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pulo Breueh

**Pulo Breueh** — also called **Bras** ("rice") — is an island off the coast of [[Acheh|Acheh]] which figures in *The Achehnese* as the site of one of the most curious of the popular superstitions connected with the prevention and combating of epidemics. The island is mentioned in the chapter on folk-medicine and the popular rites by which the Achehnese sought to avert pestilence.

### The karieng-tree and Ja' Karieng

On Pulo Breueh there stood, at the time of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje's]] writing, an old *karieng*-tree which, according to popular superstition, had the power of enchaining epidemics and letting them loose upon the people at will. This tree was called **Ja' Karieng** — "Grandfather Karieng." Once a year the inhabitants of the island held at the tree a great *kanduri*, offering up flowers and incense, and took sea-baths close by. During the course of this feast many forbidden intrigues were set on foot and indulged in; were the festival not observed, it was thought that the coming year would surely be marked by pestilence.

The cult of Ja' Karieng belongs to that class of Achehnese observances by which epidemics — small-pox, cholera, and the like — were warded off or combated. Among the other methods enumerated by Snouck are the *boh lanchang*, the "setting afloat of a lanchang" (a small ritual boat laden with sweetmeats, sirih, a live hen, two raw eggs, boiled glutinous rice, flowers and a piece of white cotton), which was employed "so to speak officially" in earlier times to prevent epidemics; the various *pantang* rules observed in the presence of the sick; and the taboo on words like *maté* ("dead"), *buta* ("blind") and *chetikèë* ("crooked") in the sick-room. The cult of Ja' Karieng is distinguished from these by its annual character, by its concentration on a single tree, and by its association with the corporate life of the islanders.

### The island's other role

The island of Pulo Breueh is also mentioned in the chapter on land tenure, in connection with the letting (*peusewa*) of rice-fields. Letting of rice-fields, Snouck remarks, used to be rare in the lowlands, but very common in the highlands and in Pulo Breueh (Bras), where the cultivated ground was too extensive for the population. The rent was usually paid in husked rice (*breueh*) — a circumstance which, in the popular etymology of the Achehnese, may have contributed to the association of the island's name with the word for husked rice.
