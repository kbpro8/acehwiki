**Lam Gugob** is a locality in [[Acheh]] known in the ethnographic record of *The Achehnese* as the place of fabrication of a particular sort of loin-cloth, the *ija krong Lam Gugob*, which formed a recognised item of the bridegroom's wedding attire. Its name is preserved not in any political or military narrative, but in the technical vocabulary of Achehnese textile production.

## The Bridegroom's Loin-Cloth

Snouck Hurgronje, in his account of Achehnese marriage ceremonial, describes how the bridegroom arrays himself in rich attire for the wedding ceremony. He usually wears a white jacket (*baju*), striped silk trousers, and a loin-cloth "of the sort known as *ija krong Lam Gugob* from the place of its fabrication, all richly laced with gold thread." On his head is placed a cap with a gold crown (*tjampo' meuleuh*) surrounded with a purple handkerchief (*tangko*); in his waistband on the left side is thrust a dagger, the *seulawah*, somewhat differing in appearance from the ordinary Achehnese *rencong*.

The *ija krong* — the term literally means "container-cloth" or "wrapper" — was thus a luxury textile, distinguished by its gold-laced weave and its association with the single locality in which it was produced. To wear it at a wedding was to display at once the wealth of the bridegroom's family and their command of the products of an established local craft tradition.

## Lam Gugob as a Craft Centre

The naming of textiles after their place of fabrication was a widespread practice in the Indian Archipelago, and Acheh — with its long-established trade links to [[Penang]], [[British India]] and the Hadramaut — was no exception. The mention of Lam Gugob in the wedding ceremonial is sufficient to show that the locality possessed, in the late nineteenth century, an organised body of weavers whose products were recognised by name throughout [[Great Acheh]] and beyond. Beyond this single notice, Snouck Hurgronje does not describe the village in detail; but its inclusion in the canonical wedding outfit suggests that its cloth ranked, in the local hierarchy of textiles, alongside the celebrated products of the West Coast and of the highland dye-works.
