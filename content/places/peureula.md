**Peureula** (also Peureulah) is an oil-field locality on the north coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — in the [[Pedirse|Pidie]] region — recorded in [[Van Bemmelen's The Geology of Indonesia (1949)|Van Bemmelen's *Geology of Indonesia*]] ([[1949]]) as the largest producing field of the [[Atjeh oil complex|Atjeh oil complex]] in the [[1940]]–[[1941]] period. The Peureula field was operated by the [[N.L.A.M.]] (Nederlandsche Lloyd Aardolie Maatschappij, the Netherlands Lloyd Petroleum Company), and its production of 48,219 tons of 1000 kg in [[1940]] and 36,640 tons in [[1941]] accounted for the great majority of the Atjeh-complex output in both years.

## Production figures


For the full North-Sumatran production table see the entry on the [[East Atjeh oil block]]. The Peureula field's production of 48,219 tons in [[1940]] made it the second-largest producing oil field in the Atjeh residency after the [[Tamiang oil block|Tamiang block]] (709,625 tons), and considerably larger than the [[B.P.M.]]-operated [[Aru Bay]] (55,924 tons) and [[East Atjeh oil block|East Atjeh block]] (no production in [[1940]]). The decline to 36,640 tons in [[1941]] — a fall of 11,579 tons, or 24.0 % — is consistent with the general contraction of East Indian oil production in [[1941]] as the colonial economy braced for the Pacific War.

The operator of the Peureula field, the [[N.L.A.M.]], is one of the smaller East Indian oil companies recorded in Van Bemmelen's text. The fact that the N.L.A.M. — rather than the dominant [[B.P.M.]] — operated the Peureula field indicates that the Atjeh north coast was one of the regions of the East Indies where the smaller oil companies were able to obtain and develop concessions, in contrast to the B.P.M.-dominated South-Sumatran and East-Borneo provinces.

## Geological context


Van Bemmelen's general classification of the Atjeh petroleum geology places the Peureula field among the "steep folds (Atjeh, Borneo)" that "were repeatedly investigated by drilling but the results have not been encouraging". The implication is that the Peureula oil is hosted in steeply-folded neogene sediments of the Atjeh north coast, in a structural setting less favourable for oil accumulation than the gently-domed anticlines of the South-Sumatran fields. The modest size of the Peureula production, and its decline between [[1940]] and [[1941]], are consistent with this assessment.

## The name "Peureula"


The name "Peureula" — also Peureulah — is the standard Acehnese form of the place-name rendered in colonial Dutch cartography as *Peureulah* or *Peureula*, and in modern Indonesian as *Peureulak*. The place is historically significant in its own right as the site of the early modern sultanate of [[Pasei|Samudra Pasai]] (Peureulak was, in the early Muslim period, a separate coastal kingdom that subsequently merged into the wider Pasai polity). The oil-field name "Peureula" accordingly carries a double resonance: it is both the designation of a colonial-era oil field and the name of a historical Acehnese kingdom.

## Significance


Peureula is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh]] history because it was the largest producing oil field of the [[Atjeh oil complex]] in the [[1940]]–[[1941]] period, and accordingly the principal site of the colonial-era oil industry on the Atjeh north coast proper (as distinct from the southern-edge Tamiang block). The Peureula field is also the clearest colonial instance of the operation of the [[N.L.A.M.]] on Atjeh territory, and it accordingly illustrates the role of the smaller East Indian oil companies in the development of the Atjeh oil economy.

## Account from *Verkenningen in Atjeh* (1923)

**Peureula** — pronounced *Perlak*, and so written in the older literature — is the chief place of the *landschap* of the same name in the *onderafdeeling* [[Langsa|Langsa]] of the *afdeeling* [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust van Atjeh]], and the site of one of the oldest historical kingdoms of northern Sumatra. The place was visited by [[Marco Polo|Marco Polo]] in the last part of the thirteenth century — he called it *Ferlac* — and was an old Atjehnese settlement of long standing when the sultans of [[Aceh|Atjeh]] brought it under their overlordship in the sixteenth century.

### The Name and Its Pronunciation


The official spelling and writing is *Peureula*; the spelling was thus restored by direction of [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]]. But the pronunciation is wholly different from what one would expect. The *eu* in Atjehnese names is almost toneless, sounding about like the *e* in the Malay word *erroee*, so that one hears Peureula as *Perlak*. When one reads the place-names at the stations without knowing this, and pronounces them accordingly, one would not be understood. Thus [[Bireuen|Bireuën]] is pronounced *Birun*, [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] as *Slimum*, [[Keureuto|Keureutò]] as *Kertó*, [[Meureudoe|Meureudoe]] as *Merdoe*.

### The Historical Kingdom


[[Marco Polo|Marco Polo]] visited Perlak in the last part of the thirteenth century and called it *Ferlac*. He reported that Mohammedan merchants were the bosses there, and that there was an inland population that ate men. He said that rice was eaten, and the arenga-palm and the coco-palm were used. The Chinese traveller [[Fei Hsin|Fei Hsin]], two centuries later, saw that pepper grew there, and that the people prepared *toewak* from the nipah-palm, and that two harvests of dry fields were obtained yearly. Sultans rode high seated on an elephant, and went to war against unbelievers.

There is now still a *kampong* Samoedra, where however no old archive is present. Here and there lie the graves of old sultans and princes, of which those at [[Blang Me|Blang Mé]] are the best known. Some of them are monumental graves for which marble has been used. A marble tombstone, standing at the head of the grave, bears the mention of names and facts in old script of mysterious characters. Remote in the water-land, this burial-place yet offers a royal rest under the high trees and remains a place full of dedication for the pious of the land. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries Samoedra and Pasé came under Atjeh, and the princes became subject to the sultan. When Atjeh in the course of time lost its power, an independence of princes and chiefs developed in all the regions outside Groot-Atjeh — a process that in some *landschappen* went so far that there came to be too many chiefs and petty chiefs.

### The Landschap


The *landschap* of Peureula (Perlak) is, in contrast to the neighbouring *landschappen* — such as [[Soengei Radja|Soengei Raja]], [[Peudawa Rajeu|Pedawa Raje]], [[Simpang Olim|Simpang Olim]] — an old Atjehnese settlement, and the pepper-cultivation is of old date there. One can imagine this, considering first that pepper has for centuries been a sought-after spice, and secondly that of old the princes of the land in the Indies were the great traders, receiving from the small people, taking in tribute and selling abroad. The Atjehnese chiefs were mostly men of business and have always been so. If they liked fine things, then the merchant from outside — the Chinese of Penang or Singapore — knew what he had to offer. If he brought chiefs into debt, they had only to have the products of the land ready — pepper especially — to deliver at a favourable price. Pepper was the old *stapel* of the country.

The *landschap* counts, according to the government's statement, 12.000 souls, of whom in the place Peureula about 800. What one sees there, in the *keude* (market-street), are the Chinese and their *toko's*; an Inlandse second-class school; a girls' school; a small clinic with ten beds, visited twice a week by the doctor of Langsa; the toll-office of the *oeléebalang*, the *landshoofd*. Outside, along the road, stand poor huts, in which a population lives that does not belong in the land. The great *kampongs* with better houses are not found along the road, but along the rivers.

### Pepper-Cultivation


The agriculture of the settled Atjehnese population is the cultivation of *padi* on the *blang* — the great open rice-field. On Java one would call it *tegal*, that is, a rain-fed rice-field. There are in all Atjeh very many *tegals*, but one gladly wishes to know of true *sawahs*. In Peureula small irrigation-works have recently been undertaken, of which one serves to improve a *wadoek* laid out in [[1906]] — that is, then was begun with a dyke. Now 250 hectares can be irrigated.

The population also makes many *ladangs* — a piece of forest, opened so far as necessary, to obtain one rice-harvest. This reckless rice-cultivation, in Sumatra still wanted and usual, has been restricted in Peureula by the rule that the laying out of a *ladang* is permitted only in the old pepper-gardens, which the *ladang*-makers must first clean. That is an excellent measure, by which the abandoned and ruined pepper-gardens do not degenerate into wilderness.

### The Petroleum of Perlak


Near [[Rantau Pandjang|Rantau Pandjang]], which lies north-west of Peureula, officers of the man-of-war *Timor* found the petroleum-spring in May [[1875]], led thither by Atjehnese chiefs. Where the oil welled up, the spring was fenced. The oil ran underground into a wooden basin, closed by a hatch. The crude oil collected in the basin was twice a day skimmed from the water with buckets of banana-leaf. Through a hole in the bottom of the bucket the scooped-up water ran out, and as soon as the brown-green oil began to run, the bucket was held over a hollow bamboo. One man could carry two or three hollow bamboos on his back to Rantau Pandjang, where the oil was sold to traders of Perlak and elsewhere. The further transport was in large earthen pots. Whoever walked with a bamboo full of oil had no need to fear a tiger's attack, for these animals of Perlak shun the smell of oil. The yield of the spring had once been 60 *gantang*; in [[1875]] it was 40 *gantang* — about one *picol* — which at Rantau Pandjang brought nearly six dollars. The chief had a monthly income of two hundred dollars from it. Besides the principal spring there were three others in Perlak.

There now lie the oil-terrains of the [[Perlak Petroleum-Mij|Perlak Petroleum-Mij]], which the [[Bataafsche Petroleum-Mij|Bataafsche Petroleum-Mij]] exploits, and the terrains of the famous [[Zuid-Perlak Petroleum-Mij|Zuid-Perlak Petroleum-Mij]]. The Bataafsche does not process the oil on the spot but pumps it through a 120 km pipe-line to [[Pangkalan Brandan|Pangkalan Brandan]]. Only the gases are compressed at Rantau Pandjang into oil, which is likewise pumped away. There is a substantial settlement there with about thirty Europeans.

### The Oeléebalang


The *oeléebalang* of Peureula (Perlak) at the time of Broersma's visit was [[Teukoe Tjhi Mohamad Thajeb|Teukoe Tjhi Mohamad Thajeb]], who had succeeded his father [[Teukoe Tjhi Perlak|Teukoe Tjhi Perlak]] in [[1913]] at the moment when the oil-cijns was regularised into the *landschapskas* and the chief received a fixed income. From the oil the *oeléebalang* had drawn his riches, *'die toch misschien wel overschat zijn'* — *which perhaps may have been overrated.* After [[1912]] the petroleum-cijns came into the *landschapskas* and the *oeléebalang* received a fixed income.
