**Priaman** — modern **Pariaman**, the historic pepper port on the west coast of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] south of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — was one of the chief pepper ports of the Sumatran west coast in the early-modern period and a port of call of the Dutch East India trade at Atjeh. Priaman was visited by [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]] in [[1600]] without result, by the [[Jan Grenier|Jan Grenier]] fleet in February [[1602]] without result, by [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]]'s [[James Lancaster's Atjeh visit (1602)|English East India Company fleet of 1602]] in search of additional pepper, and by the Zeeland ships on their way to [[Bantam|Bantam]] after the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|August 1601 mission]]. Priaman is one of the principal west-coast pepper ports named in Van der Woude's chapter "Atjeh. Djohor. Malaka." in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## The 1600 Van Caerden visit

[[Paulus van Caerden|Van Caerden]], sent by his admiral [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] to the west coast of Sumatra, "bezocht daar zonder resultaat Priaman, Tikoe en Pasaman, waar overal slechts weinig peper in voorraad was". The visit was the first Dutch commercial call at Priaman and showed that the west-coast pepper ports, like Atjeh itself, were subject to seasonal fluctuations in supply. Van Caerden's failure at Priaman, [[Tikoe|Tikoe]] and [[Pasaman|Pasaman]] was the proximate cause of his decision to try Atjeh for cargo, "hoewel hij wist wat [[Cornelis de Houtman|De Houtman]] daar was overkomen".

## The 1601 Westkust closure

The west-coast pepper ports were closed to foreigners except at Atjeh. When [[Jan Grenier|Jan Grenier]] arrived off [[Tikoe|Tikoe]] on [[19 February]] [[1602]] on his way from Atjeh to [[Bantam|Bantam]], he could buy no pepper because trade with foreigners in the west-coast ports was forbidden except at Atjeh. The closure was the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s policy: he sought to centralise the European pepper trade at Atjeh, where he could control it through the sabander and the shore batteries. The policy was largely effective in the early Dutch-Indies period: the Dutch and English were forced to call at Atjeh for their pepper, and the west-coast ports could be visited only when the sultan's enforcement lapsed.

## The 1602 Lancaster visit

When [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]] found the pepper prices at [[Acheh|Atjeh]] higher than his pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] had led him to expect, and his capital insufficient for a full cargo, he sent one of his ships to Priaman on the west coast of Sumatra to make up the deficit. The visit shows that the west-coast ports — though formally closed to foreigners — were sometimes accessible to European traders, particularly when the sultan's enforcement was weak or when the European captain had the means to circumvent it. Lancaster's use of Priaman was the principal English instance of the west-coast pepper trade in the early English East India Company period.

## Significance

Priaman is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal west-coast pepper port of Sumatra south of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the early-modern period and a port of call of the Dutch and English East India trade. The port's seasonal fluctuations in supply, the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s policy of centralising the European pepper trade at Atjeh, and the occasional accessibility of the west-coast ports to European traders — as in [[James Lancaster|Lancaster]]'s [[1602]] visit — together frame the early-modern commercial geography of the Sumatran pepper trade. Priaman's role in the early Dutch-Indies trade would grow in the seventeenth century as the Dutch shifted their pepper sourcing from Atjeh to the west coast and to [[Bantam|Bantam]] and the Sunda islands. The [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme of monopoly-by-force, applied at [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] after [[1609]], would also be applied to the west-coast pepper ports in the [[1620s]] and [[1630s]].
