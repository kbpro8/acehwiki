The colonial capital of the Dutch East Indies, mentioned throughout as the centre of Dutch administration and scholarship. Van den Berg's Catalogue of Malay MSS at Batavia is cited frequently. 'A copy of the [[Menangkabau]] Malim Diman preserved in the library of the Batavian Association.' The Malay MSS of the Batavian Association (Batavian Society) are cited for multiple hikayats. The orang melahin (clairvoyants) of Batavia are mentioned. The prang desa (village war) of [[Java]] at Batavia has been brought under control. The expression 'Kabayan' is often heard in the kampongs of Batavia. In the broader sense: 'even up to the present time Arabs who are revered as kramat by their countrymen' are found at Batavia.

**Batavia** (Acehnese: *Pentawi*; now Jakarta, Indonesia) was the colonial capital of Netherlands India and the administrative seat of the Dutch East India Company and later the Dutch colonial government. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] worked up his research materials at Batavia in [[1892]] before the original Dutch edition of *The Achehnese* was published in [[1893]]–[[1894]].

## Acehnese Understanding of Batavia

The [[Acehnese People|Acehnese]] used the word *Gömpeuni* (their pronunciation of "Compagnie" — the Dutch East India Company) to signify the Dutch Government throughout the whole country. Many of the people believed *Gömpeuni* to be the official title of the representative of that government at Batavia (Pentawi). Others used the word in the general sense of "government" and spoke of *gömpeuni Blanda* (Dutch government) or *gömpeuni Inggrëh* (English government). The military meaning of the word was also known to the Acehnese, who used *gömpeuni* in the collective sense of "soldiers."

A member of the same Sayyid *kramat* family — mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje in the context of religious veneration — lay buried at Luar Batang in Batavia, where his tomb was widely venerated.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Batavia

**Batavia** was the colonial capital of the Dutch East Indies — the modern city of Jakarta, on the north-west coast of [[Java]] — and one of the chief places of reference in *The Achehnese* Vol. I. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] worked up his materials there in [[1893]]–[[1894]] for the first appearance of his treatise, and the two maps that accompanied the English edition were prepared in the topographical bureau at Batavia. Throughout the book the city is cited as a typical Mohammedan centre of the East Indian Archipelago whose practices may be set beside those of [[Acheh]].

Batavia is one of the places where marriage contracts are concluded by *tahkim* (authorisation of an agent), since there is no legally appointed official *wali*; here "amateur" marriage-makers are common, and the *ta'liq* adat is in full vigour. The *pangulu* system of Java, with its district *pangulus* and their subordinate *ketip*, discharges in Batavia the functions that in Acheh are performed by the *teungku meunasah*; and the noisy *ratib Samman* — the litany named after the saint of [[Medina]] — may be heard almost every week in many Batavian kampongs. In the matter of the calendar, the method of computing (*hisab*) was long since substituted for the method of observation (*ruya*) in Batavia, according to Dr. A. B. Cohen Stuart, under the same influence of [[Mekka]] and [[Hadramaut]] that has more recently spread the *ruya* doctrine through the Archipelago.

The city is also a place of sacred tombs and popular superstition. A member of the same sayyid family that is settled in [[Pidie]] lies buried at Luar Batang in Batavia, and his tomb is widely venerated. Of the named cannon that the Mohammedan peoples of the archipelago regard more or less as personified — and even worship as sacred after a time — Snouck Hurgronje instances **Si Penjagio** at Batavia, with its consorts Si Amuk in [[Banten]] and Setomi at Surakarta, and many more. The same comparative habit recurs in the calendar: the Malay word *longtang*, which means "interval" in the Javanese month-names, curiously enough means "a narrow drain or ditch" in the Malay of Batavia as well as of [[Singapore]].

In short, Batavia serves in the book both as the city where the work was composed and as the standing example of the more cosmopolitan, more thoroughly Islamised, and more bureaucratically regulated Mohammedanism of Java — against which the indigenous and in many respects archaic institutions of Acheh stand out in sharp relief.
**Batavia** (modern **Jakarta**) was the colonial capital of the [[Netherlands India|Dutch East Indies]] and the seat of the Governor-General, whom the Achehnese — extending the meaning of the Dutch East India Company's name — called the **Peutawi**. Throughout *The Achehnese* the city is invoked both as the seat of the [[Holland / Netherlands|Dutch]] "Gompeuni" against which the [[Achehnese War|war in Acheh]] was fought, and as a centre of Mohammedan learning and custom which [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] repeatedly brings into comparison with the usages of [[Acheh]] and [[Java]]. It was at Batavia that the author himself worked up his materials in [[1893]]–[[1894]], and so produced the treatise of which the English translation was published in [[1906]].

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Batavia

**Batavia** (modern **Jakarta**) was the capital of the [[Netherlands India|Dutch East Indies]] and the seat of the Governor-General and Council, whose authority extended over the Dutch possessions in the Eastern Archipelago. In P. H. van der Kemp's *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819*, Batavia is the centre from which Dutch policy towards [[Acheh]] and the surrounding waters was directed throughout the period from the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|restoration of Dutch rule]] in 1816 to the [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] of 1827-1828.

## An "anti-Batavia"

Van der Kemp introduces Batavia by contrast: when [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] founded [[Singapore]] in February [[1819]], "he had, in that short time, accomplished a great deed for his country; truly, he had laid the foundation for an important city, an **anti-Batavia**." The phrase captures the British intention that Singapore should rival and outdo the Dutch colonial capital as an entrepôt of the Eastern trade — a design that Batavia, for its part, struggled to counter. Raffles was accustomed to defend his unauthorised actions to the Batavia government "in long writings," and Van der Kemp elsewhere speaks of him as wont "to plead his wrongful acts upon our government at Batavia."

## The seat of Wolterbeek and Van der Capellen

It was to Batavia that [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]], the Dutch commissioner, returned after his mission of [[1818]] which produced the contract with the Sultan of [[Riouw|Itiouw]]; and it was from Batavia that the order of 30 September [[1819]] was sent to [[Penang]] about the Dutch practice of employing Malay and Javanese crews, with whose mutiny the *Wilhelmina* affair was only the most tragic instance. [[Timmerman Thyssen|Timmerman Thyssen]], governor of [[Malacca]], wrote privately to Wolterbeek at Batavia on 16 March [[1819]] with the alarming news that three hundred troops had been seen leaving Penang for the [[Karimon Islands|Karimons]], and that Raffles was said to be on his way to Acheh with four hundred more. The Governor-General [[Godert Alexander Gerard Philip van der Capellen|Van der Capellen]], "having taken note of Thyssen's complaints," wrote privately on 9 January [[1819]] from Buitenzorg to Wolterbeek at Batavia: "I have learned with displeasure the conduct of some officials at Malacca. T. must stand firm. Every Government in India must in general be so."

## Orders on the Wilhelmina affair

On Batavia's instructions Thyssen addressed the [[Penang|Pinang]] government on 20 January [[1821]] demanding the surrender of the *Wilhelmina*'s mutineers and the restitution of the stolen specie; the dispatch of the *Irene* under [[Colonel E. Lucas|Colonel Lucas]] was Batavia's doing. After eleven mutineers had been handed over by [[Sartorius|Sartorius]] and sent on to Batavia, "the examination of these persons appears to have given our government a less favourable impression of Syf's proceedings"; hence the orders from Batavia that set the Lucas mission in motion. Sultan [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Djohor]] himself, Van der Kemp notes, asserted that "the Batavian government had abstained from any offer" of help at [[Pedir]], contradicting Raffles's allegations.

## After the 1824 treaty: salt, the Bellona, and Du Bus

By the later 1820s Batavia had become, in British eyes, the seat of a deliberate hostility to Singapore. [[John Prince|John Prince]], the former resident of Singapore, writing from Batavia on 29 November [[1827]], reported that [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Commissioner-General Du Bus]] planned to send the frigate *Bellona* to the [[Sumatra's East Coast|East Coast of Sumatra]] to draw native trade to [[Riouw|Rhio]] away from Singapore — including a drastic reduction in the price of Java salt from 70 to 30 rupees per coyan. "The feeling of the Netherlands Government at Batavia towards Singapore is so truly inimical, from the injury it has caused to their colonial commerce, that there are no exertions or pains they would hesitate to take for its destruction." Van der Kemp, in a footnote, dismisses the salt-price reduction as a measure adopted on the advice of [[Van de Graaff|Van de Graaff]] to keep Java salt competitive with Siamese, Makassarese and Bimanese salt on the [[Netherlands India|Buitenbezittingen]], and denies that it was specifically directed against Singapore. After the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 1824]], Falck wrote to Elout that Sumatra's administration could now "better come from nearby Batavia than from the gentlemen at [[Calcutta]], who were always thinking of other things."
