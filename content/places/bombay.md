## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Hindustan

**Hindustan** — the Indian subcontinent — is mentioned throughout *The Achehnese* as the country from which Islam was first introduced into the [[East Indian Archipelago|East Indian Archipelago]], and as the source of the Shi'ite tendencies and popular stories which so deeply coloured the Islam of the Archipelago. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] uses the term more specifically than the broader "British India": Hindustan is the country of the Deccani Moslims portrayed in the *Qanoon-e-islam* of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]], and of those peoples of southern India whence the creed of Islam made its first advances towards the Eastern Archipelago.

### The first introduction of Islam

Islam as first introduced into the Archipelago from Hindustan "has always shown Shi'ite tendencies." This Shi'ite provenance is treated by Snouck as one of the master keys to the religious life of the Archipelago: the reverence paid to the descendants of the Prophet — the sayyids and sharifs — the popular stories of [[Amir Hamzah|Amir Hamzah]] and Mohammad ibnu'l-Hanafiyyah, the Hasan-Husain feasts, and the choice of the eighth month for the commemoration of the dead, are all in his account to be traced to the lasting influence of Hindustani Islam. The creed of Islam, he argues, "would appear to have made its first advances towards the Eastern Archipelago" from the coastlands of the southern part of British India — that is, from the [[Deccan|Deccan]].

### The mad processions of Hindustan

The mad processions of the Hasan-Husain feast are described by Snouck as "particularly common in Hindustan, and remind one more of a fair or carnival than of a funeral pageant." These processions, with their cries of *Husain! Husain!*, their exposure of the actor who takes the part of the murderer of Husain to actual violence from the crowd, and their theatrical representations of the events of Kerbela, "specially belong to Persia and the Shi'ite portions of British India." Such observances are in sharp contrast with the more sober ritual of orthodox Islam; they are, however, characteristic of those portions of Hindustan where Shi'ite influence has been strongest.

### The chosen dish of Ashura

The cooking of special viands on the Ashura day is one of the observances which the Islam of the Archipelago shares with Hindustan. In Hindustan, the chosen dish is the *khichri*; in [[Cairo|Cairo]] it is called *hubub* ("seeds" or "grains"); in [[Java|Java]] the *bubur sura* consists of various grains or seeds. The Achehnese, too, have their own Ashura dish. The spread of this observance, with its local variations, is treated by Snouck as evidence of the common origin of the popular Islam of the Archipelago in the Islam of Hindustan.

### The predeclared ta'liq

The custom by which a husband, immediately after the marriage ceremony, declares his liability to divorce on specified grounds — the so-called predeclared *ta'liq* — is treated by Snouck as one of the institutions which the Archipelago shares with Hindustan. Such predeclared and morally compulsory *ta'liqs* are by no means rare in the [[Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]], and the same custom, Snouck suggests, would "in all probability be met with in those parts of Hindustan whence Islam was introduced into the E. Indian Archipelago." The same observation is made for the *ta'liq* in general: it is the characteristic method by which the Mohammedan law has been adapted, throughout the Archipelago, to mitigate the miseries of a wife abandoned by her husband.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — India / Hindostan

**India** — or, in the older European and Achehnese usage, **Hindostan** — is the great subcontinent whose Mohammedan and Hindu inhabitants had, from the earliest times of the Achehnese kingdom, supplied both the religion and a portion of the commercial population of [[Acheh|Acheh]]. Snouck Hurgronje's account of India in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* is accordingly of two kinds: it presents India as the original source of the Islamisation of Acheh, and it describes the contemporary Hindu and Mohammedan settlers (the Klings, the Chetties, the Mohammedan Klings from [[Madras|Madras]] and Malabar) who continued to trade in the country.
**India** — called by Snouck Hurgronje and his translator **Hindostan** — is mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the principal source from which the Mohammedan religion, and along with it a numerous class of Indian settlers and influences, reached [[Acheh|Acheh]] and the rest of the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]]. The reference occurs in the ethnological chapter of the Introduction, where Snouck discusses the Hindu element in Achehnese origins and the long-continuing commercial and religious intercourse between Acheh and the Indian sub-continent.

### Hindostan as the Source of Achehnese Islam

In the chapter on the composition of the Achehnese population, Snouck argues that Hinduism had, for a considerable time, exercised a direct or indirect influence on the language and civilisation of Acheh, though there is but little trace of such influence remaining in the present popular traditions and institutions. "Even in Mohammedan times," he continues, "there are numerous indications of contact with the inhabitants of India; it is indeed more than probable that Acheh, like other countries of the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]], was mohammedanised from Hindostan." The theory of an Indian, and more particularly a Gujerati or Bengali, origin for the Islam of the Archipelago was already, in Snouck's time, the common opinion of Orientalist scholarship, and the Achehnese case was regarded as one of its clearest illustrations.

### The Kling and Chetty Settlers

The same chapter describes the contemporary Indian settlers in Acheh. "Not only Mohammedan Klings and people from [[Madras|Madras]] and Malabar, but also heathen Klings, Chetties, and other Hindus, have carried on trade in Acheh down to the present time, and there has been from first to last no serious opposition to the permanent establishment in the country of such kafirs, harmless as they were from a political point of view." The Chetties — the money-lending caste of Southern India, as the translator explains — were a familiar feature of the commercial life of [[Banda Acheh|Banda-Acheh]], and the Mohammedan Klings, known in Achehnese as *Kling kaphé* or simply *Kling*, were among the principal trading communities of the port.

### The Hindu Element in the Achehnese

Snouck is at pains to insist that the Hindu element in the Achehnese population, though real, has left few traces in the popular traditions and institutions of the country. The Achehnese themselves, in his account, gather nothing of their origin from Hindu sources; the etymological lore by which they explain the name of their own country derives it from *Acheh* or from Arabic *ʿajam*, not from any Sanskrit original. Yet the presence of Hindu blood in the Achehnese is, in Snouck's view, "past all doubt," and the continued immigration of Kling and Chetty settlers, down to the present time, is one of the channels by which that element has been maintained.

### The Comparative Use of Indian References

The mention of India in *The Achehnese* is not confined to the historical and ethnographical chapters. In the chapters on adat and law, India is repeatedly invoked as a point of comparison — most often in connection with the parallel institutions of the Deccan, the Malabar coast, and the Coromandel shore. The Klings of [[Banda Acheh|Banda-Acheh]] appear in the chapter on the marriage ceremony as one of the stranger-communities with whom the better class of Achehnese objected to intermarry, and the parallel is drawn between the Achehnese dislike of giving daughters to Klings and the analogous restrictions in other parts of the Archipelago. India, in short, is in Vol. I at once a source, a continuous presence, and a comparative reference; the country whose Mohammedan and Hindu inhabitants had supplied Acheh with religion, traders, and a portion of its blood, and whose contemporary institutions furnished the comparative material by which the singularity of Achehnese adat could be brought into relief.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — India (Southern India)

**India** — and more particularly **Southern India**, including the **Deccan** and the **Coromandel** or Madras coast — is, in *The Achehnese*, the country from which the creed of Islam first made its way to the Eastern Archipelago, and the source of a great number of the religious ideas, festivals and popular superstitions which distinguished Achehnese Mohammedanism from the more austere Arab form of the faith. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] treats southern India as a more powerful formative influence on the Islam of the Archipelago than any other agency, Arabia included.

The author's argument rests on three classes of evidence. First, the long-continued commercial intercourse between Acheh and southern India: "Mohammedan Klings and people from [[Madras]] and Malabar, but also heathen Klings, Chetties and other Hindus, have carried on trade in Acheh down to the present time, and there has been from first to last no serious opposition to the permanent establishment in the country of such kajirs, harmless as they were from a political point of view." From this it follows, in Snouck Hurgronje's view, that "Acheh, like other countries of the Indian Archipelago, was mohammedanized from Hindostan."

Second, the direct comparison of Achehnese religious usages with those described in the **Qanoon-e-islam** of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Ja'far Shurreef]] (translated by [[G.A. Herklots|G. A. Herklots]], second edition, Madras, [[1863]]). This work relates "to a non-Shi'ite people, the inhabitants of the coastlands of the Southern part of British India, whence the creed of Islam would appear to have made its first advances towards the Eastern Archipelago." The Mohammedans of the Deccan, whose manners are portrayed in this work, "are Shafi'ites just like those of the Malay Archipelago, but their national ideas and customs have arisen to a great extent under strong Shi'ite influences. As is clear from a comparison of Ja'far Shurreef's book with what we actually find in Netherlands-India, these adventitious additions to their creed were adopted by the Malay and Javanese converts with just as much readiness as the fundamental truths of the Shafi'ite law." The Hasan-Husain festival, in particular, "in different parts of Netherlands-India and especially on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] of Sumatra (Padang, Bencoolen etc.) is celebrated on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the Qanoon-e-islam."

Third, the popular religious literature of the Archipelago — the tales of [[Amir Hamzah]] (the uncle of Mohammad), of [[Mohammad Ibnu'l-Hanafiyyah]] (the son of Ali), of the hero [[Sam'un|Sam'un]], of [[Raja Badar]] and many more — is traced to southern India. "It is more particularly in British India that works of this sort are to be met with, nor is it possible that they should have been disseminated to such an extent in any country closer to Arabia." The influence of the Deccan form of Mohammedanism is, in short, "superior in force to that of any other agency", and may be gathered from "the character of the popular religious literature, even were there no other proof."

Southern India is also the source of the Achehnese cult of **Tuan Meurasab** (in his own country called Kadir Wall Sahib), whose tomb is at Nagore on the Coromandel coast, and whose anniversary is kept in Acheh on the 9th–11th days of the month Jumadilawal. He "has also many worshippers in Acheh, seemingly through the influence of those inhabitants of Southern India who introduced here the creed of Islam." Vows of gold or silver hands, made to this saint for the recovery of sick children, are transmitted through Kling traders journeying to Madras. The word *kanduri* itself — the central term of Achehnese religious feast-life — is one of those "imported into the Archipelago from India", and the Achehnese usage corresponds closely to the *sedekah*, *slametan* or *kenduri* of the Javanese and Sundanese.
