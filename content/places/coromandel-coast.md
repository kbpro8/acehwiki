The **Coromandel coast** — the stretch of the south-eastern shore of [[India|Southern India]] which faces the Bay of Bengal — is named in Vol. I of *The Achehnese* as the locality of the tomb of the saint **[[Tuan Meurasab]]**, whose *kanduri* (annual feast) is observed in [[Acheh]] on the 9th, 10th and 11th of the month of Jumada al-Thani. The saint's tomb is at **[[Nagore]]** on the Coromandel coast, and his worship in Acheh is ascribed by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] to the influence of those inhabitants of Southern India who introduced the creed of Islam into the country.

The Coromandel coast was, throughout the medieval and early modern period, the principal seat of the Muslim commercial communities of Southern India — the Maraikkayars, the Labbais and other Tamil-speaking Muslim groups whose trade carried them across the Bay of Bengal to the ports of the [[East Indian Archipelago|Eastern Archipelago]]. Their influence in Acheh is attested not only by the cult of Tuan Meurasab but also by the wider current of South-Indian Mohammedan forms — the *peusijuk*, the *kanduri* system, the recitation of the *ratib samman* — which Vol. I repeatedly traces to the [[Deccan|Deccani]] and Coromandel coasts.

Snouck's observation regarding the days of Tuan Meurasab forms part of his wider discussion of saints' days in Acheh. He notes that "what I have observed elsewhere with regard to feasts of saints at [[Mecca|Mekka]], is equally true of these 'saints' days' in Acheh — nay throughout the whole Moslem world." The people, he continues, have no clear idea of what the "day" of a saint really is; they say it is the *haul* or anniversary of the death of the holy man, but some saints have more than one *haul* per annum, and the exact day of death is in many cases unknown. The days of Tuan Meurasab at Nagore are one of the more striking examples of this confusion, since three consecutive days are kept in his honour without any certain knowledge of which is the true anniversary.

The Coromandel coast is thus, in Vol. I, a piece of the wider Indian-Ocean Mohammedan world whose presence in Acheh is visible at once in the cult of saints, in the calendar of holy days, and in the very form of the religious customs of the country. Together with [[Bengal]], the [[Malabar]] coast, and the [[Deccan]], it forms part of the South-Indian background against which the Islam of Acheh must be read.
