**Gunong Jorai** (modern Malay *Gunung Jerai*, often called in English the "Kedah Peak") is a mountain in the Malay state of [[Kedah|Kedah]] on the Malay Peninsula, mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I only in a translator's footnote recording a personal observation by A. W. S. O'Sullivan.

The reference occurs in the discussion of *tapa* — the religious seclusion practised by Achehnese ulamas and holy men. Snouck, in his account of [[Habib Samalanga|Habib Samalanga's]] rivalry with [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]], had occasion to explain that *tapa* in the sense of absolute hermitical seclusion is admired and respected among the Malays of the peninsula, but very seldom practised by them. O'Sullivan, in a footnote, confirms the observation from his own experience: "When ascending Gunong Jorai in [[Kedah]] I heard of a holy hermit there who had spoken to no human being for five years; but he was said to be a stranger. Tapa for short periods is however popular."

Gunong Jorai is the most conspicuous landmark on the coast of Kedah, rising steeply from the coastal plain to a height of some 3,800 feet, and is visible far out to sea. It has been associated, from the Hindu-Buddhist period down to modern times, with retreats and hermitages. The Malay *Jerai* (the modern spelling of the form *Jorai* preserved in Snouck's text) is the name by which the mountain is now known in Malaysian geographical usage. The isolated stranger reported by O'Sullivan as dwelling there in absolute silence for five years is offered as a single exceptional instance of that complete withdrawal from human intercourse which, though admired in theory, is so rarely practised by Malays that its actual occurrence on the peninsula required to be attested by a foreign witness.

The comparative aside is significant for the study of religious practice in the wider Malay world. Snouck's argument is that, while short-period *tapa* is widespread and popular among the Malays, the life-long hermitical seclusion so much admired in the literary tradition is practically unknown among them save in the persons of strangers. The Gunong Jorai hermit — said to be a foreigner — thus becomes a limiting case that illustrates the rule. For Acheh itself, by contrast, the practice of prolonged *tapa* was not only admired but actually undertaken by figures such as [[Habib Samalanga]] and [[Teungku Lam Guha|Teungku Lam Guha]], who are recorded to have spent successive years in the cave of the [[Krueng Daroy|Daroy]].

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Gunong Jorai

**Gunong Jorai** (modern Gunung Jerai, also known as Kedah Peak) is a mountain in the [[Kedah|Kedah]] region of the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] mentioned by the translator of *The Achehnese* in a footnote concerning the practice of *tapa*, or absolute hermitical seclusion, among the Malays of the peninsula.

### The Holy Hermit of Gunong Jorai

In the course of Snouck Hurgronje's account of the Achehnese ulama [[Habib Samalanga|Habib Samalanga]] — whose speciality, in his competition with [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] for the leadership of the [[Acheh War|jihad]], was the practice of *tapa* — the translator inserts a footnote in which *tapa* in the sense of absolute hermitical seclusion is said to be "admired and respected, but very seldom practised by the Malays of the peninsula." The translator continues: "When ascending Gunong Jorai in Kedah I heard of a holy hermit there who had spoken to no human being for five years; but he was said to be a stranger. *Tapa* for short periods is however popular."

The footnote is one of the few passages in Vol. I in which the translator steps out from behind Snouck's text to add a comparative observation from his own Malayan experience. It serves to confirm the comparative rarity of prolonged hermitical seclusion in the peninsula, while at the same time acknowledging Gunong Jorai as one of those mountains — like the [[Eumpee Lulu|Eumpee Lulu]] of [[Great Acheh|Great Acheh]] — to which such holiness was traditionally attached.

### Gunong Jorai in the Geography of Kedah

Gunong Jorai (Gunung Jerai) is the most conspicuous landmark of the coastal plain of [[Kedah|Kedah]], rising to nearly four thousand feet above the mangrove flats and padi-fields that border the Strait of [[Malacca|Malacca]]. Visible far out to sea, it served for centuries as a navigational mark for shipping in the strait, and its commanding silhouette gave it a natural place in the cosmology of the Malay and Indian traders who frequented the [[Kedah|Kedah]] coast. Hindu, Buddhist, and Muslim holy men have in turn been drawn to its slopes; the name *Kadaram* (the Sanskrit designation of the Kedah kingdom) was long supposed by some scholars to be connected with the Sanskrit *giri* ("mountain") — though the etymology is uncertain.

### The Place of the Reference

The mention of Gunong Jorai in *The Achehnese* is incidental to the comparison being drawn between the *tapa* of the Achehnese ulama and the parallel practices of the Malay peninsula. Its function in the work is to confirm, from the translator's own observation, the survival of the ideal of the hermit-saint in the Malay world — an ideal which the Achehnese themselves seldom realised in its most extreme form, but which they revered and, in the case of a man like Habib Samalanga, occasionally approximated.
