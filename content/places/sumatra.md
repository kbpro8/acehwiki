Sumatra is the island context of the whole book. In the literary works, '[[Chamtalira]]' (a corruption of Sumatra) is the kingdom of the fictional princes in the hikayats Eseukanda Ali, Nun [[Parisi]], and elsewhere: 'By [[Chamtalira]] the Achehnese mean the same that is called Sumatra in the writings of Marco Polo and Ibn Batutah.' Also: Shamsuddin of Sumatra (= [[Pase]]) was the great heretical mystic at the Achehnese court. 'In Sumatra some even give their tariqah the special name of Qushashite.' The mountain Kaf (Arab. Qaf) appears in hikayats as bordering on the territory of the jens. The [[Gulitan Sagob]] (in the [[Hikayat Gumba Meuih]]) is described as being 'in Sumatra, according to the Achehnese.' The Malay Peninsula (Malayan Countries) The Malay Peninsula appears throughout as a major point of reference, both historically ([[Acheh]]'s conquests) and culturally (Malay literature, tariqahs, comparative customs). '[[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] conquered, among other littoral Malay States, [[Johor]] ([[1613]]) and [[Pahang]] ([[1618]]), thus gaining for [[Acheh]] an authority over the Malay Peninsula which was only balanced by that of the Portuguese who had settled at [[Malacca]] a century earlier.' [[Hamzah Pansuri]]'s writings 'are to be met with both in [[Acheh]] and in Malay countries.' The Malay hikayats supply the Achehnese market with fresh romantic material. Malay textbooks (Masailah, Bidayah, Qirat al-mustaqim) are used for elementary religious instruction in [[Acheh]] because there are few Achehnese equivalents. The Naqshibandiyyah and Qadiriyyah tariqahs flourish in West [[Java]] and in Deli and Langkat.

**Sumatra** (also *Sumatera*) is the large island that forms the western anchor of the Indonesian archipelago. [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] occupied its northwestern tip, with the limits of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Acehnese kingdom]] on the west coast extending south to [[Baros]] and on the east coast to [[Teumieng]], beyond which lay the territories of neighbouring peoples.

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes that the limits of the whole Acehnese kingdom in Sumatra were placed by the Acehnese themselves at [[Teumieng]] (Tamiang) on the East Coast and at [[Baros]] (or another point) on the West Coast, marking the boundary between Acehnese and [[Menangkabau]] territories.

Sumatra's varied geography — from the highland interior to the coastal lowlands — was directly reflected in the social distinction between the *ureuëng tunöng* (highlanders) and the *ureuëng baröh* (lowlanders) of [[Great Acheh]]. The highlands supplied much of the interior population and produced distinct customs, language, and superstitions compared to the coastal people.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sumatra

**Sumatra** is one of the great islands of the [[East Indian Archipelago]] and the geographical seat of [[Acheh]], which occupies its northern extremity. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] frequently refers to Sumatra as a whole — to its several peoples, its rice-cultivation, its west-coast islands, and its slave-trade — in order to throw into relief the distinctive features of the Achehnese.

The student of Mohammedan law in Sumatra is part of a wider religious landscape. Students in the religious schools of Sumatra who leave their native places to pursue their studies are called *gharim* in some parts. The *tariqahs*, or mystic associations, so popular in other parts of Sumatra, do not flourish in Acheh, though sundry Achehnese *hajis* have enrolled themselves as members of a *tariqah* in [[Mecca]]. The Minhaj al-'abidin, a work much read in certain mystic circles of Sumatra, is named in illustration of the literary tastes of those who frequent such circles. Other Sumatran peoples, including the [[Batak]] groups to the south of Acheh, are noticed as objects of the forced conversion that Mohammedan chiefs were accustomed to carry out as a fulfilment of the joint and several obligation of the Jihad or holy war — a duty universally approved of in the Archipelago.

Sumatra is the theatre of Achehnese naval and commercial enterprise. With the short-lived period of prosperity ended all such glorious expeditions as those undertaken in the 17th century against [[Malacca]] and Pahang, but the bold seafarers and pirates of Acheh continued to make themselves dreaded along the coasts of Sumatra and among the surrounding islands. The monopoly of trade claimed by the great sultans could in the end no longer be enforced. The Achehnese slave-traders were until quite recently the terror of [[Nias]] and the adjacent islands off the west coast of Sumatra, kidnapping their inhabitants in hundreds up to a few years before the period of which Snouck Hurgronje writes, and continuing to purchase them surreptitiously in smaller numbers.

Agricultural custom also varies across the island. *Sawah*, the Malay word for irrigated rice-fields used in the southern parts of the Peninsula (Johor, [[Malacca]] etc.) and also in [[Java]], gives way in [[Penang]], Province Wellesley, [[Kedah]] etc. — and in Acheh — to the word *blang*. The system of rice-cultivation in Acheh as compared with that in Java may be called, if not rational, at least rationalistic; the various tasks connected with the cultivation of rice are in the lowlands performed by men only, while in [[Pidie]], [[Daya]] and some parts of the highlands the planting-out (*pula*) is left to the women. In these wider Sumatran comparisons Sumatra figures not merely as the geographic setting of Acheh but as a comparative field in which its institutions can be measured against those of neighbouring peoples.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sumatra

**Sumatra** is the great island of the [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]] on which [[Acheh]] occupies the northern extremity. Throughout *The Achehnese* it serves both as the geographical setting of the Achehnese kingdom and as a frame of comparison for the institutions of Acheh with those of its Sumatran neighbours — the [[Minangkabau|Menangkabau]] of the central highlands, the Bataks of the interior, the Malays of the coasts, and the peoples of the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] dependencies. The Achehnese themselves placed the limits of their kingdom in Sumatra at [[Tamiang|Teumiang]] on the [[East Coast (of Acheh)|East Coast]] and at [[Baros]] on the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] — the latter being "whatever other point they regard as marking the boundary between the territory of the princes of Menangkabau and that of the Sultans of Acheh."

As a political arena, Sumatra was, in the nineteenth century, dominated by the [[Holland / Netherlands|Dutch]], who were "the one power which was settled in Sumatra and had brought the other territories in the island under its suzerainty or control." The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] guaranteed the security of trade and shipping in Acheh, and stipulated that Holland would respect the independence of the country — a stipulation which, combined with the "fanatical and treacherous population, turbulent and warlike to a degree unknown among the other races of the Archipelago," rendered the undertaking impossible to carry out. After a half-century of complaints by European and American traders, the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|new treaty of 1871]] left Holland free to act in Acheh, and so led to the [[Achehnese War|Dutch war]] of [[1873]] onwards. The Dutch position in Sumatra was thus the immediate political context of Snouck Hurgronje's work.

The comparative ethnography of Sumatra supplies several points of contrast with Acheh. The characteristic institutions regarding the law of person and inheritance which prevail among the Malays of the west coast of Sumatra — including the *ta'liq* marriage contract and the matrilineal institutions of the Menangkabau — are absent from Acheh, where the *ta'liq* is unknown and where the woman, instead of remaining in her mother's house, takes up residence with her husband. The *tariqahs* or mystic orders "so popular in other parts of Sumatra" do not flourish in Acheh, though sundry Achehnese *hajis* have enrolled themselves as members of a *tariqah* in [[Mecca]]. The stereotyped speeches which accompany important domestic occasions in Acheh — betrothal, marriage, the installation of chiefs — find their analogies in other parts of Sumatra and in Java, especially in the formal orations of the Menangkabau districts such as [[Kapau]].

The west coast of Sumatra also figures in *The Achehnese* as a region of related but distinct Muslim custom. The [[Hasan-Husain festival]] was, at the time of writing, celebrated in different parts of Netherlands India, "and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra ([[Padang]], [[Bencoolen]] etc.) … on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the *Qanoon-e-islam*." It was thought to have been introduced along the seaboard by the *sipahis* (sepoys) who immigrated during the English domination, though Snouck Hurgronje considers it probable that a Hasan-Husain feast had previously enjoyed much popularity both in Sumatra and elsewhere — a view supported by its observance at [[Trumon]] in the manner customary in the [[Deccan]]. The Achehnese slave-traders, until quite recently the terror of [[Nias]] and the adjacent islands, were another Sumatran-wide phenomenon of which Acheh was the most aggressive exponent.
