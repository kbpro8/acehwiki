Sumatra is the island context of the whole book. In the literary works, '[[Chamtalira]]' (a corruption of Sumatra) is the kingdom of the fictional princes in the hikayats Eseukanda Ali, Nun [[Parisi]], and elsewhere: 'By [[Chamtalira]] the Achehnese mean the same that is called Sumatra in the writings of Marco Polo and Ibn Batutah.' Also: Shamsuddin of Sumatra (= [[Pase]]) was the great heretical mystic at the Achehnese court. 'In Sumatra some even give their tariqah the special name of Qushashite.' The mountain Kaf (Arab. Qaf) appears in hikayats as bordering on the territory of the jens. The [[Gulitan Sagob]] (in the [[Hikayat Gumba Meuih]]) is described as being 'in Sumatra, according to the Achehnese.' The Malay Peninsula (Malayan Countries) The Malay Peninsula appears throughout as a major point of reference, both historically ([[Acheh]]'s conquests) and culturally (Malay literature, tariqahs, comparative customs). '[[Sultan Iskandar Muda]] conquered, among other littoral Malay States, [[Johor]] ([[1613]]) and [[Pahang]] ([[1618]]), thus gaining for [[Acheh]] an authority over the Malay Peninsula which was only balanced by that of the Portuguese who had settled at [[Malacca]] a century earlier.' [[Hamzah Pansuri]]'s writings 'are to be met with both in [[Acheh]] and in Malay countries.' The Malay hikayats supply the Achehnese market with fresh romantic material. Malay textbooks (Masailah, Bidayah, Qirat al-mustaqim) are used for elementary religious instruction in [[Acheh]] because there are few Achehnese equivalents. The Naqshibandiyyah and Qadiriyyah tariqahs flourish in West [[Java]] and in Deli and Langkat.

**Sumatra** (also *Sumatera*) is the large island that forms the western anchor of the Indonesian archipelago. [[Great Acheh|Acheh]] occupied its northwestern tip, with the limits of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Acehnese kingdom]] on the west coast extending south to [[Baros]] and on the east coast to [[Teumieng]], beyond which lay the territories of neighbouring peoples.

[[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes that the limits of the whole Acehnese kingdom in Sumatra were placed by the Acehnese themselves at [[Teumieng]] (Tamiang) on the East Coast and at [[Baros]] (or another point) on the West Coast, marking the boundary between Acehnese and [[Menangkabau]] territories.

Sumatra's varied geography — from the highland interior to the coastal lowlands — was directly reflected in the social distinction between the *ureuëng tunöng* (highlanders) and the *ureuëng baröh* (lowlanders) of [[Great Acheh]]. The highlands supplied much of the interior population and produced distinct customs, language, and superstitions compared to the coastal people.
