**The Atjehspoorweg** — the Atjeh railway (Dutch: *Atjehsche Staatsspoorwegen*) — was the colonial railway network built across the residency (Dutch: *gewest*) of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] in the early twentieth century for military and economic purposes, forming together with the [[Atjehweg|Great Atjeh Road (Dutch: *Groote Atjehweg*)]] and the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] a transport system that opened the interior of [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] to trade, settlement, and administrative integration with the wider [[Dutch East Indies|Netherlands-Indies]] state. Built in the closing phase and aftermath of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]], the railway became, by the [[1920s]], the principal carrier of the residency's export crops — pepper, copra, rubber, palm oil, and sisal — and of the contract labourers brought in to work the new plantations of the [[East Coast of Aceh|Oostkust]] and the borders of the [[Gajoland|Gajolanden]].

## Military Purpose and Early Construction

The Atjehspoorweg originated as a military line, built to support the operations of the [[Indische Leger|Royal Netherlands East Indies Army (Dutch: *Indische Leger*)]] in the protracted guerrilla phase of the [[Aceh War|Aceh War]]. The earliest section — the *smalspoorbaan* (narrow-gauge track) from [[Koeta-Radja|Kota Radja]] to [[Olehleh|Olehleh]] built in [[1874]]–[[1875]] by the Staf der Genie (Engineer Staff) under [[Cornelis Jacobus Snijders|Cornelis Jacobus Snijders]] — supplied the *bivakken* and *militaire posten* by which the successive Civil and Military Governors, from [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|van Heutsz]] onward, enforced the subjection of the *oelèëbalangschappen* of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. The line was subsequently extended in stages across the Goudberg plateau to the Noordkust and onward to the Oostkust, gradually displacing the older system of ox-cart convoys and the steam-tramway postenlinie (the [[Atjeh-tram|Atjeh-tram]]) as the principal means of military transport. The railway thus belongs, in its origin, to the same military-infrastructure project as the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] cut through the jungle on military command under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in the years around [[1904]].

## Economic Role and the Plantation Boom

After the formal close of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] in [[1904]] and the subsequent [[Pacification of Atjeh|pacificatie]] under [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]], the Atjehspoorweg shed its military role and became, in the words of *De Buitenbezittingen*, "in de voornaamste plaats economische beteekenis" — of primarily economic significance. The line connected the productive interior and the booming plantation region around [[Langsa|Langsa]] with the port of [[Belawan|Belawan]] and the deep-water anchorage of [[Sabang|Sabang]] on [[Pulo We|Pulo We]], enabling the cost-effective export of the rubber, palm-oil, sisal, and copra that the new European estates — opened under the [[Agrarische Wet 1870|Agrarian Law of 1870 (Dutch: *Agrarische Wet*)]] — were beginning to produce on the east-coast lowlands. By the 1920s the railway had become the principal carrier of the *bevolkingscultures* (smallholder crops) on which the *gewest*'s economy now rested, with subsidised tariffs for pepper, rice, and coconuts, and payments in kind to the Atjehnese pepper-planters. The projected connection to the [[Deli Spoorweg Maatschappij|Deli Spoorweg Maatschappij]] network at [[Besitang|Besitang]] would, by the mid-1910s, finally link the Atjeh system to the wider Sumatran rail network and so to the port of [[Belawan|Belawan]].

## Decline and Legacy

The Atjehspoorweg, like the rest of the colonial transport infrastructure, entered a long decline after the depression of the [[1930s]] and was largely destroyed during the Japanese occupation of [[1942]]–[[1945]] and the subsequent revolution. Its alignment, however, remained the principal axis of communication in northern Sumatra well into the late twentieth century, and the road that succeeded it still follows, in many places, the line of the original railway. The Atjehspoorweg is significant in the encyclopedia of [[Atjeh|Achehnese]] history as the colonial state's principal investment in the integration of the residency into the wider Netherlands-Indies economy — and as the material foundation on which the plantation sector of the east coast was built.
