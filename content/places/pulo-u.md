**Pulo U** — vulgo **Simalur** (the modern **Simeulue**) — is an island lying off the west coast of [[Acheh|Achehnese] territory, whose headman figures in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as a survival of the older port-king's supremacy, reduced in modern times to a merely local dignity.

## The Panguléë Side of Meulaboh

Snouck Hurgronje records that the **Panguléë Side** of [[Meulaboh]], who was undoubtedly at one time an official under the chief of that place, charged with the task of making judicial enquiries, "is now in fact no more than headman of Pulo U (vulg. Simalur)." The port-king of [[Meulaboh]] — like the other port-kings of the [[West Coast (of Acheh)|West Coast]] — had once enjoyed a supremacy extending over the neighbouring islands and over the adjacent stretch of the mainland; and the office of the Panguléë Side, originally a delegated judicial office under the port-king, had in the course of time become an hereditary headmanship of Pulo U.

## The Survival of the Port-King's Supremacy

The author further explains that "so little control had the Sultans of Acheh over the course of events, that whatever may have been their purpose in establishing these various offices, the latter became in a short time part and parcel of the indigenous institutions of the country, or else disappeared altogether." The headmanship of Pulo U is thus a typical instance of the way in which an office, originally delegated by the port-king or by the Sultan of Acheh, was transformed in the course of generations into an indigenous chieftainship, lost its connection with the central power, and survived as a merely local dignity.

## The Island and its Headman

The island of Pulo U — the Simalur of ordinary speech, the Simeulue of modern maps — lies to the south-west of [[Acheh]], at a considerable distance from the capital, and its people have long enjoyed a measure of independence under their own headmen. The reduction of the Panguléë Side to the headmanship of Pulo U marks, in Snouck Hurgronje's account, the limit of the older Achehnese supremacy in that direction, and the way in which the offices of the port-kings — once the channels of Achehnese suzerainty — became, in the late nineteenth century, the mere titles of local chiefs.
