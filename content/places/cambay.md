**Cambay** — modern Khambhat, on the coast of [[Goedjarat]] (Gujarat) in western India — is named in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) as the city from which the first Muslim tombstones were imported to the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] region of northern Sumatra, and as the chief *Moslimsch emporium* (Muslim trading emporium) of the eastern Indian Ocean in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. In the chapter *De oudste cultuurstreamen*, Dr. [[F. H. van Naerssen|Van Naerssen]] records that the first Muslim ruler of [[Samudra]] on the [[Pasei|Pase-rivier]] — whose grave dates from [[1297]] — "lies buried there in 1297. His gravestone is preserved and proves to have been imported ready-made from Cambay, the chief city of Goedjarat, which itself had only been Islamised since around 1250 and was developing more and more into a Muslim emporium."

## Role as a tombstone-exporting centre

The ready-made import of Muslim tombstones from Cambay to northern Sumatra is, in Van Naerssen's account, the principal material evidence for the early Islamisation of the Sumatran coast. The same Cambay-type tombstone is found in the oldest preserved Muslim grave on Java — that of [[Malik Ibrahim]] (d. 1419) at Gresik, near Surabaya — for whom "many legends have been woven." The two tombstones — at Samudra in 1297 and at Gresik in 1419 — together bracket the early Islamisation of the archipelago, and both point back to Cambay as the source of the first Islamic funerary monument-type to reach the Indies.

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, Cambay's significance is foundational. The Atjeh sultanate that succeeded [[Samudra]] as the dominant Muslim power on the north Sumatran coast inherited its earliest Islamic material culture — specifically its tombstone type — from Cambay. The Gujerati mercantile network of which Cambay was the chief city was the proximate vehicle through which Islam reached the [[Pasei|Pase]] region in the late thirteenth century, and through which the [[Conversion of Minangkabau via Atjeh (c. 1550)|conversion of the Minangkabau]] via Atjeh was effected in the mid-sixteenth century.
