**Kingdom of Loewoe** was a small polities situated in the northern part of [[Aceh]] that came into direct conflict with the [[Royal Netherlands Navy]] during the early twentieth‑century Dutch colonial campaigns. The Dutch records note an “actie tegen het rijk van Loewoe” (action against the kingdom of Loewoe) carried out in the early **September** of an unspecified year, when naval landing forces from the steamships [[Hertog Hendrik|Hertog Hendrik]] and [[De Ruyter|De Ruyter]] engaged the kingdom’s defenses. The operation forms part of the broader Dutch effort to suppress resistant principalities in the Aceh region after the formal end of the **Aceh War** (1873–1904).

## Context

The Kingdom of Loewoe was one of several autonomous Acehnese chiefdoms that retained a degree of self‑rule after the main phase of the Aceh War. Dutch colonial authorities regarded such polities as obstacles to the consolidation of the **Dutch East Indies** and therefore launched punitive expeditions to enforce direct control. Contemporary Dutch reports describe Loewoe as a “rijk” (realm) with its own fortified settlements, which the Dutch navy targeted to demonstrate the reach of the colonial fleet.

## Dutch Expedition against Loewoe

According to the naval log dated **23 August** (see page 152), the landing camp at **Pampanoea** was abandoned, and Lieutenant **R. G. M. A. Heg** was detached to oversee the loading of provisions and the evacuation of the sick. In early **September**, the Dutch forces launched an assault on Loewoe. The landing divisions from the steamships [[Hertog Hendrik]] and [[De Ruyter]] were placed under the command of Lieutenant‑Commander **F. Bauduin** (class F). The operation initially proceeded without gunfire; however, once the troops began disembarking, they encountered armed resistance from Loewoe’s defenders, who opened “lilla‑ en geweervuur” (rifle and musket fire). The Dutch sloops returned fire, but the fortified positions at **Pandjalaë** (likely a local stronghold) could not be taken.

During the landing, a group of locals was observed preparing two rifles; Lieutenant **F. A. Kimball** intervened, extinguishing a burning fuse and removing gunpowder to prevent an accidental explosion. A brief skirmish ensued, resulting in four Loewoe combatants killed and Dutch casualties that included Lieutenant‑Commander **J. A. van Zadelhoff** (who later died of tetanus in a Makassar hospital), a boatswain, and a drummer.

By **11 a.m.**, the Dutch forces had secured the beachhead, and by **2 p.m.** they halted approximately 600 m from the residence of **Opoe Patoenraë** (a local leader). Two companies advanced on the residence, attacking the southern façade of the **kraton** (palace). The division from [[Hertog Hendrik]] stormed the north‑western side, while the division from [[De Ruyter]] remained in reserve. After receiving fire from the village perimeter, a Dutch sailor was seriously wounded. By **2 p.m.**, the kraton was in Dutch possession, and the landing camp was subsequently occupied.

## Outcome

The capture of the Loewoe kraton marked a decisive Dutch victory in the region, effectively ending organized resistance from this particular Acehnese chiefdom. The operation demonstrated the Dutch navy’s capability to project power onto inland Acehnese territories using coordinated amphibious assaults. The success also paved the way for subsequent Dutch actions against other resistant polities in Aceh, such as the later campaigns in **Bali** and **Lombok**, although those were geographically distinct.

## Significance

The Loewoe episode illustrates the transition from large‑scale conventional warfare (the Aceh War) to targeted punitive expeditions aimed at subjugating residual autonomous entities. It highlights the role of naval landing forces in extending colonial authority beyond coastal enclaves and underscores the importance of small‑scale engagements in the final consolidation of Dutch rule over the entire Aceh peninsula.
