**Gajodorp** — a Gajo village (Dutch: *Gajo-dorp*) of the central highlands of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] — is the settlement type exemplified in the source *DLP-84-Atjeh* by the tightly packed houses built on stilts, each with a gallery (Dutch: *galerie*) and a thatched roof, that characterised the traditional architecture and social organisation of the [[Gajo|Gajo]] people in the early twentieth century. The settlement exemplifies the integration of Gajo cultural elements within the broader Acehnese landscape, especially after the opening of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] cut through the primeval forest (Dutch: *oerbos*) and connected the highland country to the colonial capital at [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta-Radja]] and the plantation economy of the east-coast lowlands.

## Architecture

The houses of a Gajodorp were constructed on piles, featured a verandah (Dutch: *galerie*), and were roofed with straw or alang-alang grass (Dutch: *alang-alang*). The pile construction kept the dwelling above the damp ground of the highland valley, while the gallery served as the principal social space of the household — the place where visitors were received, where the family gathered, and where the daily work of weaving and food preparation was carried on. The thatched roof, of cogon grass (Dutch: *alang-alang*) or of attap, was the standard roofing of the highland house and survived, in many villages, well into the late colonial period. The layout of the village — with its houses clustered around a central open space — embodied the social organisation of a people whose political structure was less hierarchical than that of the lowland Acehnese. The compactness of the settlement, with houses set wall-to-wall along narrow lanes, reflected both the defensive needs of a highland community in a country of raiding and head-hunting, and the communal patterns of a society that placed a high value on proximity and mutual support.

## Cultural Context

The Gajodorp, as a settlement type, illustrates the integration of Gajo cultural elements within the broader Acehnese landscape. The traditional dark, self-woven garments of the Gajo, their silver ornaments, and their distinctive stilt-house architecture set them apart from the lowland Acehnese, whose houses were more often constructed at ground level and whose dress was more subject to the influence of European textiles. After the opening of the [[Gajoweg (Gayo Road)|Gajoweg]] in the years around [[1904]]–[[1910]], the Gajodorp was increasingly drawn into the colonial economy: the Gajo men worked as contract labourers on the rubber and oil-palm estates of the highland fringes, and the Gajo women wove textiles for the colonial market. The village, however, retained its traditional form well into the late colonial period, and the architecture of the Gajodorp remained a visible marker of the highland culture that the colonial state had only recently subdued.
