Referenced in the sadati dialogue: 'My desire, brothers, is to go to Pulo Pinang (Penang).' The Hok Canton was a ship 'belonging to Chinese traders in Penang, trading to [[Acheh]] under Dutch colours.' After the ransom affair following the [[Hok Canton Incident]], Achehnese who had been involved found their way to [[Singapore]] and Penang. Penang thus served as both a trading partner and a place of refuge for Achehnese connected to the events of the holy war against the Dutch.

**Penang** (also *Pinang*, formerly *Prince of Wales Island*) was a British Straits Settlement on the northwest coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]], where — as [[R. J. Wilkinson|R.J. Wilkinson]] noted in his translator's note to *The Achehnese* — the English Government had adopted the word *[[Mukim|mukim]]* to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes. In Penang and Province Wellesley, the word had the same modified meaning as in [[Great Acheh|Acheh]], and in its stricter sense corresponded almost exactly to the English word "parish."

This linguistic adoption is significant as evidence of the close administrative and cultural ties between the British Straits Settlements and the Acehnese-Malay world.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Penang

**Penang** (also **Pulau Pinang**) is the British Straits Settlement — an island and port off the west coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]] — that appears in *The Achehnese* Vol. I as the chief foreign commercial destination of the Achehnese, and as a recurring point of comparison in matters of Malay vocabulary and popular superstition. Captain Francis Light had pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal, as early as [[1786]], that a settlement at Pulau Pinang possessed greater advantages than the port of Acheh: "I prefer Penang to the Port of Acheen," he wrote, "as being more healthy and entirely free from that oppression, lucre and confusion which have ever distressed and at length driven out every [European] attempt to settle there."

In commercial life, Penang is the place to which Achehnese dollars (piastres) are taken by traders to buy goods, yielding them in that port a profitable premium varying between 1 and 5 per cent — 4 per cent being reckoned as the average premium or *basi*, as the Achehnese call it. The moneylender who advances capital to the trader for a Penang voyage bases his contract on the rate of *basi* prevailing when the loan is made; the rate can always be easily ascertained from the traders or seafaring people who have last come over from Penang. The trader runs very little risk, as the rate seldom falls much in the time required for the voyage, and he at once proceeds to change his money on arrival. This method of raising money is called *meudua basi* ("dividing the *basi*"); worse still, the name of lending in consideration of a part or the whole of the *basi* is sometimes used simply as a euphemism for ordinary usury, without any money-changing or journeys to Penang. The dollars thus exported find their way back to Acheh in exchange for pepper and betel-nuts.

In matters of language and administrative usage, Penang supplies several Malay terms that have crossed into Achehnese. The word *mukim*, in its stricter sense, corresponds almost exactly to the English word "parish"; in Penang and Province Wellesley the English Government adopted the word to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes. *Sawah*, the Malay word for irrigated rice-fields used in the southern parts of the Peninsula (Johor, [[Malacca]] etc.) and in [[Java]], gives way in Penang, Province Wellesley, [[Kedah]] etc. — as in Acheh — to *blang*. *Titi*, a dialectic variation of *titian* ("a bridge"), is the word for "bridge" in the Malay of the northern part of the Peninsula; in Malacca and [[Singapore]] it is *jambatan*. *Longtang*, meaning "interval" in the Javanese month-names, curiously enough means "a narrow drain or ditch" in the Malay of Singapore and (by extension) of Penang.

In religious superstition, Penang is associated with the cult of [[Tuan Meurasab]] (the saint of [[Nagore]]). Penang Mohammedans have a superstition that articles of value vowed to this saint, if thrown into the sea at Penang, will be washed up in a few months' time close to his shrine at Nagore on the [[Coromandel Coast|Coromandel coast]]. Hewn tombstones are now largely imported from Penang for use in Achehnese cemeteries, replacing the porous and easily worked stone from Pulo Batee (Stone Island) opposite [[Ulee Lheue]] that was formerly employed.
**Penang** (also called **Pulau Pinang**) was, in the period described by *The Achehnese*, a [[Straits Settlements|British Straits Settlement]] — an island and town off the west coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]] which served the Achehnese both as their chief emporium of foreign trade and as the principal British political alternative to a settlement in [[Acheh]] itself. It had been founded in [[1786]] by [[Captain Francis Light]], whose own preference for Penang over the port of Acheh set the tone of British policy for the next half-century. "I prefer Penang to the Port of Acheen," wrote [[James Price]] to the Chairman of the East India Company in London, "as being more healthy and entirely free from that oppression, war and confusion which have ever distressed and at length driven out every European nation who has attempted to settle there." Governor [[Robert Fullerton|R. Fullerton]] added, in [[1825]], that European influence over Acheh had been "found utterly impracticable without employing a large military force to overawe the inhabitants."

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pulau Pinang

**Pulau Pinang** (Penang, also styled Prince of Wales Island in the early British records) is the island off the west coast of the Malay Peninsula which, in [[1786]], was selected by [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]] as the site of a British settlement in preference to a station on the Achehnese coast. *The Achehnese* introduces Pulau Pinang at the very outset of its account of European relations with Acheh, since the choice of Penang over Acheh fixed the political geography of the northern Straits for the next century and indirectly shaped the conditions under which the Dutch war against Acheh was eventually fought.

In that same year [[1786]], [[Warren Hastings|Warren Hastings]] received from the then [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan]] "a discourteous letter in reply to an expression of goodwill", and Captain Francis Light pointed out to the Governor-General of Bengal that "a settlement at Pulau Pinang possessed greater advantages than one on the Achehnese coast." Light's memorandum, quoted by Snouck Hurgronje, runs: "Acheen is a good road but no place of security against an enemy there. The country is fertile beyond description and very populous. The inhabitants rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous. To form a settlement there of safety and advantage, a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary." In the same spirit wrote [[James Price]] to the chairman of the Company in London: "I prefer Penang to the Port of Acheen as being more healthy and entirely free from that oppression ...".

The Achehnese themselves, in the same chapter, are said to have placed the eastern boundary of their kingdom in Sumatra at [[Tamiang]] on the East Coast, and the western at Baros, while Great Acheh proper was the kernel of the kingdom. Pulau Pinang is mentioned in Vol. I chiefly as the locus from which British observations of Achehnese affairs were made, and as a place of residence for the translator [[A.W.S. O'Sullivan|O'Sullivan]] of the English edition.

In a later footnote the translator remarks that "Penang Mohammedans have a superstition that articles of value vowed to [[Tuan Meurasab]] if thrown into the sea at Penang will be washed up in a few months' time close to the shrine at Nagore" on the Coromandel coast — a popular belief linking the island to the cult of that southern Indian saint. The word *mukim*, Snouck Hurgronje adds, has been adopted by "the English Government" in Penang and Province Wellesley "to designate the minor subdivisions of a district for administrative purposes," though in its stricter Achehnese sense it corresponds rather to a group of villages grouped around a central mosque. The English government of Pulau Pinang thus preserved, in colonial administrative vocabulary, a legacy of the Achehnese political geography it had replaced.

## Account from *Raffles' Atjeh-overeenkomst van 1819* — Penang

**Penang** (Dutch *Pinang*; Malay *Pulo Pinang*; officially, in the early nineteenth century, *Prince of Wales Island*) is an island off the west coast of the [[Malay Peninsula]] which in [[1786]] was ceded to the British East India Company by the [[Raja of Quedah]] and became, for nearly half a century, the principal British settlement east of [[Bengal]] and the political and commercial base from which the relations with [[Acheh]] were conducted. Van der Kemp's article is, in no small part, the history of how the government of Penang came to be drawn into the Achehnese succession dispute, and of how its differences with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] over the conduct of that dispute conditioned the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]].

## Foundation

In the same year [[1786]] in which the Achehnese sultan [[Sultan Ziemul Abudeen|Sultan Munson]] addressed his dismissive letter to [[Warren Hastings]], Captain [[Captain Francis Light|Francis Light]] "Deva Rajah" concluded with the Raja of [[Kedah|Quedah]] an agreement ceding Pulo Penang to the British for an annual rent of 10,000 dollars. Light, as Van der Kemp records, judged Penang "more healthy and entire free from that oppression, war and confusion which however distressed and at length driven out every European nation who has attempted to settle" at [[Acheh]]. The superintendence of political relations over Acheh was from the outset entrusted to the government of Penang, and the early years of the new settlement were "eene lijdensgeschiedenis" — a passion story — during which the idea of abandoning the island for some other station was repeatedly canvassed, though always rejected.

## The succession dispute and the Raffles–Coombs commission

By the time [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] had been driven to [[Pedir]] and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] to [[Telok Semawé]], Penang was deeply involved in the Achehnese succession. The Penang-based merchant [[Said Hussein]] had equipped the expedition that placed his son Syf on the throne in [[1815]], and the government of the island had to decide which of the rival claimants to recognise. [[Captain Coombs]]'s [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|first mission of 1817–1818]] concluded that Syf was the recognised sultan, a view that brought Coombs and Governor [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] into direct conflict with Raffles of [[Bencoolen]], who favoured Jauhar Alum. The [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|joint commission]] was organised from Penang in early [[1819]]; the proceedings opened there with disputes over troops, artillery and the order of ports to be visited, and culminated in Raffles's sudden departure on the [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|Karimon detour]] — the foundation of [[Singapore]] — without a word of farewell to either Bannerman or Coombs.

Penang's governor, [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]], died on [[8 August]] [[1819]] — the [[Death of Bannerman]] — and was succeeded by [[W. E. Phillips]]. Under Phillips the island's government continued to nurse the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]], sending circulars to the mukims and receiving the [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|defiant letter of 4 December 1822]]. It was also at Penang that the ex-sultan Syf lived out his days on his pension (the [[Syf's pension at Penang]]), and from Penang that [[John Anderson]] conducted the lengthy correspondence with the [[Sultan of Deli]] and [[Said Akil]] that marks the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)]].

## The Wilhelmina and Lucas affairs

The *Wilhelmina* affair and the [[Lucas mission to Telok Semawé (February 1821)|Lucas mission]] showed Penang in a less heroic light. The island's government, when appealed to by the Dutch governor of [[Malacca]] [[Timmerman Thyssen]], declined to permit Colonel [[Colonel E. Lucas|Lucas]] to proceed against [[Telok Semawé]], reasoning that Syf had exercised sovereign authority there and that the port would be "the last to acknowledge" [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]]. The island's secretary [[W. A. Clubley|Clubley]] formally refused a fresh examination; the Dutch *Irene* returned empty. Van der Kemp treats this as a typical episode of the "paper war" between the British and Dutch settlements in the Malay world.

## Strategic position after 1824

After the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London of 17 March 1824]], which exchanged Malacca for Benkoelen and ceded Singapore to Britain, Penang retained its supervisory role over the northern Sumatran coast. [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] on the Acheh situation — the [[Fullerton's minute on Acheh (21 March 1825)]] — was drawn up for the Bengal government at Penang. The island's trade benefited from the disintegration of central authority in [[Acheh]]: as Fullerton observed, the more ports were independent of the sultan, the more the trade of Penang flourished. The [[Pinang Nota (1825)]] and the [[Dutch Salt Price Reduction]] of the late 1820s show Penang's continuing preoccupation with the commerce of [[Sumatra's East Coast]] and with the competition of [[Singapore]] itself, which under [[John Prince]] and [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] rapidly eclipsed the older settlement.
