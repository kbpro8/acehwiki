**Kota Gédang** — also spelled *Kota Gēdang* in the Bezemer atlas — was, in the Dutch colonial administrative geography of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], a town in the *Gouvernement van Sumatra's Westkust* (Government of Sumatra's West Coast), and the place of origin of the pedal-driven weaving-loom illustrated as plate 67 of Prof. [[T. J. Bezemer|T. J. Bezemer]]'s [[Indonesische Kunstnijverheid (Bezemer 1933)|1933 plate-atlas *Indonesische Kunstnijverheid*]]. The town appears in the Bezemer atlas on the same page-spread as the [[Atjeh weefgetouw (Bezemer plate 62)|Atjeh loom of plate 62]] and the [[Atjeh imported silk warp ikat (Bezemer plate 65)|Atjeh silk ikats of plates 65 and 66]], and the juxtaposition is comparative: the Kota Gédang loom represents the more advanced, pedal-driven type of Sumatran weaving-loom, contrasted with the Atjeh loom of plate 62 as the more conservative, hand-operated type.

## The plate and its four-language caption

Plate 67 of the Bezemer atlas (p. 49) is captioned, in the four-language convention of the atlas, as follows:

- **Dutch**: *Weefgetouw met voetbeweging. Kota Gédang. Sumatra's Westkust.* — "Weaving-loom with foot-movement. Kota Gédang. Sumatra's West Coast."
- **English**: *Loom with Pedal Driving. Kota Gēdang. West Coast of Sumatra.*
- **French**: *Métier à tisser avec motion de pied. Kota Gédang, Côte ouest de Sumatra.* — "Loom with foot-movement. Kota Gédang, West Coast of Sumatra."
- **German**: *Webstuhl mit Fussbetrieb. Kota Gēdang, Sumatra's Westküste.* — "Loom with foot-drive. Kota Gēdang, Sumatra's West Coast."

The four-language caption identifies the object as a weaving-loom with foot-movement — that is, a pedal-driven loom of the type that Bezemer's *Inleiding* (§ B. *Weven*, pp. 5–7) identifies as the most advanced of the three principal types of Indonesian weaving-loom. The caption also identifies the place of origin as Kota Gédang on Sumatra's Westkust — placing the town in the colonial administrative region of the *Gouvernement van Sumatra's Westkust*.

## The pedal-driven loom

The pedal-driven loom of plate 67 is the type that Bezemer describes in § B of his *Inleiding* as the most *doelmatig* (purposeful, efficient) of the three principal Indonesian loom-types. The pedal-driven loom differs from the other two types in that the weaver, seated on a bench, operates the even-and-odd warp-threads by means of pedals (*pedalen*) rather than by hand; this frees her hands for the weft-passing, and so speeds up the weaving. The pedal-driven loom also does not require the weaver to use her back to keep the warp-threads taut — a feature of the eastern-Archipelago loom-type — and so allows her to weave longer pieces without fatigue. The pedal-driven loom is, in Bezemer's account, used in the late colonial period on Sumatra's Westkust (at Kota Gédang), on South-Celebes for narrow weavings, on Soerabaja, in the eastern corner of Java, and in the Vorstenlanden of Middle-Java. The West-Sumatran centre at Kota Gédang is therefore one of several regional centres of the pedal-driven loom-type in the late colonial archipelago.

## Comparative juxtaposition with the Atjeh loom

The juxtaposition of the Kota Gédang pedal-loom of plate 67 with the [[Atjeh weefgetouw (Bezemer plate 62)|Atjeh loom of plate 62]] on the same page-spread of the Bezemer atlas is, in this sense, a comparative juxtaposition of two Sumatran loom-types. The Atjeh loom of plate 62 represents the older, hand-operated type — one of the first two types described in Bezemer's *Inleiding* § B, in which the weaver operates the warp-threads by hand and uses her back to keep the warp taut. The Kota Gédang loom of plate 67 represents the more advanced, pedal-driven type — the third type described in the *Inleiding*, in which the weaver operates the warp-threads by pedals and sits on a bench. The juxtaposition places Atjeh, in [[1933]], on the conservative side of the Sumatran weaving-technology spectrum — a placement consistent with the broader late-colonial Dutch characterisation of Atjeh as a region where older craft techniques persisted.

The juxtaposition also places the Atjeh silk ikats of plates 65 and 66 — which were presumably woven on the Atjeh hand-loom of plate 62 — within the broader Sumatran weaving-technology context. The Atjeh weavers produced their silk ikats on the older, hand-operated loom; the West-Sumatran weavers, at Kota Gédang, used the more advanced pedal-loom. The two regions represent two stages of Sumatran weaving technology, with Atjeh on the conservative stage and the Westkust on the more advanced stage.

## Geography and people

Kota Gédang lies in the highlands of west-central [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], in the colonial *Gouvernement van Sumatra's Westkust* — the same colonial administrative region as the [[Padangsche Bovenlanden|Padangsche Bovenlanden]]. The town is in the heartland of the [[Menangkabau|Menangkabau]] people, and the pedal-loom of plate 67 is accordingly a Minangkabau craft object. The Minangkabau were, in the late colonial period, one of the most commercially and technologically dynamic of the Sumatran peoples, and the adoption of the pedal-driven loom at Kota Gédang is consistent with the broader Minangkabau pattern of technological modernisation in the late colonial period. The contrast with Atjeh — where the older hand-loom persisted — is, in this sense, a contrast between a more conservative and a more modernising Sumatran weaving tradition.

## Significance for Atjeh

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, Kota Gédang is significant principally as the Sumatran weaving-technology foil against which the Atjeh loom of plate 62 is defined in the Bezemer atlas. The Kota Gédang pedal-loom of plate 67 represents the more advanced Sumatran weaving technology of the late colonial period; the Atjeh hand-loom of plate 62 represents the more conservative technology. The juxtaposition of the two looms on the same page-spread of the atlas is a comparative juxtaposition of two Sumatran weaving-technology stages, within which the Atjeh loom is placed on the conservative side. The town is, in this sense, a Sumatran craft-geographical reference point for the Atjeh weaving tradition — the place against which Atjeh's technological conservatism is measured in the late colonial Dutch scholarship.
