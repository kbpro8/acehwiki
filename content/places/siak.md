**Siak** (Dutch and English *Siak*, also *Siac*, *Siack*; in full *Siak Sri Indrapura*) was the most considerable of the Malay sultanates on [[Sumatra's East Coast]], lying south of [[Deli]] and [[Batu Bara]] along the lower reaches of the river of the same name. Although the Sultan of Siak claimed a sort of pre-eminence among the petty states of the coast, his authority in the early nineteenth century was contested by younger branches of his own house and hemmed in by the Padri insurgents inland. The state is repeatedly mentioned in Van der Kemp's article, above all in connection with the correspondence of [[1828]] between Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of [[Penang]] and the Siak chiefs — correspondence which Van der Kemp prints in Appendix 11 as the principal illustration of the British policy of treating the East Coast as a commercial preserve against Dutch intrusion.

## In Fullerton's minute of 1825

The first substantial mention of Siak comes in Appendix 10, where Van der Kemp prints the passages of Fullerton's minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] on the [[Acheh treaty of 1819]]. Like [[Deli]] and [[Langkat]], Siak is enumerated among the "petty states" whose trade with the British settlements was threatened by the Dutch assertion of supremacy over [[Sumatra]]. Fullerton pointed out that under Article 3 of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of 1824]] the Dutch could make no treaty with Siak imposing differential duties against British trade; but that under Article 2 they might do so if Siak were deemed a dependency. The reservation of Achehnese independence therefore preserved Siak's independence as well — and with it, Penang's trade.

## The Bellona affair

In late [[1827]] the Commissioner-General [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Du Bus]] at [[Batavia]] resolved to send the frigate *Bellona* to the East Coast, partly to revive the trade of [[Riouw]] and partly to substitute Java salt, at thirty rupees the coyan, for the Siam salt then supplied through [[Singapore]]. [[John Prince|Prince]], late Resident of Singapore, reported the scheme from Batavia on [[29 November]] [[1827]] in a letter which Van der Kemp prints in Appendix 11. Prince observed that the appearance of such a ship at "the several ports of Siak Kampar, Jambi & c." might intimidate the natives into compliance with the Dutch views, contrary both to their interests and to the treaty stipulations.

## Fullerton's letter of 7 February 1828

Fullerton had already, on [[7 February]] [[1828]], written from [[Singapore]] to the [[Sultan of Siak]], assuring him that Malacca was now permanently British and expressing his satisfaction at the growing trade between Siak, [[Penang]], Malacca and Singapore. The Sultan's reply, dated [[4 June]] [[1828]], was cordial: he was "ten times as anxious as my friend can be" to preserve the friendly relations, and reported that he had lately succeeded his younger brother, "who has been afflicted with sickness and has lost his understanding". The translator [[John Anderson]] had, by order of the Penang government, addressed a similar letter to the *pangeran* (younger prince) of Siak. Van der Kemp prints both letters in full.

## The circular of 21 May 1828

Prompted by the Bellona affair, Fullerton laid a minute before his council on [[16 March]] [[1828]] announcing his intention to address a [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular to nine of the Siak chiefs]] inviting them to send their traders to Malacca free of duties, and assuring them of British protection against Dutch requisitions. The circular, dated [[21 May]] [[1828]], is printed by Van der Kemp immediately after the Siak correspondence. Together these documents form, in Van der Kemp's argument, the principal evidence of the British government's settled policy — from [[1825]] onward — of treating the East Coast as a commercial preserve, in direct resistance to the Dutch claim of "suprematie over Sumatra".

## A note on "Djokor en Siak"

In a footnote to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Said Akil affair]], Van der Kemp warns the reader not to confuse the unimportant [[Said Akil]] with "that other Akil, which in our history during the same years played an important role, the crown-pretender Radja Akil of the Siakseken", of whom [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Veth]] had given an attractive biography in *Borneo's Westerafdeling* II, pp. 150 ff., and who in [[1828]] ended as prince of [[Sukadana]] on [[Borneo]]. The reader is referred to [[L. W. G. N. Netscher|Netscher]]'s *Djokor en Siak*, p. 162. The "Siak" of this Akil's title refers to the Siak royal house, which had branches in several west Bornean states; the present article, by contrast, treats Siak proper on the Sumatran coast.
