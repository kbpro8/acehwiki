**Siak** (Dutch and English *Siak*, also *Siac*, *Siack*; in full *Siak Sri Indrapura*) was the most considerable of the Malay sultanates on [[Sumatra's East Coast]], lying south of [[Deli]] and [[Batu Bara]] along the lower reaches of the river of the same name. Its former ruler, [[Mohamed Ali (Malaysian prince)]], had governed the region before being installed by the Dutch as the client ruler of [[Salangore]] in [[1784]], reflecting the colonial strategy of employing established Malay elites. The Sultan of Siak claimed a sort of pre‑eminence among the petty states of the coast, though his authority in the early nineteenth century was contested by younger branches of his own house and hemmed in by the Padri insurgents inland. The state is repeatedly mentioned in Van der Kemp's article, above all in connection with the correspondence of [[1828]] between Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of [[Penang]] and the Siak chiefs — correspondence which Van der Kemp prints in Appendix 11 as the principal illustration of the British policy of treating the East Coast as a commercial preserve against Dutch intrusion. The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's *De Buitenbezittingen*, Deel II, Afl. 3 (1918) supplements this account with a long *Geschiedkundig Overzicht* of the Siak sultanate — from its origin in the destruction of the [[rijk Gasip|kingdom of Gasip]] by [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the early sixteenth century, through its seventeenth‑century subjection to [[Sultan Iskander Muda|Sultan Iskander Moeda of Atjeh]]'s [[sultan-iskander-moeda's-oostkust-veldtocht|Oostkust‑veldtocht]], its brief eighteenth‑century independence under [[Radja Ketjil|Radja Ketjil]] and the [[Sultan Ismail of Siak|Ismail]]–[[Sultan Said Ali|Said Ali]] expansion, and its nineteenth‑century decay under [[Tongkoe Ismail|Tongkoe Ismail]] culminating in the [[Siak tractate of 1 February 1858|1858 tractate]] and the [[Siak Contract of 23 June 1884 (cession of rights from Panei to Atjeh)|1884 contract]] by which the sultanate was absorbed into the Dutch colonial state.

## Dutch colonial strategy and the appointment of Mohamed Ali

The Dutch East India Company and later the Netherlands‑Indies Government frequently installed compliant Malay princes as client rulers to secure loyalty in newly‑conquered territories. In [[1784]] the Dutch appointed [[Mohamed Ali (Malaysian prince)]] as ruler of [[Salangore]], a move that exemplified this policy of leveraging established elite networks. This appointment pre‑dated the later British‑Dutch diplomatic contests over the East Coast and set a precedent for the later Dutch interventions in Siak during the mid‑nineteenth century.

## In Fullerton's minute of 1825

The first substantial mention of Siak comes in Appendix 10, where Van der Kemp prints the passages of Fullerton's minute of [[21 March]] [[1825]] on the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]]. Like [[Deli]] and [[Langkat]], Siak is enumerated among the "petty states" whose trade with the British settlements was threatened by the Dutch assertion of supremacy over [[Sumatra]]. Fullerton pointed out that under Article 3 of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 1824]] the Dutch could make no treaty with Siak imposing differential duties against British trade; but that under Article 2 they might do so if Siak were deemed a dependency. The reservation of Achehnese independence therefore preserved Siak's independence as well — and with it, Penang's trade.

## The Bellona affair

In late [[1827]] the Commissioner‑General [[Commissioner-General Du Bus|Du Bus]] at [[Batavia]] resolved to send the frigate *Bellona* to the East Coast, partly to revive the trade of [[Riouw]] and partly to substitute Java salt, at thirty rupees the coyan, for the Siam salt then supplied through [[Singapore]]. [[John Prince|Prince]], late Resident of Singapore, reported the scheme from Batavia on [[29 November]] [[1827]] in a letter which Van der Kemp prints in Appendix 11. Prince observed that the appearance of such a ship at "the several ports of Siak Kampar, Jambi & c." might intimidate the natives into compliance with the Dutch views, contrary both to their interests and to the treaty stipulations.

## Fullerton's letter of 7 February 1828

Fullerton had already, on [[7 February]] [[1828]], written from [[Singapore]] to the [[Sultan of Siak]], assuring him that Malacca was now permanently British and expressing his satisfaction at the growing trade between Siak, [[Penang]], Malacca and Singapore. The Sultan's reply, dated [[4 June]] [[1828]], was cordial: he was "ten times as anxious as my friend can be" to preserve the friendly relations, and reported that he had lately succeeded his younger brother, "who has been afflicted with sickness and has lost his understanding". The translator [[John Anderson]] had, by order of the Penang government, addressed a similar letter to the *pangeran* (younger prince) of Siak. Van der Kemp prints both letters in full.

## The circular of 21 May 1828

Prompted by the Bellona affair, Fullerton laid a minute before his council on [[16 March]] [[1828]] announcing his intention to address a [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular to nine of the Siak chiefs]] inviting them to send their traders to Malacca free of duties, and assuring them of British protection against Dutch requisitions. The circular, dated [[21 May]] [[1828]], is printed by Van der Kemp immediately after the Siak correspondence. Together these documents form, in Van der Kemp's argument, the principal evidence of the British government's settled policy — from [[1825]] onward — of treating the East Coast as a commercial preserve, in direct resistance to the Dutch claim of "suprematie over Sumatra".

## Early history (as presented in *De Buitenbezittingen*)

The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's survey of the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]] opens its *Geschiedkundig Overzicht* with the destruction of the [[rijk Gasip|kingdom of Gasip]] by [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the early sixteenth century, after which the [[Sultan of Djohore|Sultan of Djohore]] established himself at the [[Sabah Aoer|Sabah Aoer]] station at the mouth of the [[Siak|Siak River]]. "Het bestuur over deze Djohore'sche nederzetting (welke eene belangrijke immigratie uit dat rijk tengevolge had) werd in naam van den Sultan uitgeoefend door een sjahbandar, die gevestigd was te Sabah Aoer, en wiens invloed in het binnenland ongeveer tot de S. Mandau reikte" — the Djohorese administration was exercised, in the name of the Sultan, by a *sjahbandar* (harbour‑master) established at Sabah Aoer, whose influence inland reached approximately as far as the [[Mandau|Mandau River]].

In the early eighteenth century [[Radja Ketjil|Radja Ketjil]] — claiming to be an illegitimate son of a former [[Sultan of Djohore|Sultan of Djohore]] — drove [[Djohor|Djohore]] out of [[Siak|Siak]] with the help of the [[Pagarroejoeng|prince of Pagarroejoeng]] and, in [[1717]], made himself master of the throne of [[Djohor|Djohore]] itself. Although he soon lost the [[Djohor|Djohorese]] throne, he retained [[Siak|Siak]], which from that time was independent of [[Djohor|Djohore]]. After [[Radja Ketjil|Radja Ketjil]]'s secession the sultanate rapidly grew in power. The Dutch East India Company (the *Oost‑Indische Compagnie*) established a post at [[Poelau Gontong|Poelau Gontong]] on the [[Siak|Siak River]] in [[1755]], in return for supporting [[Raja Moehamad|Raja Moehamad]] against his brother [[Raja Alam|Raja Alam]] in their disputes over the Siak succession. The post was destroyed in [[1759]] by [[Raja Moehamad|Raja Moehamad]] himself — an act that the Bureau attributes to "onoordeelkundig optreden der Compagnie's gevolmachtigden" (unjudicious conduct of the Compagnie's envoys) — and the Compagnie thereupon reversed its allegiance to [[Raja Alam|Raja Alam]], who in [[1761]] overcame [[Sultan Ismail of Siak|Raja Ismail]] (the successor of [[Raja Moehamad|Raja Moehamad]]) and drove him out to [[Pelalawan|Pelalawan]].

[[Sultan Ismail of Siak|Raja Ismail]]'s [[1779]] restoration to the Siak throne inaugurated the sultanate's brief late‑eighteenth‑century zenith. "Vooral gedurende het bestuur van Sultan Said Ali, die in het einde der achttiende eeuw weder Ismail's opvolger had verdrongen, breidde Siak zijn heerschappij over zijne nabuurstaten uit, tot in de Noordelijke kustlandschappen toe" — especially during the administration of [[Sultan Said Ali|Sultan Said Ali]], who in the end of the eighteenth century had again driven out Ismail's successor, Siak extended its rule over its neighbouring states, even into the Northern coastal lands. The East Coast statelets — [[Langkat|Langkat]], [[Deli|Deli]] and [[Serdang|Serdang]] — having shaken off the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] overlordship in [[1769]], were forced by the armed force of [[Sultan Ismail of Siak|Ismail]] and [[Sultan Said Ali|Said Ali]] to acknowledge [[Siak|Siak]] as their overlord.

## Decay of Siak in the nineteenth century

After [[Sultan Said Ali|Said Ali]]'s death in [[1821]] the Siak sultanate was riven by "voortdurende binnenlandsche twisten" — continuous internal disputes — and the Siak overlordship of the East Coast statelets rapidly declined. "Vooral sedert het optreden van Sultan Tongkoe Ismail, in [[1827]], nam Siaks macht af en waren de Noordelijke Onderhoorigheden weldra inderdaad geheel onafhankelijk" — especially after the accession of [[Tongkoe Ismail|Sultan Tongkoe Ismail]] in [[1827]], Siak's power declined and the Northern Onderhoorigheden were soon in fact entirely independent. Even the old ceremonial exchange of *galar*s — the titles of recognition that the East Coast sultans had once received from the hands of the [[Sultan of Siak|Sultan of Siak]] — was neglected; in its place, the princes of the statelets who already held a Siak *galar* now issued such recognitions to newly acceding princes themselves.

[[Tongkoe Ismail|Tongkoe Ismail]]'s effort to restore Siak's authority led him to enlist the services of the British adventurer [[Wilson|Wilson]], who however soon quarrelled with the Sultan and was nearly successful in driving the entire royal house out of Siak. Having failed to obtain British assistance from the authorities at [[Singapore|Singapore]] — refused "met het oog op het bekende tractaat van 1824 met Nederland" (in view of the well‑known treaty of 1824 with the Netherlands) — [[Tongkoe Ismail|Tongkoe Ismail]] turned, "met meer succes" (with more success), to the Netherlands‑Indies Government. The Dutch intervention against [[Wilson|Wilson]] was the immediate prelude to the [[Siak tractate of 1 February 1858|tractate of 1 February 1858]], by which [[Tongkoe Ismail|Tongkoe Ismail]] ceded his realm to the [[Netherlands-Indies Government|Nederlandsch‑Indisch Gouvernement]] in return for Dutch protection of his rights over the Dependencies (as far as the [[Tamiang|Tamiangrivier]]).

The [[1858]] tractate's confirmation of Siak's overlordship over the East Coast Dependencies was, the Bureau records, "meer schijn dan wezen" — more appearance than reality — because the territorial princes continued to resist every actual interference in their affairs. The transfer of the Dutch administrative seat from [[Riouw en Onderhoorigheden|Riouw]] to [[Bengkalis|Bengkalis]] in [[1873]] (which Siak had ceded to the Government against an annual compensation of ƒ 8000) increased Dutch direct influence, until the formal transfer of the residual Siak claims became the natural next step. By the [[Siak Contract of 23 June 1884 (cession of rights from Panei to Atjeh)|contract of 23 June 1884]], the Government took over all Siak's residual claims over the Northern Onderhoorigheden from [[Panei]] northward as far as [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — "tot Atjeh toe" — against payment of an annual compensation of ƒ 40,000 and the remission of a debt of ƒ 50,000.

## A note on "Djokor en Siak"

In a footnote to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Said Akil affair]], Van der Kemp warns the reader not to confuse the unimportant [[Said Akil]] with "that other Akil, which in our history during the same years played an important role, the crown‑pretender Radja Akil of the Siakseken", of whom [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Veth]] had given an attractive biography in *Borneo's Westerafdeling* II, pp. 150 ff., and who in [[1828]] ended as prince of [[Sukadana]] on [[Borneo]]. The reader is referred to [[L. W. G. N. Netscher|Netscher]]'s *Djokor en Siak*, p. 162. The "Siak" of this Akil's title refers to the Siak royal house, which had branches in several west Bornean states; the present article, by contrast, treats Siak proper on the Sumatran coast.
