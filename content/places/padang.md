**Padang** is the chief Dutch colonial station on the west coast of [[Sumatra]], and the surrounding residency of the same name. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje invokes Padang chiefly for comparison: as a centre of Minangkabau culture, as a locus of the Hasan-Husain festival, as the seat of colonial lithographic publishing, and as the source of certain settlers in [[Acheh]].

## The Hasan-Husain Festival on the West Coast

In different parts of [[Netherlands India]] and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra (Padang, [[Bencoolen]] etc.), the [[Hasan-Husain festival]] was, Snouck Hurgronje writes, "celebrated on a smaller scale, but in much the same manner as we find it described in the Qanoon-e-islam." It had been thought that the festival was introduced along the sea-board by the sipāhīs (sepoys) who immigrated thither during the English domination. The author considers it probable, however, that even previously to this a Hasan-Husain feast enjoyed much popularity both in Sumatra and elsewhere, given that it is celebrated to this day in [[Trumon]] in the manner customary in the [[Deccan]]. The noisy celebration of these festivals, which may now be witnessed year by year at [[Kuta Raja]] in Acheh, "are for the most part got up by the Padang people who have settled there." Some Klings and Hindus take part in them, but the Achehnese "merely act as spectators."

## Minangkabau Eloquence and Padang Publications

The formal orations to which the adat of [[Menangkabau]] gives rise have their specimens, the author notes, in the work printed by R. Edw. van Muien in [[1890]] at Padang: *"Adat tiang bagai fafanai luarpakai dalam nan salajas Kampung."* This is far from being the sole point of resemblance between the eloquence of the Menangkabau people and that of the Achehnese. The continuous residence of Padang-printed adat manuals in the public sphere made Padang a clearing-house for the comparative study of Sumatran institutions.

## Sugar-Mills and Material Culture

The very primitive Achehnese sugar-mills, Snouck Hurgronje observes, are "similar to those found in [[Banten]], the highlands of Padang and other places." This remark places Achehnese material culture within a broader Sumatran-Javanese continuum of agricultural technique, in which the highlands of Padang represent a recognisable regional variant.

## Folk Poetry and Reference

In a fragment of Achehnese ritual dialogue, the formulaic verse "On the Padang grows keutumbèt (a plant used as a vegetable), sirahèt-fruits tied up in the corner of the garment" shows *padang* employed in its generic Achehnese sense of an open field or plain — but the comparison with the West-Coast Padang is inescapable in the same breath. A footnote refers the reader to the *"Geschicdenis van prinses Balkis"* by D. Gerth van Wijk (Padang, p. 70, No. 46) for further information on a related legend.

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Padang (highlands of)

The **highlands of Padang** are the upland country of west-central [[Sumatra]], forming the heartland of the [[Menangkabau|Minangkabau]] people and centred on the Dutch colonial station of Padang. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, Snouck Hurgronje invokes the highlands of Padang as the classical home of the matrilineal *adat* of the Minangkabau, and as the comparative foil against which the more limited matrilineal survivals of [[Acheh]] are to be measured.

### The Father's Role in the Upbringing of Children

The author draws a sharp distinction between the position of the father in Achehnese society and that enjoyed — or rather, denied — to him in the highlands of Padang. "In Acheh," he writes, "no one would dream of prohibiting the father from seriously concerning himself in the bringing up of his children, and reserving that right for the mother's brother, as is done in the highlands of Padang." The Minangkabau *adat*, in which the mother's brother (*mamak*) is the proper guardian of the children and the father a stranger in his wife's household, is thus the standard against which the weaker survivals of matriarchate in Acheh are to be judged.

### The Wife's Family and the Husband

The comparison runs through the whole of Snouck Hurgronje's account of Achehnese domestic life. In Acheh, "the wife's family are only too anxious to leave the ground free to the husband, and it is not till after years of wedded bliss that the ban which severs the latter from his wife's people is gradually removed to some extent. Thus the father has as great a share as he pleases to take in the bringing up of his children." The highlands of Padang thus supply the contrast: a system in which the husband is, from the first, a mere visitor in the house of his wife, and in which the children belong, by every tie of custom and law, to the mother's kin.

### Material Culture

The highlands of Padang recur in *The Achehnese* also as a point of comparison for material culture. The very primitive Achehnese sugar-mills, Snouck Hurgronje observes, are "similar to those found in [[Banten]], the highlands of Padang and other places." This remark places Achehnese material culture within a broader Sumatran-Javanese continuum of agricultural technique, in which the highlands of Padang represent a recognisable regional variant, characterised alike by the minangkabau *adat* and by the simple appliances of upland tillage.
