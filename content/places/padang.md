**Padang** was the chief Dutch colonial station and major port on the west coast of [[Sumatra]], serving as the administrative centre of the VOC’s Westkust government, a cultural hub of the [[Menangkabau|Minangkabau]] *adat*, and later, after the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo‑Dutch Treaty of 1824]] on [[2 January]] [[1665]], transferred to [[British Empire]] control under the 1824 treaty; the cession reshaped Dutch strategic focus toward the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] and intensified competition over the Strait of Malacca. In *The Achehnese* Vol. I, C. Snouck Hurgronje cites Padang for its role in the [[Hasan-Husain Festival|Hasan-Husain festival]], as a centre of Minangkabau cultural production, and as the source of settlers in [[Acheh]].

## Dutch VOC Presence and Institutional Role

Padang became the institutional capital of the VOC’s Westkust system after the [[Jacob Cau|Cau]] campaign of [[1664]]–[[1665]]. The [[Painansch Contract]] of [[6 July]] [[1663]] named Padang as one of the four contracting *landheeren*. Upon arrival in [[1664]], Cau forced the Atjeh forces from a coastal fortification and, on [[2 January]] [[1665]], established four definitive factories: Padang (the head office or *hoofdkantoor*), [[Poeloe Tjingkoek|Poeloe Tjingkoek]] (or [[Salido]]), [[Priaman]] and [[Tikoe]]. The designation of Padang as the seat of the *secunde* of the Westkust commandery in [[1692]] confirmed its centrality.

The native military governor, the [[panglima-radja|panglima‑radja]], acted as the senior indigenous counterpart to the VOC *commandeur*. The panglima‑radja was a signatory of the [[Padang Conference (29 August 1680)|Padang Conference of 29 August 1680]], presided over by Commissioner [[Laurens Pit de Jonge|Laurens Pit de Jonge]] and hosted by the commandeur [[Melchior Hurdt|Hurdt]].

## Colonial Administration and Native Authority

The [[Padang Conference (29 August 1680)|1680 conference]] produced a new general bond between the VOC and the Westkust regents. In August [[1671]] the panglima‑radja and the Padang regents declared “from their own motion” the transfer of tolls and import‑export rights previously owed to [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|the sultane of Atjeh]]. The 1659 [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (5 October 1659)|peace of 5 October 1659]] had already allowed the VOC to establish offices (*kantoren*) in Padang and [[Indrapoera]].

## Cultural and Economic Life

### Hasan‑Husain Festival

Snouck Hurgronje records that the [[Hasan-Husain Festival|Hasan-Husain festival]] was celebrated on the West Coast of Sumatra—including Padang and [[Bencoolen]]—in a manner similar to the description in the *Qanoon‑e‑islam*. He notes that the festival “were for the most part got up by the Padang people who have settled there” in [[Acheh]], with occasional participation by Klings and Hindus, while the Achehnese acted mainly as spectators.

### Minangkabau Eloquence and Padang Publications

The formal orations of Minangkabau *adat* were printed in Padang by R. Edw. van Muien in [[1890]]. The work contains specimens such as “*Adat tiang bagai fafanai luarpakai dalam nan salajas Kampung*,” illustrating the eloquence that Padang‑printed *adat* manuals supplied to the comparative study of Sumatran institutions.

### Material Culture and Sugar‑Mills

Snouck Hurgronje observes that the primitive Achehnese sugar‑mills are “similar to those found in [[Banten]], the highlands of Padang and other places,” placing Achehnese material culture within a broader Sumatran‑Javanese continuum in which the Padang highlands represent a distinct regional variant.

## Domestic Customs Comparison with Acheh

The highlands of Padang provide the comparative foil for Achehnese family law. In Padang, the Minangkabau *adat* entrusts child‑rearing to the mother’s brother (*mamak*), relegating the father to a peripheral role; in [[Acheh]] the father may take an active part. Snouck Hurgronje contrasts the two systems, noting that in Acheh “the wife’s family are only too anxious to leave the ground free to the husband,” whereas in Padang “the husband is, from the first, a mere visitor in the house of his wife.”

## British Transfer and 19th‑Century Issues

Under article 10 of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]], Padang was transferred from Dutch to [[British Empire]] control, joining [[Benkoelen|Benkoelen]] as a British possession. The cession altered Dutch strategic priorities, prompting a greater focus on the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] and on suppressing piracy and smuggling that threatened Dutch trade.

### Coombs’s Report (1817–1818)

When [[Captain Coombs|Captain Coombs]] of the Bengal establishment conducted his first Acheh mission in [[1817]]–[[1818]], he reported that Padang and [[Benkoelen|Benkoelen]] were the two European stations on the west coast, Padang being Dutch. His observations fed into the [[Raffles‑Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Acheh commission]] of [[1819]].

### Java‑Salt Complaint (1827)

In a report dated [[29 November]] [[1827]], the former Singapore resident [[John Prince]] noted that the Dutch government at Padang complained that salt imported from Singapore into the east coast of Sumatra was undermining the sale of Java‑salt imported into Padang. Van der Kemp’s footnote explains that the reduction in Java‑salt prices was advised by [[Van de Graaff|Van de Graaff]] to keep Dutch salt competitive with Siamese, Makassarese and Bimanese salt, and that Padang’s complaint was a routine fiscal grievance rather than an anti‑Singapore plot.

## Significance

Padang’s long‑term role as the VOC’s Westkust headquarters, the seat of the panglima‑radja, and the venue of the 1680 Padang Conference made it the administrative and cultural heart of Dutch western Sumatra. Its transfer to British control in 1824 marked a pivotal shift in colonial geopolitics, influencing Dutch focus on Aceh and the broader competition over the Strait of Malacca. The town’s cultural legacy—visible in the Hasan‑Husain festival, Minangkabau *adat* publications, and comparative material‑culture studies—continues to inform the historiography of both Acehnese and Minangkabau societies.

## Account from *DLP-84-Atjeh* — the post-Atjeh posting of Pastoor Verbraak

The *DLP-84-Atjeh* pamphlet records that [[Padang]] was a later posting for [[Pastoor Verbraak|Pastoor Verbraak]] after his service in [[Atjeh|Atjeh]]. Following his tenure in the forts of Atjeh, Verbraak continued his apostolate (*apostolaat* (apostolate)) in the Dutch colonial barracks of Padang before moving on to [[Malang|Malang]]. Padang thus served as a west-coast station in the broader chaplaincy network of the *soldier-pastor*, between the Atjeh forts of his principal service and his subsequent posting in east Java.

## Nineteenth-Century Historiography & Campaign Analysis

## Staging and Reserve Base for the Aceh War

During the planning of the [[First Aceh Expedition]] in [[1873]] and the [[Second Aceh Expedition]] under General [[J. van Swieten]], Padang served as a major regional supply depot and reserve troop staging ground. 

In *Atjeh gedurende de tweede expeditie en later* ([[1876]]), the author noted that critics of General [[J. van Swieten]] argued that the 1st Brigade stationed in reserve at Padang should have been deployed immediately to attack Aceh from the west coast, rather than being held back to prevent unnecessary climate attrition among European infantry.
