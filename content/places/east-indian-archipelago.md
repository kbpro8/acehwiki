The **East Indian Archipelago** — also called the **Indian Archipelago**, the **Eastern Archipelago**, or the **Malayan Archipelago** — is the great island world of South-East Asia, of which [[Acheh|Acheh]] forms the westernmost part. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] uses the term throughout *The Achehnese* as the geographical frame within which the institutions of Acheh are to be understood: Acheh is "a part of the East Indian Archipelago," and its customs are continually set beside those of the other Mohammedan peoples of the Archipelago — those of [[Java|Java]], [[Madura|Madura]], the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]], the [[Straits Settlements|Straits Settlements]], [[Celebes|Celebes]], and so on.

## Marriage and the ta'liq

Snouck's treatment of the marriage contract opens with the observation that "as Acheh forms a part of the East Indian Archipelago, our remarks on the marriage contract would be incomplete if we failed to notice one negative peculiarity, namely the complete absence in Acheh of the *ta'liq*." The *ta'liq* — a method to which the Mohammedan law itself gives the impulse — was resorted to throughout the East Indian Archipelago in order to mitigate the miseries of the wife whose husband had abandoned her without severing the lawful bond of marriage. The wife's position, in such a case, was in Mohammedan society a miserable one. The same custom, Snouck notes, exists in the Straits Settlements, and would in all probability be met with in those parts of [[Hindustan|Hindustan]] whence Islam was introduced into the East Indian Archipelago.

The universally applied *ta'liq* and the system of official marriage-makers are the two great characteristics which the marriage law of the East Indian Archipelago shares in common. The method by which a marriage is concluded — the official deputy, having been empowered by the *wali* as his *wakil*, enters into the marriage contract on the wali's behalf — is, Snouck observes, the one "most generally resorted to in other Mohammedan countries, as for example in [[Arabia|Arabia]]." The persons entrusted with the task of concluding marriage contracts in their division or district have, in the East Indian Archipelago, a natural dislike of competition, and are human enough to feel a strong inclination to monopolize the profits arising from their office.

## The introduction of Islam from Hindustan

The East Indian Archipelago is repeatedly named as the country into which Islam was first introduced from [[Hindustan|Hindustan]]. The creed of Islam, Snouck argues, "would appear to have made its first advances towards the Eastern Archipelago" from the coastlands of the southern part of British India. The Mohammedans of the [[Deccan|Deccan]], whose manners are portrayed in the *Qanoon-e-islam* of [[Jaffur Shurreef|Jaffur Shurreef]] (translated by [[G. A. Herklots|G. A. Herklots]]), are Shafi'ites, just like those of the Malayan Archipelago. It is clear, Snouck continues, "beyond all doubt that the Deccan form of Mohammedanism exercised an influence on that of the Indonesians superior in force to that of any other agency."

This Indian origin is reflected in the popular religious literature of the Archipelago. The stories which are such favourites in the Eastern Archipelago — of the Titanic wars and numerous love adventures of [[Amir Hamzah|Amir Hamzah]] (the uncle of Mohammad), of the romantic adventures of Mohammad ibnu'l-Hanafiyyah (the son of [[Ali|Ali]]), of the hero Sam'un, Raja Badar and many more — are all in conflict both with the history and the legendary tradition of Islam as fixed in Arabia, and bear the marks of their Indian and Persian provenance.

## The month of all souls

The choice of the eighth month of the year for the commemoration of the dead — the *kanduri bu* (Sha'bān) of Acheh, the *ruwah* of Java — is "a further corroboration of the introduction of Islam into Acheh from the Deccan," since in [[Arabia|Arabia]] the commemoration generally takes place in the seventh month. Throughout the whole of the Indian Archipelago, the month of Sha'bān is dedicated to the commemoration of the dead: "this does not imply grief for their loss, but rather care for their souls' repose, which is not inconsistent with merrymaking." The name of the month in many native languages is borrowed from this pious custom: in Javanese it is called *Ruwah* (month of all spirits), and in Achehnese *Bulèë kanduri*, since on some one day of this month every household holds, in honour of the departed, a religious feast in which rice forms the principal dish. The funeral observances after death among the Moslim populations of the Indian Archipelago are noted by Snouck as "practically identical with those of Acheh."
