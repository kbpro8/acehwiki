**Blang Kédjérèn** is the subdistrict headquarters-town and former subdistrict in the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] highlands of Central Atjèh, the last redoubt of the Dutch defense of Sumatra in March [[1942]] and the place to which General [[Major-General Overakker|Overakker]] moved his headquarters after the fall of [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]], where, on [[28 March]] [[1942]], the capitulation of the combined Dutch forces in the highlands of Atjèh took place. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats Blang Kédjérèn as the keystone of the entire Dutch defensive plan for northern Sumatra — the only road junction linking the [[Alasvalley|Alasvalley]] with the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] and the [[Takéngón|Takéngón]] highlands.

## The road connection of 1939

The road connection from [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]] in the Alas valley to Blang Kédjérèn in the Gajó Loeós — "located over 100 km. further north" — "had only been completed at the end of [[1939]] on the initiative of the then Assistant Resident of the East Coast of Atjèh, Mr. Th. O. B. Gunther. In harmonious cooperation of the population and military personnel, with financial support from the Government's resin and turpentine company, the old horse track for auto transport — albeit, that for the time being only one-way traffic was possible — was made suitable." Blang Kédjérèn was thus brought within a one-day motor drive of Koeta Tjané, replacing the three-day foot journey "still so picturesquely described in Zentgraaff's 'Atjeh'." On the eve of the [[1942|1942]] invasion the subdistricts of [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]] (with headquarters Blang Kédjérèn) and [[Alaslanden|Alaslanden]] (with headquarters [[Koeta Tjané|Koeta Tjané]]) were merged into the new subdistrict Gajó Loeós and Alaslanden, and Mr. Gunther was appointed district chief.

## The military role

"In contrast to the low-lying Alas valley, the Gajó Loeós valley lies at 800 to 1000 m. altitude with a surrounding mountain range of 2000 to 3000 m." The Gajó Loeós was, after [[Takéngón|Takéngón]] and the [[Alasvalley|Alasvalley]], the third great defence centre of the Atjèh highlands. "With a view to a prolonged defense considerable supplies of military equipment, foodstuffs, medicines, etc. had been gathered here." As late as [[1939]] the area could only be reached on foot; from [[1939]] by motor road from Koeta Tjané. "Bivouacs for 2,000 men had, thanks to the cooperation of Teukoe Radja Hoesin, been built by conscripts through paid labor and were ready for use on [[26 March]] [[1942]]. Food supplies, sufficient for several months, were available." Plans also existed, "with a view to a further expansion of the Government's resin and turpentine company," for "a road connection between [[Takéngón|Takéngón]] and Blang Kédjèrèn"; the road network of the resin company had "advanced to Manggis in the Isa' valley, 70 km. in the direction of the Blang," but "some hefty day marches through heavy mountain terrain, leading over the Goenóng Pró'" still separated the two road networks.

## The capitulation of 28 March 1942

When the Japanese broke through from the valley of the Lawé Rénoen on [[25 March]] [[1942]], "a further retreat to the last stronghold, the [[Gajó Loeós|Gajó Loeós]]," became necessary, and "the headquarters was moved to Blang Kédjèrèn." Colonel [[Gosenson|Gosenson]], after the fall of [[Takéngón|Takéngón]], "fell back on Blang Kédjèrèn with two very weary brigades, closely followed by the Japanese vanguard." The Japanese, "initially under the impression that the surrender of Takéngón meant the surrender of the entire force, set off in pursuit of Colonel Gosenson and his brigades as soon as they realized their mistake. As early as [[21 March]] a reconnaissance detachment departed for the Isa' valley, followed a few days later by a larger force, which at Manggis … turned onto the footpath to Blang Kédjèrèn." The Japanese vanguard passed "the Goenóng Pró', the highest point in the [[Takéngón|Takéngón]]-Blang Kédjèrèn connection, before this could be occupied by General Overakker." When General Overakker "decided on [[27 March]] to surrender, there was no longer any enemy to be acknowledged in the Blang, so that the surrender could only be offered on [[28 March]]. On that same day the Japanese troops held their entry into Blang Kédjérèn." The few European women and children present in Blang Kédjérèn were transferred to [[Kaban Djahé|Kaban Djahé]] at the end of March. With the Japanese troops "came also some 50 F-men," who were sent back at the request of Teukoe Radja Hoesin a few days later.
