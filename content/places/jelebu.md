**Jelebu** is a small state in the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]], now comprised within the federation of Negri Sembilan, mentioned in *The Achehnese* Vol. I in a translator's footnote on the Malay title *penghulu*. The reference is included as a parallel to the political condition of [[Acheh|Acheh]] and especially to the office of the Achehnese *uleebalang*.

The footnote is appended to Snouck Hurgronje's discussion of the Achehnese title *pangulee* — a variation, borrowed from the Pidie dialect, of the more familiar *penghulu*. "Penghulu," remarks the translator, "has a wide range in Malay, just as *imeum* in Achehnese. The *penghulu masjid* is quite a humble individual, a sort of sacristan of the mosque. The headmen of large villages are also called *penghulus*; but in some states the same title is given to an official whose position somewhat resembles that of the uleebalang in Acheh. Such for example was [[Sayyid Ali|Sayyid Ali]] the Penghulu of Jelebu in the [[Malay Peninsula]], who revolted against the hereditary [[Yam Tuan|Yam Tuan]] and became the ruler of that small independent state before it came under British protection."

The comparison is significant for several reasons. First, it supplies a peninsular Malay parallel to the Achehnese phenomenon of the self-made territorial chief — the man who, by ability and prestige, raises himself from a subordinate office to that of an independent ruler. Snouck's whole discussion of the Achehnese political order turns on the distinction between the hereditary uleebalang and the upstart panglima or title-bearer without territory; the rise of Sayyid Ali in Jelebu is a peninsular instance of the same process, in which a religiously sanctioned office (that of *penghulu*) was made the foundation of an independent territorial principality. The parallel suggests that the political habits of Acheh were not peculiar to it but were shared, in greater or less degree, by the Malay world of which it formed a part.

Second, the example is instructive as to the role of the sayyid in the politics of the archipelago. Sayyid Ali's success in Jelebu depended, like that of [[Sayyid Abdurrahman Zahir|the Habib]] in Acheh, on the impregnable prestige that sacred descent conferred on a man of ability; and his revolution, like the Habib's centralisation, was achieved not by the overthrow of the existing order but by the conversion of a religious or quasi-religious office into the seat of a new temporal power. Snouck's recurrent insistence on the political importance of the sayyids in Acheh finds in the peninsular parallel a wider archipelago confirmation.

Jelebu itself was, before the British protectorate, a small and loosely organised state of the Negri Sembilan confederacy, ruled by a *Undang* (or *Penghulu*) of its own who enjoyed an hereditary but elective authority under the suzerainty of the [[Yam Tuan|Yam Tuan]] of Sri Menanti. The revolution ascribed by the translator to Sayyid Ali — in which a sayyid penghulu set himself up as ruler in place of the hereditary Yam Tuan — was one of those dynastic episodes by which the small states of the peninsula were periodically disturbed before the establishment of the British residential system. The mention of Jelebu in *The Achehnese* is the only occurrence of the state in Vol. I; it serves as a single comparative illustration, drawn from the translator's peninsular experience, of an institution common to the Malay and Achehnese worlds.

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Jelebu

**Jelebu** (Achehnese *Jélébu*, modern spelling *Jelebu*) is a small state of the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] mentioned by Snouck Hurgronje, in a translator's footnote, in connection with the political ascent of the sayyid **Sayyid Ali the Penghulu of Jelebu**, who revolted against the hereditary Yam Tuan and became the ruler of the state before it came under British protection.

### Sayyid Ali the Penghulu of Jelebu

In the chapter on the sayyids of [[Acheh|Acheh]], Snouck observes that the title *penghulu* has a wide range in Malay, just as *imeum* has in Achehnese. The *Penghulu Masjid* is a humble individual, a sort of sacristan of the mosque; the headmen of large villages are also called *penghulus*; but in some states of the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] the same title is given to an official whose position somewhat resembles that of the uleebalang in [[Acheh|Acheh]]. "Such for example was **Sayyid Ali the Penghulu of Jelebu** in the Malay Peninsula, who revolted against the hereditary Yam Tuan and became the ruler of that small independent state before it came under British protection."

The mention is brief, but it is significant. It places Jelebu in the comparative horizon of the Achehnese sayyids, and it records a historical instance of the political ascent of a sayyid — a descendant of the Prophet — by revolt against a hereditary chief. The parallel with the Achehnese sayyids, who enjoyed "an impregnable basis of action" by virtue of their sacred descent, is implicit; the example of Sayyid Ali of Jelebu shows that the same basis could, in the right circumstances, be turned to political account in other parts of the Malay world.

### The Yam Tuan and the Political Order of the Peninsula

The **Yam Tuan** (more fully, the *Yang di-Pertuan Besar*) was the hereditary ruler of [[Sungei Ujong|Sungei Ujong]], one of the constituent states of the later [[Federated Malay States|Federated Malay States]]. The same title was borne by the rulers of the neighbouring state of **Jelebu**, where the Yam Tuan was, in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the senior of the local chiefs. The revolt of Sayyid Ali, the Penghulu of Jelebu, against the Yam Tuan is one of the few recorded instances in the Peninsula in which a sayyid successfully displaced a hereditary ruler; it is the more interesting in that the displaced ruler was himself a Malay chief of standing, and not merely a local headman.

### The Place of Jelebu in the Comparative Frame

The mention of Jelebu in *The Achehnese* is otherwise incidental to the argument of the work. It belongs to that comparative apparatus — Malay, Indian, Javanese, Sumatran — by which Snouck and his translator together brought the institutions of [[Acheh|Acheh]] within the comprehension of the European reader. The comparison is drawn between the *penghulu* of the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] and the *imeum* of [[Acheh|Acheh]]; the political weight which a sayyid could acquire, by virtue of his sacred descent, is illustrated from the parallel case of Sayyid Ali of Jelebu. The function of the reference is to confirm, by a comparative example, the political potential inherent in the sayyid's position.

### Jelebu under British Protection

The final clause of the translator's note — "before it came under British protection" — fixes the chronological horizon of the comparison. Jelebu was, in the late nineteenth century, one of the small states of the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] which were brought within the British protectorate, and which, in 1895, became part of the [[Federated Malay States|Federated Malay States]]. The revolt of Sayyid Ali accordingly belongs to the period before the establishment of the British protectorate; it is a fact of the older political order of the Peninsula, in which hereditary rulers and enterprising sayyids contended for local supremacy without the intervention of a European overlord.
