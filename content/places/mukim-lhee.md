**Mukim Lhee** — literally the "Three Mukims" — was a *wakeuëh*-district of [[Great Acheh]] belonging to none of the [[Three Sagis (XXVI, XXV, XXII Mukims)|three sagis]], although claimed by both the [[XXII Mukims|XXII]] and the [[XXVI Mukims]]. In Snouck Hurgronje's account it is the paradigmatic example of the *wakeuëh*-districts: territories severed from the control of the great uleebalangs and placed directly under the sultan.

## A District Between the Sagis

Even now, Snouck Hurgronje writes, this district is usually described as **Mukim Lhèe** ("the Mukims Three"), whilst other uleebalangships of three mukims are called "the three mukims so and so" — e.g. [[Lhee Mukim Lam Rabo]], [[Lhee Mukim Kayee Adang]]. In like manner the IV Mukims of the [[XXV Mukims]] are always known as "the IV Mukims," and the VII Mukims of [[Pidie]] as "Mukim Tujoh." Both the sagi of the [[XXVI Mukims]] and that of the [[XXII Mukims]] claimed that Mukim Lhee belonged to them, but both allowed that it had for a long time past been withdrawn from all connection with their Panglimas. The people of the Mukims Three acknowledged that they were *reuëng Tunong* (highlanders), thus admitting the correctness of the assertion of the sagi of the [[XXII Mukims]].

## Neutrality in the Sagis' Wars

In the wars between the two above-named sagis, the Mukims III took part with neither side. The people of the latter thus removed the corpses of the slain for both parties, and permitted no fighting to be carried on within their own territory. This neutrality was the practical expression of the *wakeuëh* institution: severed from the great uleebalangs, the district stood "without and above parties," maintaining a "neutral field of observation and operation" for the sultan.

## The Brief Rule of Teungku Chot Putu

The only instance of the administration of these Mukims by a single ruler within living memory was that of a certain ulama named [[Teungku Chot Putu]], who succeeded in attaining to some degree of authority there in the middle of the nineteenth century. This expounder of the law derived from his piety, learning and severity such an overwhelming influence over the three imeums and their subjects that, although not invested with any political power, he acted in fact as ruler of the Mukim Lhee. Before his appearance and after his death these mukims were self-governing, yet held in due respect the tradition of their peculiar constitution. His son [[Cheh Chot Putu]], though essentially a worldly man and no scholar, inherited some of the respect paid to his father, but his efforts at playing the role of uleebalang of the III Mukims were not crowned with success.

## The Keureukons and the Modern Name

The name **III Mukims Keureukon** now usually given to that district, coupled with the fact that a family resided therein one male member of which enjoyed the hereditary title of [[Teuku Keureukon]], points to the efforts made by the holders of that dignity to gain the supremacy there. Among the principal court officials in the period of prosperity of the sultanate there had been a royal secretary with the title **Keureukon Katibulmuluk** (vulgarly "Katiboy mulut"). This title could more easily pass from father to son than the art of composing and writing Malay letters; and the importance of such correspondence dwindled with the decay of the court. The Keureukons thus provide another example of the degeneration of offices in Acheh, alongside that of the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]].
