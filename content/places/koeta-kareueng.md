**Koeta Kareuëng** is a locality in the interior of the [[Noordkust van Atjeh|North Coast of Atjeh]], reached from [[Geudong Concession|Geudong]] by more than an hour's walk, known for one of the *heilige graven* (holy graves) of the country: the grave of a queen of [[Pasé]], dated 1500 A.D. (*1500 n. C.*). In November [[1913]] the archaeologist [[De Vink]] guided the traveller [[W. Moens|Moens]] thither from [[Lho' Seumawe|Loh Semaweu]] — forty-five minutes by the [[Atjeh-tram|tram]] to Geudong and then the walk, over the ankles in the mud but along an old watercourse, to the plain of Koeta Kareuëng.

## The grave of the queen of Pasé

The grave bore head and foot stones of white marble with Arabic inscriptions; on the reverse of the stones Hindu inscriptions were found. Since no marble is found in Atjeh itself, the material showed that the stones had originally been brought from India — presumably as sculptured material of an older, Hindu monument, afterwards reused and inscribed for the grave of the Muslim queen. De Vink, who had served for years as assistant to kapitein Van Erp at the restoration of the Borobudur, made photographs of such antiquities and impressions of the inscriptions in starch-paper, which he sent to Leiden to be studied by Professor [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]].

## Significance

Koeta Kareuëng is significant in the written record as one of the first monuments of the old kingdom of [[Pasé]] brought to the notice of science after the *[[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacificatie]]*: a dated queen's grave of about 1500 A.D., in which the Indian marble, the older Hindu inscriptions, and the Arabic lettering together marked the transition of the North-Sumatran coastal kingdoms from the Hindu to the Islamic period. The excursion of De Vink and Moens illustrates the beginning of organised antiquarian research in Atjeh in the years of the definitive peace.
