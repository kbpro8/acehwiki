**Aroe** — also **Aru**, in Portuguese *Aru* or *Arua*, in the [[Sadjarah Malajoe]] the realm of the *Djao* (Portuguese *Jaoa*), and in the Encyclopaedisch Bureau's survey of the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]] the *Batakrijk Aroe* — was the kingdom on the east coast of [[Sumatra]] whose bay, [[Aroe-baai|Aroe-baai]], opens into the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] opposite the Nine Islands of [[Perak]], and whose fall to the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] [[Atjehsche vloot|fleet]] in [[1539]] — recorded by [[Mendez Pinto]] — marks the close of the independent history of the east-coast Hindu-Buddhist commonwealth and the beginning of the Atjehnese maritime age. A kingdom once ranked in the [[Sadjarah Malajoe]] on a level with [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] and [[Malacca]], and one that stretched from [[Tamijang|Tamijang]] to [[Siak]] — and, in the Encyclopaedisch Bureau's account, north-westward along the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malakka]] into south-eastern [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — Aroe was reduced by the Atjehnese conquest to "poverty and piracy", so much so that in the colonial period only a "*soekoe Aroe*" — an Aroe clan — was remembered. The Encyclopaedisch Bureau, drawing on Batak saga, places the destruction of the *Batakrijk Aroe* "probably in [[1589]]" — a dating tradition that complements (rather than supersedes) Pinto's [[1539]] account, and that anchors the Batak memory of a coast-dwelling people driven into the hills by the Atjehnese conquest.

## Geography and the bay

Aroe's strategic heart was [[Aroe-baai|the bay]] — known to [[Mendez Pinto|Pinto]] as the *port de Surotilau* on the *cite du royaume d'Auru* — into which the river of [[Tandjoengpoera]] empties. The bay is sheltered by a half-moon of islands: [[Poelo Kampi|Kampi]] (Pinto's *Paneticano*), [[Pulo Sembilan|Sembilan]], [[Pulo Breueh|Salahadji]] and others; the landward side is heavily forested, with apes, pythons, and crocodiles that "dragged men from the prauwen and devoured them". The approach by sea from Malacca runs by the Nine Islands off [[Perak]], across the Strait, and into the bay; the inland approach runs up the [[Besitang]] river, past the [[Pangkalan Soesoe|Pangkalan Soesoe]] oil-springs, and up the *soengej Si Kondoer* and *Sérang djaja* — old fortifications, the colonial official [[Van Rijn van Alkemade|Van Rijn]] reported in [[1888]], that were said to be the old *bentengs* of the earlier rajas of Aroe.

## Geographical extent: the two accounts

The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's survey of the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]] records the geographical extent of the *Batakrijk Aroe* in terms that complement Pinto's account. "In het N. de Bataks niet alleen het binnenland beheerschten, doch de grenzen van hun groote Batak-rijk Aroe uitbreidden langs de straat van Malaka tot in Z. O. Atjeh" — "in the north [the Bataks] not only controlled the interior, but the borders of their great Batak realm of Aroe extended along the Strait of Malakka into south-eastern Atjeh." Aroe thus extended, in the Bureau's account, from the present cultivation district of the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]] (the future [[Deli|Deli]], [[Serdang|Serdang]], [[Langkat|Langkat]] belt) north-westward along the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malakka]] into south-eastern [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. The Bureau's placement of Aroe "in het tegenwoordig Cultuurgebied" — "in the present Cultivation District" — locates the Batak realm in precisely the territory that would, three centuries after its destruction, become the economic heart of the colonial East Coast.

The two accounts are mutually consistent in their geographical framing: both place Aroe on the east coast of Sumatra, with a reach extending from the [[Tamijang|Tamijang]] area southward to [[Siak]] (Pinto) and from the cultivation district north-westward into south-eastern [[Acheh|Atjeh]] (Bureau). The difference is one of emphasis: Pinto's account foregrounds the bay and the maritime trade, while the Bureau's account foregrounds the Batak ethnic identity and the inland reach.

## Pinto and the Atjeh-Aru war (1539)

Aroe enters European record through [[Mendez Pinto|Pinto]]'s second Sumatran mission. In October [[1539]] he was dispatched by [[Pero de Faria]] to Aroe, whose king had asked for Portuguese help against the [[Atjehsche vloot|Atjehnese fleet]] then bearing down on him. Pinto's anchorage in the bay, his reception by the [[Radja of Aroe]], the arrival of the [[Alleredin Mahomet|Atjehnese fleet]] of 130 vessels, the [[Radja of Aroe|radja]]'s death in the breach, the [[Queen of Aroe|queen]]'s counter-stroke and flight, and the kingdom's absorption into the Atjehnese sphere are the substance of the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)]] — the foundational military event of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Atjehnese sultanate]]'s sixteenth-century expansion.

## The Batak saga and the 1589 dating tradition

The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's survey of the Buitenbezittingen records a second dating tradition for the destruction of the *Batakrijk Aroe*. "Reeds werd gesproken over het mythische Batakrijk 'Aroe' in het tegenwoordig Cultuurgebied, dat, volgens de sage, waarschijnlijk [[1589]] door de Atjehers vernietigd werd" — "Already was spoken of the mythical Batak realm of 'Aroe' in the present Cultivation District, which, according to saga, was probably destroyed by the Atjehers in 1589." The Bureau's attribution of the date to "saga" (*sage*) and its qualifier "probably" (*waarschijnlijk*) signal that the [[1589]] date rests on traditional Batak memory rather than on a dated contemporary chronicle.

The [[1539]] and [[1589]] dating traditions are best read as complementary rather than contradictory: the [[1539]] date records the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|Pinto-attested naval campaign]] by which the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] fleet shattered the kingdom's maritime power, while the [[1589]] date records the final destruction of the Batak realm's inland residues, as preserved in Batak oral tradition. The half-century between the two dates would, on this reading, mark the protracted Atjehnese consolidation of the east-coast interior that followed the [[1539]] naval victory.

## Decoding the *bedekte taal* of Aroe

Pinto's account of Aroe is dense with names that [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]] decodes as *bedekte taal* — veiled-language forms concealing the older Hindu-Buddhist geography of the bay. *Surotilau* is read as *Soera-sakti-lawoe-kir*, the roadstead of [[Malajoer]] in Aroe. *Ilikandure*, the "fish (*ikan*) on Hindu" (*hin-doe ka-ri*) riddle, marks where Hindu population still survived. *Minhatoley* is read as *mina-ng-k-a da-toe Olèh-leh*, the territory of the *dotoe* of [[Olèh-leh]], an Atjeh-chief marking the start of the Suratte-passage. *Batorrendan* is *batoe rendang*, a joke on the [[Batu|Batu]] islands. *Turban* is *Batoe-poer-wa-boewana*. *Paneticano* is [[Poelo Kampi|Kampi]]. *minha cumbaa* is *minjak sembawa*, the white earth-oil of [[Pangkalan Soesoe]].

The bay's islet, [[Poelo Kampi|Kampi]] — Pinto's *Paneticano*, also *Koempej* — is, in this reading, the older *Malajoer* of the [[Pararaton]], the *Noesa-n-ta-ra* "standing under the protection of the goddess Tara", where the *pabgjan* (the toll-house at which ships paid import duties) stood. The bay and the islet are, in turn, the *Taroema* of the Adiparwa's Churning of the Ocean of Milk — the *tasik soesoe* from which the gods drew the *amrta*.

## Political structure and trade before the fall

Before its fall, Aroe was administered in Malay style: a *Xabandar* (harbourmaster) commanded five *lanchares* and twelve *balo* (war-vessels), while above him stood the *banda(ha)ra* (bendahara), the "gouverneur du royaume", who directed the *hoeloebalang* (war-chiefs). The bendahara's personal guard were the *hamba radja* — "the most noble of his Court", though many of them, Pinto noted, were in fact poor. [[Ibn Battuta|Ibn Battuta]], visiting the region in [[1345]]–[[1346]], likewise recorded a Hindu kingdom (*Moel Djawa*) with elephants, stone walls, and cloves and nutmeg on its market — evidence of regular trade with the Moluccas conducted through Aroe's entrepôt.

## The Atjehnese and Djohorese invasions after the fall

The destruction of Aroe — whether placed in [[1539]] (Pinto) or [[1589]] (Batak saga) — was, the Encyclopaedisch Bureau records, immediately followed by an [[atjehsche-invasie-in-tamiang-en-noordelijk-langkat-(c-1589)|Atjehnese invasion of Tamiang and Northern Langkat]] and by a parallel movement of the [[Djohor|Maleiers van Djohore]] further south. "In [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] en Noordelijk [[Langkat|Langkat]] waren het de Atjehers, welke onder een vertegenwoordiger van hun Sultan de macht aan zich trokken; meer naar het Zuiden maakten de Maleiers van Djohore, evenals na de vernietiging van het rijk Gasip gebruik van den verwarden toestand om de kuststreken (riviermonden) te bezetten en daardoor een groot economische overwicht op het binnenland te verwerven" — "In Tamiang and Northern Langkat it was the Atjehers who, under a representative of their Sultan, drew the power to themselves; more to the south, the Malays of Djohore, just as after the destruction of the realm of Gasip, made use of the confused situation to occupy the coastal regions (river mouths) and thereby to acquire a great economic preponderance over the interior." The destruction was followed by a recognition of [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] influence — "voluntary or forced" — "tot in het gebied der Noordelijke hoogvlakte" — "as far as the territory of the Northern high plateau" (i.e. the [[Batak-Karo|Karo hoogvlakte]]).

## Decadence and the *soekoe Aroe*

After the [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|fall of Aroe]], the [[Queen of Aroe|queen]]'s flight, and the brief [[Djohor|Johorese]] reconquest, Aroe was finally lost for good, and "with the realm of the Djao — as the Portuguese wrote *Jaoa* — it was done forever". Once a *negeri* ranked in the [[Sadjarah Malajoe]] on a line with [[Pasej-Samodra|Pasej]] and [[Malacca]], a realm that stretched from [[Tamijang|Tamijang]] to [[Siak]], it sank into poverty and piracy, so much so that in the colonial period "one could only remember a *soekoe Aroe*" — an Aroe clan — in its place. Aroe had once been a *ravitailleeringsplaats* (revictualling station) for merchant shipping through the Strait, a place that traded on its own account and trans-shipped to other places, renowned as the source of the *minjak tanah* — the white medicinal petroleum that was exported as a balsam. In the seventeenth century that reputation had already much declined.

## The Batak dispossession and the Karo Doesoens

The institutional memory of Aroe survived in the [[Batak-Karo|Karo-Batak]] tradition of the [[radja berempat|radja berempat]] and in the broader cultural memory of the [[Batak]]s as a people driven from the coast into the hills. The Encyclopaedisch Bureau records that the Bataks were "steeds meer teruggedrongen naar het binnenland, vanwaar zij vroeger uitgezwermi waren naar de kust" — "driven ever further back into the interior, from which they had earlier swarmed out to the coast" — and that the [[Batak-Karo|Karo]] *doesoen* population of [[Langkat|Langkat]], [[Deli|Deli]] and [[Serdang|Serdang]] — the Batak communities of the hill country immediately behind the coastal plain — were the descendants of the Batak population that had once inhabited the Aroe kingdom's coastal core.

## Van Rijn's visit (1888)

In [[1888]] the Controleur S.B. [[Van Rijn van Alkemade|Van Rijn]] brought a visit to the Aroe-bay from [[Bengkalis]], in connection, De Roo de la Faille notes, with Tiele's essay on the history of those days. He found the bay heavily forested, hilly, a timber-concession of beautiful damar-trees; on the islet of [[Poelo Kampi|Kampi]] he saw the half-moon of hills whose whitish strand — "making the impression that one has a fortress with breastwork before one" — recalled the Tanjore inscription of [[1030]]'s "ancient Malayur situated on a high hill". On the mainland, at [[Pangkalan Soesoe]], he found ten dwellings of Malays who had fled from [[Besitang]]; inland, he found the two old *bentengs* of *soengej Si Kondoer* and *Sérang djaja*, said to have been the fortifications of the earlier rajas of Aroe.

## Significance

For AcehWiki, Aroe is the principal east-coast polity whose fall marks the formal opening of the [[Aceh Sultanate|Atjehnese sultanate]]'s maritime age. Its [[Atjeh-Aroe War (1539)|loss to Atjeh]] in [[1539]] is the single event by which the Atjehnese sultanate closed the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malacca]] to Portuguese shipping and began to draw the commerce of the Strait into its own sphere; its *bedekte taal* geography, as decoded by [[P. de Roo de la Faille|De Roo de la Faille]], is the principal instance of the survival of the older Hindu-Buddhist place-world beneath the chronicle record. The Encyclopaedisch Bureau's account, in turn, anchors Aroe in the Batak memory as the *Batakrijk* whose destruction in [[1589]] (per Batak saga) opened the east coast to the [[atjehsche-overheersing-der-noordelijke-kuststaatjes|Atjehnese overlordship of the northern coastal statelets]] and the [[atjehsche-invasie-in-tamiang-en-noordelijk-langkat-(c-1589)|Atjehnese invasion of Tamiang and Northern Langkat]].
