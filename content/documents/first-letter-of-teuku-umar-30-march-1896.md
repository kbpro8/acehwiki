**The First Letter of Teuku Umar to the Governor of Atjeh** — reproduced as Annex II of Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* and dated from [[Lampisang]] on [[30 March]] [[1896]] — was the earliest of three letters in which [[Teuku Umar]] (writing as *Toekoe Oemar*) set out to the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Governor of Atjeh]], [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. C. Deykerhoff]], the reasons for his [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection]] from the [[Dutch]] colonial government. The letter enumerated seven grievances, principally alleged insults by the [[Controleur]] te [[Oleh-leh]] and the head public prosecutor Mohamad Arief, and offered to be beaten without resistance if his feelings toward the *Kompeni* had not changed — while pointedly confirming that the guns supplied by the Government were still in his hands, used to guard the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]]. Kruisheer records that the reasons given in this letter "proved to be entirely fabricated" — proved to be entirely fabricated.

## Background

The letter was written the day after Umar's defection became known at [[Koeta Radja]] around 1 p.m. on [[29 March]] [[1896]]. For nearly two and a half years, since his appointment as [[Panglima Prang Besar]] of the Government under [[Gouvernementsbesluit|Government Resolution]] of [[5 September]] [[1893]], Umar had been armed and paid by the Dutch to lead a native auxiliary *legioen* against the recalcitrant [[Lamkrak Moekims|Lamkrak]] mukims. On [[26 March]] [[1896]] he had received a final issue of 380 + 500 guns, 25,000 cartridges, 500 kg of gunpowder, 120,000 percussion caps, 5,000 kg of lead, opium, and 18,000 dollars — and immediately turned these weapons against his patrons.

The position of [[Controleur]] te [[Oleh-leh]] and the conduct of the *hoofddjaksa* Mohamad Arief had been for some time a source of friction with Umar and with other Acehnese territorial chiefs. [[Controleur]] [[Gisolf]] had warned the general that Umar's attitude was more than suspicious; the warning was ignored. Umar was present at a conference in the Governor's office as late as [[28 March]] [[1896]], even as preparations for defection were already far advanced.

## Content

The letter, written from his house at [[Lampisang]] on [[30 March]] [[1896]], set out seven reasons for Umar's defection. The most prominent were alleged insults to himself and his family: he charged that the [[Controleur]] te [[Oleh-leh]] and the *hoofddjaksa* Mohamad Arief — whom he described as resembling a water buffalo — had insulted him, and that his older brother T. Nja Mohamad of the [[IX Moekims]] had been insulted at [[Pakan Badak]]. He further alleged that the imams of Goerah and Lamtengah (in the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]]) had been kicked by the [[Controleur]] te [[Oleh-leh]] in public.

The letter then offered a striking and theatrical gesture: Umar declared that, if his feelings toward the *Kompeni* had not changed, the Government might send someone to beat him and he would offer no resistance. As proof of good faith he requested the signature of the [[Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies|Governor-General]] at [[Batavia]] — that is, of [[Jhr. C. H. A. van der Wijck|Jhr. Van der Wijck]]. Crucially for the unfolding military crisis, the letter confirmed that the guns supplied by the Government were still in his hands, used to guard the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]] — an admission that the Dutch-supplied weapons were now turned against the *Kompeni* itself.

## Reception and Aftermath

Deykerhoff's government immediately recognised the letter's explanations as hollow. Kruisheer records that the reasons given "proved to be entirely fabricated" (proved to be entirely fabricated). The letter nonetheless served Umar's purpose: it bought time, established a negotiating posture, and provided a quasi-legal pretext that could be circulated among the *hoeloebalang* and clerics of Groot-Atjeh to justify his act. Umar followed it with two further letters dated [[12 April]] [[1896]] and [[23 April]] [[1896]] (the [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar (12 April 1896)|Second]] and [[Third Letter of Teuku Umar (23 April 1896)|Third Letters]]), in which the pretextual grievances of the first gave way to explicit demands for ƒ150,000 per month, a Royal decoration, and the hereditary succession of his son as [[Panglima Prang]].

The Dutch administration responded with the [[Government Telegram of 26 April 1896, No. 409]] and the [[Gouvernementsbesluit of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11 (Bijlage VI)|Government Resolution of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11]] dismissing Umar from his offices, and with [[Luitenant-Generaal Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]]'s [[Proclamation of Lt. Gen. Vetter to the heads and people of the 3 sagi of Groot-Atjeh (27 April 1896)|Proclamation of 27 April 1896]] announcing military operations against him.

## Significance

The First Letter marks the formal opening of the diplomatic phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] crisis of [[1896]]. As a historical document it preserves the only detailed self-defence that Umar offered for his defection; the rhetorical structure — grievances against named officials, theatrical offer of personal suffering, conditional loyalty, demand for higher signature — reveals both Umar's negotiating method and his reading of Dutch colonial bureaucratic culture. Its immediate practical effect was to confirm, in writing, that Umar retained the Dutch-supplied arms, a fact that transformed the strategic situation and forced the dispatch of [[Luitenant-Generaal Vetter|Vetter]] as [[Regeeringscommissaris]].
