**Beschouwingen over Atjeh en onzen oorlog met dat rijk** — *Reflections on Aceh and our war with that kingdom* — is a Dutch-language pamphlet on the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] written by an anonymous author under the pen name *Een Ooggetuige* ("An Eyewitness") and published by [[Van Meurs & Stufkens]] in [[Rotterdam]] in the [[1880s]]. The pamphlet belongs to the large body of contemporary Dutch pamphlet literature that debated the conduct, aims, and strategic value of the war against the [[Acheh|Acehnese]] sultanate, and it is notable for accompanying its text with a [[Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel|map of the theatre of war]] covering the waterways and the coastal lights held to be needed for world trade.

## Authorship and character

The pamphlet was issued under the pseudonym *Een Ooggetuige* — "An Eyewitness" — a pen name that signals the author's claim to first-hand knowledge of the Atjeh theatre, without revealing the writer's identity. The choice of a pseudonym was common in the Dutch pamphlet literature of the Atjeh War, in which serving and retired officers, colonial officials, merchants, and private commentators debated the war in print while preserving their anonymity. The author's stance, as far as the damaged text allows it to be recovered, is critical of the existing colonial administration and of the way the war was being conducted, while insisting that the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] should not abandon Atjeh but should instead settle the country on a firmer footing.

## Contents

The pamphlet opens with a description of the kingdom of Atjeh, its pepper plantations, and its geographical situation at the northern tip of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]]. The author stresses the richness of the Atjehnese pepper country and the importance of the pepper trade to the world market, and notes that the interior of Atjeh was at that time too little known to Europeans for its full value to be judged. The description of the pepper country is interwoven with remarks on the difficulty of moving through the interior, on the state of the roads, and on the need for better coastal lights (*kustlichten*) along the Atjehnese coast for the safety of world shipping.

### The argument on Atjeh's strategic position

A central argument of the pamphlet concerns the strategic position of Atjeh in relation to world trade. The author compares the position of Atjeh — commanding the northern entrance to the [[Strand Malaka (Strait of Malacca)|Strait of Malacca]] through the [[Strait of Halakka|Strait of Halakka]] — with that of [[Singapore|Singapore]], and argues that Atjeh lies on the great sea-route between the [[Suez Canal|Suez Canal]] and the Far East in a way that makes it, properly developed, a natural station for world shipping. The author holds that ships bound for [[Australia|Australia]] from Europe could pass more easily through the Strait of Halakka, north of [[Sabang|Pulau Weh]], than through the Strait of Malacca itself, and that a properly lighted and buoyed approach to the Atjehnese coast would shorten the passage for trade between Europe, [[India|India]], and the archipelago. The comparison with Singapore is made explicitly: the author argues that, for several branches of world trade, Atjeh is more favourably placed than Singapore, and that the neglect of the Atjehnese ports and waterways has handed an advantage to the British colony that the Dutch could yet recover.

### The pepper trade and the interior

The pamphlet devotes attention to the Atjehnese pepper trade, noting the extent of the pepper plantations (*pepperplant teelt*) along the Atjehnese coast and the dependence of the Atjehnese planters on foreign — chiefly [[Penang|Penang]] and [[Singapore|Singapore]] — buyers. The author remarks on the flow of pepper from the Atjehnese interior to the coastal ports, and on the role of the Atjehnese traders in carrying that pepper to market. The discussion of the pepper trade is tied to the argument on strategy: the author holds that a settled Atjeh, with its pepper trade properly organised, would be a source of revenue to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and a safeguard against the further intrusion of foreign traders into the Atjehnese market.

### The conduct of the war

The pamphlet discusses the conduct of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] and the controversy then current in the Dutch press over the course of the campaign. The author refers to commentary in the London *[[Pall Mall Gazette|Pall Mall Gazette]]* on the Atjeh question, and to the argument — then current in the Dutch and British press — over whether the Netherlands should continue the war or should withdraw from Atjeh. The author argues against withdrawal, holding that a withdrawal would be read by the Atjehnese as a defeat and would weaken the Dutch position throughout the archipelago, but is critical of the way the war had been conducted and of the absence of a clear plan for the settlement of the country once the war should be brought to an end.

### Places mentioned

The pamphlet names a number of places along the Atjehnese coast and in the surrounding region. The [[Strait of Halakka|Strait of Halakka]] between the Atjehnese mainland and [[Sabang|Pulau Weh]] is discussed in relation to the shipping route. [[Panton Laboe|Panton Laboe]] is named as a place on the coast. The interior places mentioned include [[Kampang|Kampang]], which the author discusses in connection with the pepper trade. The author likewise refers to the pepper country of the Lampong districts — including [[Kroeë|Kroeë]] and [[Oosthaven|Oosthaven]] — on the southern tip of Sumatra, and to the [[Sunda Strait|Sunda Strait]] as part of the wider discussion of the sea approaches to Atjeh. [[Padang|Padang]] on the west coast of Sumatra is named in connection with the comparison of Dutch colonial ports.

### The map

The pamphlet was issued with a folded map, the [[Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel|*Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel, de Vaarwaters en vereischte Kustlichten voor den Wereldhandel*]] — "Map of the Theatre of War, the Waterways and required Coastal Lights for World Trade" — which set out the Atjehnese coast, the waterways around it, and the positions at which coastal lights were held to be needed. The map is one of the elements that gives the pamphlet its character as a practical contribution to the strategic debate, since it allowed the reader to follow the author's argument on the shipping routes and the lighting of the coast in visual form.

## Significance

The *Beschouwingen over Atjeh en onzen oorlog met dat rijk* is significant within the AcehWiki corpus as an example of the contemporary Dutch pamphlet literature on the Atjeh War. It belongs to the body of writing — including the accounts of [[Terwogt's account of the Atjeh-oorlog (1891)|Terwogt]], [[Beuming's account of the origin of the Atjeh-oorlog|Beuming]], and others — that shaped the Dutch public debate on the war. Its anonymous authorship, its insistence on the strategic and commercial value of Atjeh, and its accompanying map of the theatre of war and the coastal lights together make it a document of the practical, strategic, and commercial thinking that underlay the Dutch continuation of the war. The pamphlet's comparison of Atjeh with Singapore, and its argument for the development of the Atjehnese coast as a station for world trade, place it within the wider nineteenth-century debate on the sea approaches to the archipelago, of which the [[Suez Canal|Suez Canal]] — opened in [[1869]] — was the decisive modern fact.
