***Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)*** ("West Monsoon (Coast of Atjeh)") is a Dutch-colonial landscape-poem by **F. Kunst**, published in the [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] collection ([[Bandoeng|Bandoeng]], [[1947]]), that depicts the coast of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] in the rains of the west monsoon — the rain on the coconut-plantations, the swollen rivers carrying uprooted trees to the sea, the smell of rotting leaves and overripe fruit, and the sudden late-sun breaking through the clouds. The poem is the principal Dutch-colonial literary document of the Atjehnese coast in the west-monsoon season, and a counterpart — within the same collection — to the political elegy "*[[Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh|Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh]]*."

## The poem

The poem, as published in [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] (p. 32), reads:

> *De regen ratelt op de klappertuinen,
> Die mijlenver de huivergrauwe zee bezoomen.
> Uit regenfloers en wolkenflarden doemen
> Van verre bergen somb're, ruigbegroeide kruinen,
> En gonzend stuwen zwaargezwollen stroomen
> Ter monding voort leemgeler wastren wilden vloed,
> Meevoerend willoos losgewoelde boomen,
> Wier wankel evenwicht de wieling kant'len doet.
>
> De geur van rottend loover en d'aromen
> Van overrijpe vruchten, die met doffen,
> Gedempten val op molmzachten bodem ploffen,
> Verwaait de regenwind in vochte vlagen,
> Die rits'lesd door het groen der blanke sawahs zwerven,
> Om aan den grauwen einder weg te sterven...
> Daar splijt de late zon de lage wolkenlagen:
> Goudlichte glorie overstraalt de landen
> En purper vlammen op de duist're tufsteenwanden.*

## Poetic structure

The poem is composed in the sonnet-like form typical of Kunst's *Melati en rotan*: a single fourteen-line stanza of regular Dutch-colonial verse, with the turn (*volta*) at line 13 — "*Daar splijt de late zon de lage wolkenlagen*" ("There the late sun splits the low cloud-layers") — that introduces the sudden illumination of the closing couplet. The imagery of the first twelve lines is uniformly dark: the "*huivergrauwe zee*" ("shivering-grey sea"), the "*somb're, ruigbegroeide kruinen*" ("sombre, roughly-grown peaks"), the "*leemgeler wastren wilden vloed*" ("clay-yellow wild flood"), the smell of "*rottend loover*" ("rotting foliage") and the overripe fruit falling with a "*gedempten val*" ("muffled fall") on the "*molmzachten bodem*" ("mould-soft ground"). The closing couplet reverses the darkness: the late sun splits the clouds, the gold-light glory overstrains the lands, and the purple flames on the dark tufa-stone walls — the sudden illumination that is the structural turn of the poem.

## The Atjehnese landscape

The poem's Atjehnese landscape is composed of four elements: the coconut-plantations (*klappertuinen*) that fringe the grey sea for miles; the distant mountains with their sombre, roughly-grown peaks; the swollen rivers that carry uprooted trees to the sea in a clay-yellow flood; and the *sawahs* (rice-fields) — described as "*blanke sawahs*" ("white sawahs") — through which the rain-wind rustles before dying at the grey horizon. The imagery is the documentary image of the Atjehnese coast in the west-monsoon season: a landscape of rain, swollen rivers, and tropical decay, lit at the last moment by the late sun breaking through the clouds.

## Significance

The *Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)* is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] history as the principal Dutch-colonial literary document of the Atjehnese coast in the west-monsoon season. The poem's imagery — the rain, the coconut-plantations, the swollen rivers, the rotting leaves, the late sun — is the poetic counterpart of the geographical image of Atjeh fixed in the [[Atlas van Nederland en de Indiën (1930)|*Atlas van Nederland en de Indiën*]] of G. Prop: the territory recently pacified, productive of pepper, with its coast exposed to the west monsoon. The poem's placement in the [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] collection — alongside the political elegy for [[H. N. A. Swart|General Swart]] — fixes the two poles of the Dutch-colonial poetic imagination of Atjeh: the scenic landscape of the coast and the political memory of the pacification. The closing couplet's sudden illumination — "*Goudlichte glorie overstraalt de landen*" ("Gold-light glory overstrains the lands") — is the poem's structural counterpart to the political lament of the Swart elegy: where the Swart poem laments the destruction of the colonial achievement, the *Westmoesson* poem finds, in the late sun breaking through the rain, a momentary image of glory that the political poem denies.
