***Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh*** ("On the Death of General Swart, the Pacifier of Atjeh") is a Dutch-colonial elegy by **F. Kunst**, published in the [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] collection ([[Bandoeng|Bandoeng]], [[1947]]), that laments not the death of General [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]] — the *Pacificator van Atjeh* — but the post-war destruction of his life's work: the reduction of the country's well-being to chaos, the raging of fanatic hatred where Swart had brought peace, and "*Holland in blindheids nacht*" ("Holland in night of blindness"). The poem is the principal Dutch-colonial literary document of the political memory of the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification of Atjeh]], and a counterpart — within the same collection — to the landscape-poem "*[[Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)|Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)]]*."

## The poem

The poem, as published in [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] (p. 41), reads:

> *Wanneer wij treuren aan het graf van Swart,
> Die nu geland is aan Doods veil'ge kust
> En van een daadzwaar, roemrijk leven rust,
> Is 't niet om hem, die dwars door vreugde en smart
> Atjēh gedragen heeft in zijn groot hart
> En van volbrachten plicht zich was bewust,
> Toen 's Levens laatst bivakvuur werd gebluscht.
> Ons voegt geen rouw om hem, maar het is hard,
> Zijn levenswerk roek'loos te zien vernield:
> Het heil van 't land, waar hij zoovéél van hield,
> Chaos, waar hij eens orde en welvaart bracht,
> Het razen van den fanatieken haat,
> Waar vredestichten was zijn grootsche daad,
> En ergst van al Holland in blindheids nacht.*

## Poetic structure

The poem is composed in a single sonnet-form stanza of regular Dutch-colonial verse, with the turn (*volta*) at line 9 — "*Zijn levenswerk roek'loos te zien vernield*" ("to see his life's work recklessly destroyed") — that introduces the three complaints of the closing sestet. The octave (lines 1–8) is a declaration of the proper object of mourning: not Swart himself — who has reached "*Doods veil'ge kust*" ("Death's safe coast") after a "*daadzwaar, roemrijk leven*" ("deed-heavy, glory-rich life") — but the destruction of his work. The sestet (lines 9–14) enumerates the three complaints: the reduction of the country's well-being to chaos, the raging of fanatic hatred where Swart had brought peace, and "*Holland in blindheids nacht*" ("Holland in night of blindness") — the motherland's failure to defend the colonial achievement.

## The occasion

The poem's occasion — the death of General [[H. N. A. Swart|H. N. A. Swart]] — is the rhetorical point of departure for a lament on the destruction of the colonial order established by Swart in Atjeh. The collection's publication in [[1947]] — in the immediate aftermath of the Japanese occupation and the early phase of the Indonesian National Revolution — fixes the historical horizon of the lament: the "*razen van den fanatieken haat*" ("raging of fanatic hatred") is the Indonesian revolution, and "*Holland in blindheids nacht*" is the Dutch political failure to defend the colonial achievement. The general's death, in this frame, is the occasion for a lament on the destruction of the colonial order itself.

## The title *Pacificator van Atjeh*

The poem's title — "*Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh*" — fixes Swart, in the colonial memory of the mid-twentieth-century Dutch community of the Indies, as the *Pacificator van Atjeh* — the officer credited with bringing the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] to its definitive close. The title is itself a document of the colonial memory: the war that had opened with the death of [[J. H. R. Köhler|Köhler]] in [[1873]] and had been brought to its military conclusion by [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in [[1903]] is, in the colonial memory of [[1947]], identified with the *Pacificator van Atjeh* — the officer under whose command the pacification was completed.

## Significance

The *Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh* is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] history on three counts. First, it is the principal Dutch-colonial literary document of the political memory of the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacification of Atjeh]] — the elegy that fixes General [[H. N. A. Swart|Swart]] in the colonial memory as the *Pacificator van Atjeh*. Second, it is a document of the colonial reaction to the destruction of the Atjehnese colonial order in the Indonesian National Revolution — the lament for the "*heil van 't land*" ("well-being of the country") reduced to chaos, the "*fanatieken haat*" ("fanatic hatred") raging where Swart had brought peace. Third, it is a document of the colonial judgement of the Dutch motherland — "*Holland in blindheids nacht*" — for the failure to defend the colonial achievement. The poem's placement in the [[Melati en rotan|*Melati en rotan*]] collection — alongside the landscape-poem "*[[Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)|Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)]]*" — fixes the two poles of the Dutch-colonial poetic imagination of Atjeh: the scenic landscape of the coast and the political memory of the pacification.
