**The Second Letter of Teuku Umar** — reproduced as Annex IV of Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* and dated from [[Lampisang]] on 29 *Sawal* 1313, equivalent to [[12 April]] [[1896]] — was the second of three letters from [[Teuku Umar]] (writing as *Toekoe Oemar*, *T. Djohan Pahalawan*) to the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Governor of Atjeh]]. The letter was addressed simultaneously to the Governor, the Chief of Staff, the [[Resident voor Atjehsche zaken en de scheepvaartregeling|Resident van Langen]], and the *assistent-resident* at [[Koeta Radja|Koeta-Radja]], and contained, according to Kruisheer, "not only his demands but also a complete *program of action*" he had drawn up for himself. The letter dropped much of the pretextual grievance-list of the [[First Letter of Teuku Umar to the Governor of Atjeh (Bijlage II)|First Letter]] of [[30 March]] [[1896]] and stated openly Umar's disappointment at being told he would never receive the expected knighthood, while demanding ƒ150,000 per month to pay his warriors and demanding the hereditary succession of his son as [[Panglima Prang]].

## Background

By [[12 April]] [[1896]] the strategic situation in [[Groot-Atjeh]] had deteriorated sharply since Umar's [[Defection of Teuku Umar (29 March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]]. The Dutch outer line (*[[buitenlinie]]*) was under pressure everywhere: the posts of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit|Lam Koenjit]] had been besieged since the defection, [[Senelop]] was being heavily bombarded from [[Lam Bada]] and [[Ana' Galong|Ana Galoeng]] was repeatedly attacked. [[Luitenant-Generaal Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]] had arrived as [[Regeeringscommissaris]] on [[7 April]] [[1896]], had [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. Deykerhoff]] honorably relieved of his functions, and had assumed both civil and military command. On [[8 April]] [[1896]] Vetter had launched [[J. W. Stemfoort|Kolonel Stemfoort]]'s column to relieve [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]], and on [[12 April]] [[1896]] — the very day of Umar's letter — Vetter ordered the evacuation and burning of both posts. The first [[Proclamation of the Regeeringscommissaris (12 April 1896)|Proclamation of the Regeeringscommissaris]] announcing the purpose of his arrival was spread on or about the same day.

Umar wrote from his house at [[Lampisang]], at the centre of his stelling in the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]], surrounded by his warriors and by the defected *hoeloebalang* of neighbouring mukims — among them [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb|T. Tjoet Toengkoeb of XXII Moekims]], [[T. Tjoet Machmoed|T. Tjoet Lamtengah]] of [[IV en VI Moekims|Moekim Lamtengah]], and [[T. Hoesin Lhongbatta|T. Hoesin Lehong Bata]] of [[Lamkrak Moekims|Lam-Krak]]. He addressed his letter not only to the Governor but to the entire Dutch chain of command, signalling that he considered himself no longer a subordinate but a negotiator.

## Content

The letter is the most candid of the three. Umar begins by clarifying that he has *not* changed allegiance — a transparent fiction given the simultaneous evacuation of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]] — but he adprovided that openly his disappointment at being told he would never receive the Royal knighthood he had been led to expect. He then sets out his demands: ƒ150,000 per month to pay his warriors; a promise to fight the enemies of the *Kompeni* but to exclude territorial chiefs from military affairs; and a request that his son succeed him as [[Panglima Prang]] — a hereditary claim foreign to Acehnese custom.

Umar then names the *benting te Ana' Galong* — that is, the [[benteng]] (fortification) of [[Ana' Galong|Anak Galoeng]] — as the place where vivres (*vivres*) are to be delivered for his forces, an extraordinary demand that the Dutch should provision his army at a post he himself had recently conquered for them in [[1893]]. He declares he will not appear before the Governor until "het land van Selimoen" is acquired — a territorial demand whose meaning Kruisheer does not gloss. The letter mentions the the chief of [[Lhong]] as his *helper*, suggesting that the network of Acehnese chiefs aligned with him had extended toward the west coast. The letter is signed with the seal — the formal insignia of his office as [[Panglima Prang Besar]].

## Reception and Aftermath

The Dutch administration treated the Second Letter as a definitive statement of Umar's *program of action* — Kruisheer's phrase. From this point onward, Dutch policy moved decisively toward military confrontation. The [[Regeeringscommissaris]] replied to Umar in writing on [[25 April]] [[1896]] with three points: (1) Umar's profession of unchanged loyalty was false, else he would have returned the *geleende oorlogsmaterieel* from the Lamkrak campaign; (2) his profession of not wanting to fight the Government was equally false, else he would not have built versterkingen in the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]]; (3) to be received in grace, Umar must by [[26 April]] hand in all *geleende oorlogsmaterieel*, demolish the versterkingen, and submit anew. Umar's reply of [[26 April]] [[1896]] morning asked for a three-day extension without any definite promise — which the [[Regeeringscommissaris]] denied the same day. The[[Third Letter of Teuku Umar (23 April 1896)|Third Letter]] of [[23 April]] [[1896]] followed, and on [[26 April]] [[1896]] the [[Gouvernementsbesluit of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11 (Bijlage VI)|Government Resolution No. 159/11]] formally dismissed Umar from his offices.

## Significance

The Second Letter is the central document of the Umar crisis. Its open enumeration of grievances and demands destroyed any possibility of negotiation: the demand for ƒ150,000 monthly, the hereditary *Panglima Prang* claim, and the insistence on Dutch provisioning of his forces at [[Ana' Galong]] could not be accepted by any colonial administration. Equally significant is the letter's list of addressees, which shows Umar attempting to deal with the Dutch chain of command as a peer rather than a subordinate. The mention of the *Kedjoeroean van Lhong* as helper reveals the geographic scope of his alliance network, reaching across the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI]] and [[IV en VI Moekims|IV Moekims]] toward the west coast. As a historical document, the letter is the clearest single statement of Umar's strategic programme and the most candid expression of his disappointment over the denied knighthood — a personal grievance whose consequences reshaped the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]].
