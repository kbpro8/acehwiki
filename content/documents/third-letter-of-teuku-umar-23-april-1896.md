**The Third Letter of Teuku Umar** — reproduced as Annex V of Kruisheer's *ATJEH '96* and dated from [[Lampisang]] on 1 *Dsoelkaidah* 1313, equivalent to [[23 April]] [[1896]] — was the last of the three letters from [[Teuku Umar]] (writing as *Toekoe Oemar*, *T. Djohan*) to the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|waarnemend Governor at Koeta-Radja]] before his formal dismissal by the [[Gouvernementsbesluit of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11 (Bijlage VI)|Government Resolution of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11]]. The letter reiterates Umar's claim of unchanged allegiance while demanding the Royal decoration promised by [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Deijkerhoff]], demands a signed declaration by the Queen of the Netherlands, the [[Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies|Governor-General]] at [[Batavia]], the Governor of Atjeh, and the European princes via their consuls, and reiterates the demand for ƒ150,000 per month. The surviving copy of the letter is signed by *De Secretaris* [[Udo de Haes]] with the formula "*for a true copy*" (For a conformed copy), indicating that the document reached Kruisheer through the official Dutch archive rather than the original.

## Background

By [[23 April]] [[1896]] the diplomatic crisis around Umar had reached its decisive phase. The [[Luitenant-Generaal Vetter|Regeeringscommissaris]] had been in [[Koeta Radja]] for just over two weeks; the [[buitenlinie]] had been progressively abandoned, with [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]] evacuated on [[12 April]] [[1896]], [[Ana' Galong|Anak-Galoeng]], [[Lamsoet|Lam-Barih]], [[Lamsoet|Lam-Soet]] and [[Senelop]] lifted on [[17 April]] [[1896]], and the final outer posts of [[Kroeng Gloempang]], [[Tjot Rang]] and [[Toengkoeb]] lifted on [[20 April]] [[1896]]. On [[21 April]] [[1896]] the [[Regeeringscommissaris]] wrote to [[J. W. Stemfoort|Kolonel Stemfoort]] thanking the troops for their "*moed... volharding en trouwe plichtsbetrachting*" (courage, perseverance, and faithful performance of duty) over the previous fourteen days.

Umar, surrounded at [[Lampisang]] by his increasingly unified coalition of *hoeloebalang* and *teungkoe*, was simultaneously attempting to negotiate with the Dutch and to extend his alliance. He was in contact with [[Panglima Polim]] of the [[Sagi der XXII Moekims|XXII Moekims]], whose letter to [[Tengkoe Sjech Tjot Poctoe|Tjot Poetoe]] reported that Polim was on his way to [[Lampisang]]. He had also authorised [[Soetan Ali]] as his intermediary with the Dutch. The Third Letter was written in this context — as the [[Regeeringscommissaris]]'s reply to the [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar (12 April 1896)|Second Letter]] was already in preparation.

## Content

The Third Letter is the most demanding and most quixotic of the three. Umar opens by reiterating that his allegiance is unchanged — a formula the Dutch had already dismissed in their reply of [[25 April]] [[1896]] as false. He then makes the central demand: the decoration, that is, the Royal knighthood promised by [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Deijkerhoff]]. To this he adds a new and extraordinary requirement — a signed declaration by the Queen of the Netherlands, the [[Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies|Governor-General]] at [[Batavia]] (i.e. [[Jhr. C. H. A. van der Wijck|Jhr. Van der Wijck]]), the [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur van Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Governor of Atjeh]], and the European princes via their consuls. This demand — for an international guarantee signed by European sovereigns — was self-evidently impossible for the Dutch to meet and appears designed to be refused.

Umar reiterates the demand for ƒ150,000 per month from the [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar (12 April 1896)|Second Letter]]. He promises, in return, to handle war affairs himself and to hand the country over to the *Kompeni* when the war ends — a formulation that casts him as a quasi-sovereign ally rather than a Dutch-appointed officer. He refers to [[Soetan Ali]] as his authorised intermediary for any further negotiations. The letter was copied into the official Dutch archive by [[Udo de Haes|De Secretaris Udo de Haes]] under the formula "*for a true copy*" (For a conformed copy), confirming that the original was in Umar's hand and reached the Dutch through the intermediary channel.

## Reception and Aftermath

The Third Letter was the immediate trigger for the final Dutch decision. On [[24 April]] [[1896]] a further letter of identical content from Umar to the *bestuur* was received. On [[25 April]] [[1896]] the [[Regeeringscommissaris]] replied in writing with three articles: (1) Umar's professions of unchanged loyalty were false, else he would have returned the *geleende oorlogsmaterieel*; (2) his professions of not wanting to fight the Government were equally false, else he would not have built versterkingen in the [[IV en VI Moekims|VI Moekims]]; (3) to be received in grace, Umar must by [[26 April]] hand in all *geleende oorlogsmaterieel*, demolish the versterkingen, and submit anew. Umar's reply on the morning of [[26 April]] [[1896]] asked for a three-day extension without any definite promise; the [[Regeeringscommissaris]] denied the request the same day. On [[26 April]] [[1896]] the [[Gouvernementsbesluit of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11 (Bijlage VI)|Government Resolution No. 159/11]] formally dismissed Umar from both his offices, and the [[Proclamation of Lt. Gen. Vetter to the heads and people of the 3 sagi of Groot-Atjeh (27 April 1896)|Proclamation of 27 April 1896]] announced the commencement of military operations against him.

## Significance

The Third Letter marks the end of the diplomatic phase of the Umar crisis. Its catalogue of impossible demands — the Queen's signature, the European princes' consuls, the hereditary *Panglima Prang* for his son, the ƒ150,000 monthly subsidy, the Dutch provisioning of his forces — was self-evidently designed to be rejected, and was so rejected within three days. As a historical document, the letter reveals the full scope of Umar's strategic ambition: not a return to Dutch favour on the old terms, but recognition as a quasi-sovereign ally whose authority over Groot-Atjeh would be internationally guaranteed. The survival of the letter in the official Dutch archive, copied under [[Udo de Haes|De Secretaris Udo de Haes]]'s "*for a true copy*" formula, gives it a particular evidentiary weight: it is the text the Dutch government itself used to justify the dismissal that followed.
