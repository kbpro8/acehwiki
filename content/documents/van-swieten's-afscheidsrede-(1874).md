**[[Van Swieten's afscheidsrede (1874)|Van Swieten's afscheidsrede]]** was the farewell address delivered by [[Jan van Swieten|generaal van Swieten]] in the [[Kraton of Atjeh|Kraton]] at [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] on [[23 April]] [[1874]], in which he declared: "Mijne heeren, ik beschouw den oorlog met Atjeh als geëindigd" (Gentlemen, I consider the war with Atjeh ended). Borel treats the speech as the supreme example of the self-deception that allowed the Dutch to depart [[Atjeh (Borel account)|Atjeh]] on [[26 April]] [[1874]] in the belief that the campaign was over, when in fact it had barely begun. The speech "maakte op zijne toehoorders bepaald een pijnlijken indruk"; "Qui veut trop prouver, ne prouve rien"; "niemand believed him."

## The Text of the Speech

In the speech van Swieten first praised the troops' "dapperheid, ijver en volharding" (bravery, zeal and perseverance). Then came the famous opening: "Mijne heeren, ik beschouw den oorlog met Atjeh als geëindigd." He supported the declaration with a series of optimistic reasons. The enemy, he said, was "geheel verslagen en ontmoedigd" (completely defeated and discouraged). The bevolking lacked wapens, munitie, kleederen and "zelfs aan de voornaamste levensbehoeften zooals rijst, zout" (even the principal necessities of life such as rice, salt). The trade was vernietigd (destroyed). The vazalstaten had accepted the Dutch flag — a reference to the submission of the [[Radja van Gigieng|radja van Gigieng]], who had accepted the Dutch flag in exchange for 10 000 rijksdaalders in klinkende munt. He promised the troops that they would have nothing to do but rest; Kota Radja, he predicted, would become "eene aangename gezellige garnizoensplaats" (a pleasant sociable garrison place). The speech closed with the customary expressions of gratitude and good wishes.

## The Reaction

The speech "maakte op zijne toehoorders bepaald een pijnlijken indruk" — made on its hearers a decidedly painful impression. Borel's commentary is sharp: "Qui veut trop prouver, ne prouve rien" (he who proves too much proves nothing); "niemand believed him" — nobody believed him. The pain of the audience was, in Borel's reading, the pain of men who knew the speech was false but who could not yet say so. The [[Battle of Lampoe Oek (16 April 1874)|échec at Lampoe Oek]] of [[16 April]] [[1874]] — just one week before — had cost "bijna 100 dooden en gewonden" and had revealed the existence of a strongly fortified enemy stelling near [[Longbatta]] of which the Dutch had known nothing. The [[Desecration of Dutch graves at Lampoeloe (1874)|desecration of Dutch graves at Lampoeloe]] had not yet occurred, but the [[Acehnese attack on Penajoeng (11 April 1874)|coordinated Acehnese massed attack on Penajoeng]] of [[11 April]] [[1874]] had already shown the falsity of the claim that the enemy was "geheel verslagen en ontmoedigd." The speech was, in Borel's account, a public act of self-deception by a commander who, "te goed soldaat" not to know the truth, chose the comfort of the optimistic formula over the unpalatable reality.

## The Consequences

The afscheidsrede had immediate strategic consequences. On [[24 April|24]]–[[25 April]] [[1874]] the troops returning to Java were ingescheept. On [[25 April]] van Swieten boarded the stoomschip *Prins Alexander der Nederlanden* — the same vessel that had carried him thither. On [[26 April]] [[1874]] a "saluut uit de zware vuurmonden der oorlogschepen" announced his departure. "Met zijn vertrek was het tweede bedrijf van het Atjehsche drama afgespeeld" (with his departure the second act of the Atjeh drama had been played out). The very first night after van Swieten's departure — the night of [[26 April|26]]–[[27 April]] [[1874]] — [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] was "bij herhaling door den vijand gealarmeerd"; the entire bezetting came under arms; at the east side of the Kraton, the Atjehneezen approached so close that "de aldaar geplaatste schildwachten hen duidelijk konden hooren spreken." The [[Desecration of Dutch graves at Lampoeloe (1874)|desecration of Dutch graves at Lampoeloe]] followed on the morning of [[26 April]], and the enemy attack on Marassa the same night. The afscheidsrede thus stands in Borel's account as the supreme example of the gap between Dutch optimistic proclamation and Atjehnese reality — a gap that would, in the [[Crisis at Kota Radja (May–June 1874)|crisis of May–June 1874]] that followed, nearly cost the Dutch the entire vestiging.
