**De onderwerping van Atjeh** ("The Subjugation of Atjeh") is a strategic and political treatise published in [[1893]] by [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] — then a major of infantry and former Chief of Staff in [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]] — in which he argued that the Dutch defensive strategy in [[Atjeh]] had failed and that only a rigorous, systematic application of coercive measures, above all the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] backed by naval power, could bring about the complete subjugation of the [[Sultanate of Aceh|Achehnese sultanate]]. The brochure, published at 's-Gravenhage by [[De Gebroeders van Cleef]] and at Batavia by [[G. Kolff & Co.|G. Kolff and Co.]] — the title-page bears both houses in parallel — and printed by the Typ. Zuidholl. Boek- en Handelsdrukkerij, was written in October [[1892]] and issued in [[1893]]; it constitutes Van Heutsz's first published strategic argument and the intellectual foundation for the aggressive policy he would implement as Governor from [[1898]] onward. It is also the principal *ex ante* text of the [[Pacificatie van Atjeh|pacificatie]] doctrine and the document against which [[C. Th. van Deventer|Van Deventer]]'s [[1908]] parliamentary critique of the post-conquest administration is measured. The book opens with the warning that "De Atjeh-oorlog knaagt aan ons Koloniaal bezit, hij moet eindigen" (the Atjeh-war gnaws at our Colonial possession; it must end).

## Purpose and Argument

Van Heutsz wrote the brochure in response to the protracted failure of Dutch policy in Atjeh. The [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], begun in [[1873]], had dragged on for nearly twenty years without producing the subjugation that was its stated objective. Van Heutsz diagnosed the cause as a perpetual oscillation between systems — each change of governor or minister bringing a new policy, each new policy signalling weakness to the [[Achehnese|Atjehers]], who interpreted every shift as proof that the Dutch "did not know what they wanted" and were "too weak to carry a principle through consistently." The [[Atjehers|Atjehers]], he wrote, had learned this lesson from experience: at [[Kemala|Kemala]] shortly before the departure of [[General van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]], the chiefs and elders had declared that they would not submit before the impending change of administration, because "experience had taught them that with the Hollanders every change of administration brings a change in administrative ideas."

Van Heutsz's central thesis was that a free and warlike people never submits except under compulsion — "Een vrijheidlievend en krijgshaftig volk onderwerpt zich niet anders dan gedwongen". He argued that the only instrument capable of producing that compulsion, without excessive cost to the Dutch treasury, was the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] — a system of controlled maritime trade that placed the Dutch "on the money-chest of Atjeh" and enabled them to reward, punish, and compel obedience at will. He rejected both the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentratie-stelsel]] (concentration system) and the rigorous blockade as primary strategies — the former as a mere display of weakness, the latter as a temporary war measure that produced bitterness without lasting authority — and argued that the scheepvaartregeling, properly and forcefully applied, was the permanent instrument of both compulsion and prosperity.

The book was written from the perspective of an author who, as former *Chef van den Staf*, had served under successive Governors and had personally witnessed every policy reversal since [[1874]]. Van Heutsz signs the title-page as *gewezen Chef van den Staf in Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*, a stance that lets him claim both insider knowledge (he had been at Kota-Radja through the [[Van der Heijden]] conquests of [[1878]]–[[1879]]) and outsider independence (he was no longer in office when he wrote). The book is dedicated to no one and bears no ministerial authorisation: it is a private printed memorandum, intended for the political and military class in The Hague and Batavia. The book was written in direct response to two events: the [[1892]] appointment of the eleventh Governor of Atjeh (Generaal Pompe), and the warning that the retired [[Van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] had addressed to the Minister of Koloniën in a letter dated [[23 November]] [[1891]] from Utrecht — "dat telkens veranderen van politiek; dat gebrek aan eene vaste consequent doorgezette richting" — which Van Heutsz takes as the rhetorical starting point of his tract.

## Survey of Dutch Policy, 1873–1892

The brochure opens with a systematic review of Dutch policy from [[1873]] to [[1892]], organised by the successive governorships and their shifting strategies. Van Heutsz catalogues eleven Governors of Atjeh — [[Van Swieten]], [[Pel (governor)|Pel]], [[Wiggers van Kerchem]], [[Diemont]], [[Van der Heijden]], Pruijs van der Hoeven, [[Laging Tobias]], [[Demmeni]], [[Van Teijn|Van Teijn]], Pompe van Meerdervoort, Deijkerhof — and sets the catalogue against the simultaneous rotation of five Governors-General (Loudon, van Lansberge, 's Jacob, van Rees, Pijnacker Hordijk) and thirteen Ministers van Koloniën — together with "zevental algemeene secretarissen". The point of the catalogue is the diagnosis: that "de Regeering den juisten weg nog niet weet aan te geven" (the Government does not yet know how to indicate the correct path) because every landvoogd and every minister undoes the policy of his predecessor.

### The Van Swieten System (1874)

[[Jan van Swieten|Generaal van Swieten]], whose instructions dated [[26 May]] [[1874]] (Besluit of the [[J. Loudon|Gouv. Gen. Loudon]] No. 1) and his note for [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Kolonel Pel]] dated [[22 April]] [[1874]], prescribed the occupation of a small but strong position in the most important part of the valley of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], which should be actively defended while political policy completed what the weapons had so fortunately begun. Van Heutsz praised Van Swieten as both an excellent statesman and a military commander, thoroughly acquainted with Dutch capabilities, and argued that had his system been followed, political policy would have soon revealed the correct path for the subjugation of the interior and the dependencies without the expenditure of vast military resources.

### The Pel System (1875)

After Van Swieten's departure, military operations for the expansion of the occupied position consumed nearly all attention. In [[1875]], on the proposal of [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Generaal Pel]], the [[Governor-General van Lansberge|Gouv. Gen. van Lansberge]] abandoned Van Swieten's prescribed path entirely and resolved on a large-scale offensive with the weapons in the valley of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. Pel's letter to the Governor-General, dated [[30 April]] [[1875]] (published as Appendix IV in the second volume of [[Kielstra|Kielstra]]'s *Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog*), laid out a plan for the closure of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] from the sea between [[Kroeng Raja|Kroeng Raja]] and [[Kroeng Raba|Kroeng Raba]] and the occupation of the [[VI Moekims|VI Moekims]], which would reduce the inland population to a collection of inland villages dependent on Dutch-controlled supply. Van Heutsz regarded this as a well-thought-out system whose completion would have posed the question of what further political measures were needed — a question Pel would certainly have answered correctly had he not died during his campaign in the beginning of [[1876]].

### The Van der Heijden Conquests (1878–1879)

Van Heutsz devoted the most sustained admiration in the brochure to the campaigns of [[K. van der Heijden|Generaal van der Heijden]]. The conquest of the [[XXII Moekims|XXII Moekims]] and [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI Moekims]], the submission of [[Habib Abdurrahman|Habib Abdoer Rachman]] and [[Tekoe Moeda Baid|Tekoe Moeda Baid]] on [[13 October]] [[1878]], the reduction of the [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI Moekims]] and the expedition to [[Selimoen|Selimoen]] — these were, Van Heutsz wrote, operations of which "we all know the brilliant results." He quoted Van der Heijden's dayorder of [[5 September]] [[1879]] in full, with its careful statement that "large-scale military operations will presumably no longer be necessary, but for a considerable time the strong support of the military will be required to introduce a good administration and create a regulated order of things."

Van Heutsz argued that Van der Heijden had the "brand under the foot" at [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] — a position no governor before or after him had attained — and that his dismissal in the beginning of [[1881]] was "irresponsible." He further praised Van der Heijden's [[Nota of 19 April 1880|Nota of 19 April 1880]], which laid out the political measures needed to crown the military conquest with lasting subjugation, and argued that had Van der Heijden been allowed to implement these measures — particularly the scheepvaartregeling as later refined by [[Scherer|Scherer]] — the complete subjugation would have been achieved in short order.

### The Civil Governor and the Concentration (1881–1884)

Van Heutsz described the period from [[April 1881]] to [[March 1883]] — the administration of the first civil governor — as one of rapid deterioration. The [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] proposed by [[Scherer|Scherer]] was not introduced; the conquered positions were progressively abandoned; the army was reduced to a "passive role, sometimes even the humiliating role of target." He quoted [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]]'s own assessment that his predecessor had "managed in two years to turn [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], where at his entry everything pointed to a peaceful solution, into a region where no one belonging to the government, or even showing signs of goodwill toward the government, was safe outside our fortifications."

The [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] ordered in [[June 1884]] by the [[Staten-Generaal|States-General]] in secret session, and proclaimed by [[Governor-General van Rees|Gouv. Gen. van Rees]] on [[19 August]] [[1884]], was, Van Heutsz argued, a display of weakness that the [[Atjehers|Atjehers]] immediately recognised as forced rather than voluntary — despite the Governor-General's proclamation that they would "never succeed in driving us back a single step." The system combined a withdrawal into a concentrated line at [[Kota-Radja|Kota-Radja]] with a rigorous closure of the entire coast from [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] to [[Troemon|Troemon]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]]. Van Heutsz argued that the closure was the correct instrument but was undermined by [[A. J. E. Demmeni|Generaal Demmeni]]'s half-hearted implementation, which produced a "twofold thing" — a defective scheepvaartregeling alongside a partial blockade — that satisfied no one. The closure, however, was the immediate precedent for the prescription of the brochure itself.

### The Cajolery Period (1885–1892)

After the withdrawal of the blockade in [[1885]] (Staatsblad No. 85 of [[1885]] repealed Staatsblad No. 105 of [[1884]]), Van Heutsz described the policy as "the most wretched system that can be imagined": the Dutch waited in their concentrated position, without exercising any compulsion, for the [[Kemala|Kemala]] party to offer its submission. This produced what Van Heutsz called a "policy of cajolery" — the paying of monthly allowances (about f 8000 a month, much of which flowed onward to Kemala and to the priest) to chiefs who rendered no service, the sending of emissaries and gifts to [[Kemala|Kemala]], and the vain hope that the [[Atjehers|Atjehers]] would eventually prefer Dutch administration to the old order under the sultan. He quoted [[Napoleon|Napoleon]]'s dictum on ruling subject Mohammedan peoples: "Obey for them, is fear."

### The Van Teijn Governorship and the Closure (1887–1891)

Van Heutsz served as Chief of Staff under [[General van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] and described his governorship in considerable detail. He praised Van Teijn's energy in driving out [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]]'s bands from the Dutch line in [[1887]] and [[1888]], and his systematic introduction of the coastal closure from [[January 1888]] onward — beginning with a partial closure of the [[Westkust|West Coast]], extending to the whole [[Noordkust|North Coast]] and [[Westkust|West Coast]] by the beginning of [[1890]]. Van Heutsz described the closure as "not a blockade but a scheepvaartregeling" — a temporary coercive measure whose purpose was to compel submission and then be replaced by a permanent controlled-trade system. The closure of the Noord- en Westkust, completed by the beginning of [[1890]], had by early [[1891]] produced "een effect … dat zelfs de verwachting van Generaal van Teijn overtrof" — an effect that surpassed even Van Teijn's own expectation.

Van Heutsz also recorded his disagreement with Van Teijn on the question of [[Herstel van het sultanaat|herstel van het sultanaat]] (restoration of the sultanate). Van Teijn shared the government's view that the restoration of the sultanate under [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed|Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] was the correct means of obtaining submission; Van Heutsz argued that it was a fundamental error.

## The Bugis Origin of the Sultanate

Van Heutsz included a detailed account of the origin of the present sultan's lineage, which he stated was not correctly given in [[Veth|Veth]]'s *Atchin en zijne betrekkingen tot Nederlands*, [[Gerlach|Gerlach]]'s *Atjeh en de Atjineezens*, or [[K. F. H. van Langen|van Langen]]'s *De inrichting van het Atjehsche staatsbestuur onder het sultanaat*. According to Van Heutsz, two Bugis brothers (*Daengs*) from [[Wadjo|Wadjo]] (on [[Celebes|Celebes]]) had set out to trade. One went directly to [[Benkoelen|Benkoelen]]; the other, sailing around [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], was wrecked on [[Poeloe Bras|Poeloe Bras]], found by [[Loong|Loong]] fishermen, and brought to [[Loong|Loong]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]]. He prospered and was called *Daeng Mancoer Boegis*. His son, *Tekoe Loöng*, settled at [[Tanam|Tanam]] near [[Nesoch|Nesoch]] and married a granddaughter of [[Soetan Mogal|Soetan Mogal]] (also known as [[Sultan Iskander Sanie Mogajat Sjah|Sultan Iskander Sanie Mogajat Sjah]]), who was a son of [[Sultan Mohamad Iskander Sjah van Pageroeyoeng|Sultan Mohamad Iskander Sjah van Pageroeyoeng]] and had married the daughter of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Sultan Makota Alam Iskander Moeda]] ([[1606]]–[[1636]]). The offspring of this marriage, *Maharadja Lela Malajoe*, became *ferdanamantrie* (first minister) at the court of [[Sultan Djohar Alam Imadoedin Sjah|Sultan Djohar Alam Imadoedin Sjah]] around [[1723]]. When Djohar Alam died, the chiefs of the [[V Moekims Montassik|V Moekims Montassik]] proclaimed a courtier, [[Sjamsoel Alam|Sjamsoel Alam]], as sultan, but Maharadja Lela Malajoe persuaded the assembled chiefs in the [[Balei Blerong Sri|Balei Blerong Sri]] in the [[Kraton Atjeh|Kraton]] to elect his son, *Potjoet Din*, as sultan. To secure this election, he sent each of the [[Sagi|sagi]] chiefs of the [[XXII Moekims|XXII]] and [[XXVI Moekims|XXVI]] ten *katies* of gold, to [[Tekoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Tekoe Kadli Malikoel Adil]] two *katies*, and to [[Tekoe Nek Poerba|Tekoe Nek Poerba]] of the [[IX Moekims|IX Moekims]] and [[Tekoe Nek Radja Moeda Setia|Tekoe Nek Radja Moeda Setia]] of [[Merassa|Merassa]] five *katies* each. Potjoet Din was proclaimed under the title [[Sultan Alaïdin Achmad Sjah|Sultan Alaïdin Achmad Sjah]] — the first sultan of the present lineage, placed on the throne by bribery.

From this event dates the custom of *[[Djenamoe Atjeh|Djenamoe Atjeh]]* — the payment of thirty-two *katies* of gold by each new sultan upon accession. The section is the genealogical-constitutional heart of Van Heutsz's argument against the herstel-van-het-sultanaat programme (printed pp. 41–77 of the brochure): he traces the Polim-line, the Iskander Moeda tradition, and the succession disputes of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to demonstrate that the sultanate had never commanded effective allegiance over the coast, and that restoring Mohamad Daoed would merely prolong "een politiek van cajoleeren" (a policy of cajolery).

## The Argument Against Restoration of the Sultanate

Van Heutsz devoted a sustained argument to the question of [[Herstel van het sultanaat|herstel van het sultanaat]] (restoration of the sultanate). He noted that the idea had been supported by [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]] (since [[1879]]), [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]], [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]], [[Governor-General van Rees|Gouv. Gen. van Rees]], [[Minister van Bloemen Waanders|Minister van Bloemen Waanders]], [[Minister Sprenger van Eijk|Minister Sprenger van Eijk]], and [[General van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] — the last named having revived the programme in [[1886]] on the prompting of Laging Tobias's December [[1886]] article in the *Indische Gids* and of Fransen van de Putte's *Parlementaire Redevoeringen*. His reasons for opposing it were:
1. The present sultan's lineage was of Bugis origin and had never possessed real power.
2. It had been placed on the throne by bribery, not by hereditary succession or force of arms.
3. At the time of the Dutch arrival, the sultan was "of nearly no authority and influence, besides nearly reduced to poverty."
4. The chiefs had not wanted a sultan in [[1874]] and had chosen [[Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed|Toeankoe Mohamad Daoed]] only in [[1878]] under the influence of [[Toeankoe Haschim|Toeankoe Haschim]] and [[Habib Abdurrahman|Habib Abdoer Rachman]] — and then chose a child who could not govern, clearly indicating that each chief wished to hold the reins in his own territory.
5. The restoration would create an authority the coastal chiefs did not want above them, and would compel them to support that authority financially against the Dutch.
6. The chiefs of the principal coastal states — particularly [[Pedir|Pedir]] — had explicitly stated that they did not want a sultan under Dutch protection, because such a sultan would wield indirect influence over the dependencies and draw income from them.

## The Proposed Strategy

Van Heutsz's proposed strategy for the complete subjugation of Atjeh rested on two pillars: the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] on the coasts, and a combination of political pressure and limited military action in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]]. The prescriptive section occupies pp. 78–115 of the brochure; here Van Heutsz works systematically through the four-point plan that Resident [[Scherer]] had set out in his lecture of [[20 January]] [[1891]] to the Indisch Genootschap (smokkelbestrijding; bondgenootschap with the weak coastal statelets; isolatie van Groot-Atjeh; pacificatie with or without a sultan), rejects each, and substitutes his own programme. The three pillars of the prescription are marine-enforced closure of the entire coast, a rigorous scheepvaartregeling on the ports retained under Dutch control, and the assumption of the *hatsil* with the abrogation of the sultanate-idea.

### Marine-enforced closure of the entire coast

Van Heutsz insists that the closure of the *Noord- en Westkust* which [[Van Teijn|Generaal van Teijn]] had introduced in stages from January [[1888]] and completed in the beginning of [[1890]] must be extended across the entire coast, "van Tamiang op de Oostkust tot Troemon op de Westkust". This was the measure Demmeni had been ordered to apply in [[1884]] but had refused to carry through, and which Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884 had authorised but Staatsblad No. 85 van 1885 had withdrawn.

### The Scheepvaartregeling on the Coasts

All trade — import and export — on the entire coast of Atjeh, from [[Tamiang|Tamiang]] on the [[Oostkust|East Coast]] to [[Troemon|Troemon]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]], should pass through a few designated places under Dutch control, where Dutch establishments with military garrisons, administrative officials, customs, postal, telegraphic, police, and harbour personnel would supervise all movements. The remaining coastal places would be closed to foreign trade. Van Heutsz endorsed [[General van Teijn|Van Teijn]]'s selection of [[Edi|Edi]], [[Telok Semawé|Telok Semawé]], and [[Segli|Segli]] on the East and North Coasts, and [[Telok Kroet|Telok Kroet]] (with the establishment on [[Poeloe Raja|Poeloe Raja]]), [[Melaboeh|Melaboeh]], and [[Tampat Toean|Tampat Toean]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]], with [[Oleh-leh|Oleh-leh]] as the free port for [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] itself. Van Heutsz quotes Scherer's own warning that "eene scheepvaartregeling zonder of met eene onvoldoende marine is als een leger zonder of met gebrekkige wapenen" (a scheepvaartregeling without or with an inadequate navy is like an army without or with defective weapons).

The scheepvaartregeling was not merely a system of trade regulation; it was an instrument of compulsion. The Dutch would sit on the money-chest of Atjeh — "plaatse ons in den volsten zin des woords op de geldkist van Atjeh". Fixed revenues would come from the [[hatsil|hatsil]] (the Sultan's share, now the Dutch's by right of succession under the *18 artikelen* sworn by each submitting statelet), import duties on luxury goods, and the [[opiumpacht|opium farm]] or regie. In [[1890]] a European firm had offered to farm the opium-pacht at f 250,000 per month for the first year, rising to f 400,000; the existing *licentie*-system and the retail margin made the offer plausible, and Van Heutsz treats the figure as proof that the revenue-yield of the closed coast could meet the cost of the war. Variable revenues — fines — would be imposed on statelets that did not comply with Dutch instructions, deducted from the proceeds of their export products. Van Heutsz concludes the prescription: "Het aangewezen middel was toen scheepvaartregeling, wat Scherer wilde" (the indicated means was then the scheepvaartregeling, as Scherer wanted it) — but applied, as Scherer had failed to apply it, with a marine force adequate to make the closure real. The *hatsil*, the tractates, and the abrogation of the sultanate-idea together form the third pillar: Van Heutsz rejects both the cajoleering-policy of paying chiefs "traktement" and the sultanaat-restoration policy, insisting that the Dutch should themselves exercise the fiscal rights formerly reserved to the sultan.

### Oleh-leh as Free Port and the Poeloe Waij Plan

Van Heutsz argued that [[Oleh-leh|Oleh-leh]] should be a free port — free of import duties except on opium — so that the valley of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], where the representative of the Dutch government resided, would become a centre of trade and prosperity. He also supported the plan for [[Poeloe Waij|Poeloe Waij]] — an island on the great shipping route from Europe to China, Japan, and Australia — to be developed as a major maritime station with a free port, a direct steam connection with the Netherlands, and all the facilities that trade and shipping had hitherto found only at [[Singapore|Singapore]].

### Political Pressure in Groot-Atjeh

In [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], Van Heutsz argued, the scheepvaartregeling alone would not produce submission without some use of military force. The resistance bands, led by fanatical priests and composed of adventurers from various regions, would not disperse merely because trade was controlled. The correct approach was a combination of: (a) the prohibition of export from the Dutch line; (b) the payment of monthly allowances to chiefs who rendered genuine service, with the cancellation of allowances as an instrument of pressure; (c) the imposition of heavy fines on chiefs who harboured resistance bands or permitted shooting at Dutch posts; and (d) swift, limited military expeditions to deliver "bloody blows" when necessary, followed by immediate return to the line.

### The Naval Requirement

Van Heutsz emphasised that the scheepvaartregeling required a strong and fast navy for its enforcement. Smuggling would be attempted on the extensive coastline; war contraband and opium would be the most profitable smuggled goods; armed prahus would blockade each other's harbours. A scheepvaartregeling without or with an inadequate navy was "like an army without or with defective weapons."

## The Most Cited Passage

The most frequently cited passage of the brochure — quoted by [[C. Th. van Deventer|Van Deventer]] in the [[1908]] [[Tweede Kamer]] debate on [[Van Heutsz's report on Atjeh (June 1908)|Van Heutsz's June 1908 report]] — is:

> "Wij zitten niet in Atjeh om vliegen te vangen en maar steeds onze millioenen weg te werpen; Atjeh moet onderworpen worden, dat is de zaak, en om dit resultaat na al ons morsen en knoeien nog te erlangen moet Atjeh zelf betalen wat daarvoor voortaan nog aan dit land wordt ten koste gelegd."

— "We are not in Atjeh to catch flies and to keep throwing away our millions; Atjeh must be subjugated, that is the point, and to obtain this result after all our messing and botching Atjeh itself must pay what is henceforth to be spent on this land."

The passage is preserved by [[W. H. Vermeulen|Vermeulen]] with the citation: "J. B. van Heutsz, *De onderwerping van Atjeh*, blz. 79."

## The 1908 Parliamentary Debate on the Brochure

[[C. Th. van Deventer|Van Deventer]] suspected that Governor-General [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] had permitted [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Van Daalen]] to act in accordance with the [[1893]] view set out in the brochure, and had only abandoned him when the [[Van Heutsz's inspection tour of Atjeh (December 1907)|December 1907 inspection tour]] had made the unworkability of "this harsh and grasping policy" plain. The [[Van Heutsz's report on Atjeh (June 1908)|June 1908 report]] — with its emphasis on the restoration of the *hoofden* (chiefs) and on "tactvolle omgang" (tactful dealings) — was, on this reading, the repudiation of the [[1893]] brochure's doctrine that Atjeh itself must pay for its own subjugation.

IJzerman replied that it was unfair to pursue Van Heutsz with a brochure fifteen years old. "Hebben dan alleen de grote mannen in Nederland het recht om er oude plunje op na te houden?" — Have then only the great men in the Netherlands the right to keep old clothes in the closet? [[A. W. F. Idenburg|Idenburg]] — Minister of Colonies — endorsed the IJzerman view: it was Van Heutsz's greatest merit that he had put an end to the Dutch feeling of impotence with respect to the [[Buitenbezittingen|Buitenbezittingen]].

## Significance

The brochure *De onderwerping van Atjeh* is the first published statement of the strategic doctrine that Van Heutsz would carry into execution from [[1898]] onward. Its argument for the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]] as the principal instrument of subjugation — supplemented by limited military action and the systematic exploitation of economic leverage — anticipates the combination of blockade, controlled trade, and mobile offensives that eventually ended the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. The [[Concentratiestelsel]] — the defensive perimeter around Kota-Radja from which Van Heutsz in the 1890s was still recoiling — was, in his Governorship, transformed into a forward base; the *scheepvaartregeling* he prescribed in 1893 became the fiscal instrument of his pacification; and the marine-enforced closure he demanded in 1893 became the [[Blokkade|blokkade]] of the 1898–1903 campaigns. Its detailed knowledge of [[Achehnese|Atjehnese]] political structures, family connections, and regional interests reflects Van Heutsz's service as Chief of Staff and his close familiarity with the territory. The 1908 parliamentary exchange between Van Deventer and IJzerman on the continuing authority of the brochure is the canonical exchange on the relation between the Van Heutsz of [[1893]] (the conqueror) and the Van Heutsz of [[1908]] (the pacificator). The brochure is also the principal source on the policy debates of 1873–1892: Van Heutsz quotes at length from Kielstra's *Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog*, from the dagorder of [[5 September]] [[1879]] (the [[Day-order of 5 September 1879|Dagorder of 5 September 1879]]), from Scherer's lecture of [[20 January]] [[1891]], from the *Indisch Militair Tijdschrift* and the *Indische Gids*, and from Van Teijn's warning letter of [[23 November]] [[1891]]. It is the only place where the policy oscillations of eleven Governors are narrated in a single sustained argument by an author who had himself been a witness.

## Account in du Croo’s Generaal Swart (1943)

Its motto ran: "The Atjeh war gnaws at our colonial possession. It must end; let us at last show the civilised world that we are capable of ending it!" Its two principal conclusions ran wholly parallel to Snouck Hurgronje's argument: "a freedom-loving, warlike people submits only under compulsion," and "only he who shows that he possesses the power to make his will respected will bring Atjeh to submission." Van Heutsz still held in it to a *[[scheepvaartregeling]]*, and his aim was not so absolute as the scholar's, "but in spite of that, this brochure at last brought the new, strong and correct note. It was accordingly regarded by many either with disapproval or as not serious — only many younger men understood and endorsed it."
