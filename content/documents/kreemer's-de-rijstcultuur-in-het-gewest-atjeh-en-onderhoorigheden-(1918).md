**Kreemer's *De rijstcultuur in het gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*** ("Rice Cultivation in the Aceh Region and Dependencies") is a [[1918]] monograph by [[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]], *Archivaris van het [[Atjèh Instituut|Atjèh Instituut]]* (Archivist of the Atjèh Institute), published as **Mededeelingen No. 6** of the Atjèh Instituut in *Tijdschrift van het Koninklijk Nederlandsch Aardrijkskundig Genootschap*, deel XXXV, afl. 3 (1918), and printed for the *Directeuren der Maatschappij ter Bevordering van het Natuurkundig Onderzoek der Nederlandsche Koloniën* by [[E. J. Brill|E. J. Brill]] in Leiden. The work is the principal Dutch-language technical and ethnographic study of [[rijstbouw|rice cultivation]] across the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] — covering [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], the [[Noordkust van Atjeh|Noordkust]], the [[Oostkust van Atjeh|Oostkust]], the [[Alasland|Alasland]] and the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust]] — and a key source for the early-twentieth-century "rice question" of whether Atjeh could become the rice-storehouse of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra's East Coast]].

## Publication Context

The monograph appeared as the sixth in the *Mededeelingen* (Communications) series of the [[Atjèh Instituut|Atjèh Instituut]], the Amsterdam-based colonial research institute that had been founded in [[1914]] to coordinate scholarly study of the Atjeh region. The series aimed to make available in offprint form the most important specialist articles on Atjeh appearing in the major Dutch learned journals. The host journal, the *Tijdschrift van het Koninklijk Nederlandsch Aardrijkskundig Genootschap* (Proceedings of the Royal Dutch Geographical Society), was the principal geographical outlet of the Netherlands, and its choice as host for the offprint placed Kreemer's work squarely in the geographical-colonial literature.

The offprint was further "overgedrukt voor de Directeuren der Maatschappij ter Bevordering van het Natuurkundig Onderzoek der Nederlandsche Koloniën" — printed for the directors of the Society for the Promotion of Natural-Science Research in the Dutch Colonies, the parent body of what later became the *Koninklijke Vereeniging Indisch Instituut*. The printer was the Leiden house of [[E. J. Brill|E. J. Brill]], the same house that four years later would publish Kreemer's two-volume *Atjèh* (1922-23).

## Author and Position

[[J. Kreemer|J. Kreemer]] signs himself on the title page as "Archivaris van het Atjeh-Instituut" — Archivist of the Atjèh Institute. By [[1918]] Kreemer had already spent nearly two decades in close contact with northern Sumatra. He had entered the Binnenlands Bestuur as a *Kontroleur B.B.* on [[Sumatra's Westkust|Sumatra's Westkust]] in [[1907]], had traversed the [[Gajo-weg|Gajo-weg]] in [[1917]], and would later conduct his twenty-month field research for *Atjèh* (1916-1917) in [[Koeta Radja|Koeta Radja]]. The 1918 rice monograph thus sits mid-career: after his first field articles but before the great synthesis of 1922-23.

## Scope and Contents

The work is organised as a technical-and-ethnographic survey of the entire rice economy of the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden*. Kreemer systematically treats:

### The Typology of Rice Fields

Kreemer opens by distinguishing four kinds of [[sawah|sawah]] (rice fields) in Atjeh, each with its own Acehnese, Gayo and Alas terminology:

- *rijstladang's* — dry upland rice fields (called *ladang* in Malay)
- *kunstmatig bevloeide velden* — artificially irrigated fields (*oemòng it peunieuëh* in Acehnese; *oemo mòc-woih* in Gayo; *djoemo mèlawe* in Alas)
- *van regen afhankelijke velden* — rain-dependent fields (*oemòng preh ie oedjèn*)
- *moerassawah's* — swamp-rice fields (*oemòng paja*; *oemo paja* or *oemo rölöm*; *djoema rawang*)

He also treats abandoned fields (*pòk ròh*), terraced sawahs (*oemòng teurata*), and the *blang* (a complex of fields, named in Atjeh after a nearby *gampong*; in Gayo called *oemo*).

### The Annual Cycle of Cultivation

Kreemer describes in detail each stage of the cultivation cycle, with the full Acehnese-Gayo-Alas vocabulary: the making of seedbeds (*kweekbedden*), the two sowing methods (the *poela* or transplanting method, and the *taboe* or direct-broadcasting method), the working of the field with plough (*langaj*), hoe (*tjangkòë*), and trampling-buffalo (*poeblòh*), the planting of seedlings, the weeding, the ripening stages (thirteen named stages from *manja'* to *masak*), the harvesting, threshing, winnowing, measuring, storage, drying, and hulling. Each operation has its own extensive vocabulary, and Kreemer systematically gives the equivalents in the three languages of the region.

### Rice Varieties

Kreemer lists the named varieties grown in [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], including late-ripening varieties (*padé rajeu'*: *P. keudah*, *P. keudah gloempang*, *P. keudah mandé*, *P. poetéh rajeu'*, *P. reunti paja*, *P. radén*, *P. lansajé*, *P. poetrë banan*, *P. bàh sàmboh*, *P. peunjaman*), early-ripening varieties (*padé cut*: *P. aka*, *P. poetéh cut*, *P. seudoët*), and glutinous varieties (*leukat*: *L. adang*, *L. djeuët*, *L. bring*, *L. oendé*, *L. dadéh*, *L. lidah kendéui*, *L. boengong seulimèng*, *L. bin*, *L. koemoenjan*).

### Labour Systems

The work documents the four labour arrangements under which the Atjeh rice crop is grown: own cultivation (*kòh keudòe*), wage-labour (*kòh peung-oepah*), sharecropping (*mawaih*, known as *mawah* in Gayo and *blah dudé* or *boeloeing dudé* in Alas), mutual aid (*toempoh* or *bantoe*; collective *meuseuraja*), and reciprocal labour with the obligation of return (*taelòng-moclòng* or *meugantché* or *meunoerdé*; Gayo *panggèlò*; Alas *plingari*).

### The Regional Survey

Kreemer then walks through each of the five major divisions of the *Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden* and gives the area under rice, the type of cultivation, the average yield, and the local consumption-and-export balance:

- [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] — ~20,000 ha cultivated, mostly rain-dependent, average yield 8 *pikoel*/ha
- [[Pidie|Pidie]] (Noordkust) — ~16,000 ha irrigated in the Pidie valley, "the rice-granary of Atjeh"
- [[Meureudu|Meureudu]] — ~3,000 ha, partly irrigated
- [[Bireuen|Bireuen]] — irrigated around Samalanga, II Moekim Toenòng and Keudé Peudada; rain-dependent eastward to the Peusangan delta
- [[Lho Seumawè|Lho Seumawè]] — almost all rain-dependent
- [[Lho Soekon|Lho Soekon]] — about three-quarters rain-dependent; ~30,000 ha still to be opened
- [[Takengon|Takengon]] — only living-water irrigated fields, around the [[Laut Tawar|Tawar-meer]] and the [[Pegasing|Pegasing]] valley
- [[Peureula|Peureula]] (Oostkust) — ~6,000 ha in cultivation and ~6,000 ha more suitable; ladang cultivation important in Témiang
- [[Blang Kedjérén|Blang Kedjérén]] — usually a surplus in good years
- [[Lokop|Lokop]] — produces only about three-quarters of local consumption
- [[Alasland|Alasland]] — only living-water fields; surplus exported to Singkel and the Karo highland
- [[Simaloer|Simaloer]] — almost all rain-dependent, regular rice imports (e.g. 1,394,285 kg in [[1915]])
- [[Singkel|Singkel]] — Benèeten-Singkelsche has some fields but suffers flooding; Simpang Kanan better; Simpang Kiri worse; Troemon largely self-sufficient
- [[Tapa Toean|Tapa Toean]] — Laboh Hadji the best rice land; then Meuké and Manggéng; Kloeët often short
- [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]] — much ladang cultivation in the upper regions; much swamp-sawah in the lowlands; the Seumagan valley on the left bank of the Kroeëng Seunagan, upstream of the Kroeëng Rama, an irrigated complex with good clay soil
- [[Tjalang|Tjalang]] — mostly ladang and swamp rice; the Daja landschappen a favourable exception
- [[Daja|Daja]] — the historic rice-storehouse of the Westkust
- [[Panté Tjeureumèn|Panté Tjeureumèn]] — the only landschap on the Westkust with artificial irrigation

### The "Rice Question" (Rijstvraagstuk)

The closing section of the monograph addresses the contemporary policy question raised in [[1916]] in a series of articles in the *[[Deli Courant|Deli Courant]]* (Nos. 162, 167, 171, 172, 183, 184) by [[Ackerstaff|Ackerstaff]], [[Van Tubergen|Van Tubergen]] and [[J. H. Heyl|J. H. Heyl]]: could Atjeh, with improved irrigation, become the rice-storehouse of [[Sumatra's Oostkust|Sumatra's East Coast]], supplying the needs of the Deli tobacco-and-rubber plantations? Kreemer cites the calculation of [[J. H. Heyl|Landbouwadviseur J. H. Heyl]] that of the 1.6 million *pikoel* of rice required by Deli, "nearly half" could come from Atjeh, more specifically from [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]], [[Pidie|Pidie]], [[Meureudu|Meureudu]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]], [[Lho Soekon|Lho Soekon]] and [[Peureula|Peureula]]. Kreemer prints Heyl's table estimating that ~84,160 ha of Atjeh sawah could produce ~1,622,800 *pikoel* of rice, of which the surplus available for export to Deli would be ~786,300 *pikoel*.

### A Note on Rice Imports

Kreemer notes that despite producing enough rice for the indigenous population, Atjeh imported roughly 200,000 *pikoel* of rice annually — chiefly *Rangoon-rijst* imported by the [[Atjehsche Handelmaatschappij|Atjehsche Handelmaatschappij]] and a few European firms, because Atjeh-grown rice was not preferred by the non-indigenous population (the plantation workers of [[Langsa|Langsa]], the timber-workers of [[Sinabang|Sinabang]], the naval-coal station at [[Sabang|Sabang]], and the military garrison, which alone consumed ~14,000 *pikoel* of foreign rice per year).

## Method and Sources

Kreemer's method combines direct field observation (he had travelled extensively through the regions described), the extensive use of native terminology recorded from informants, the consultation of the colonial agricultural advisors — in particular the *Landbouwadviseur* [[J. H. Heyl|J. H. Heyl]], whose report in the *Deli Courant* of [[8 August]] [[1916]] (No. 183) Kreemer cites — and the prior literature, including [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *Het Gajöland en zijne bewoners* (1903) for Gayo rice-names and the *Encyclopaedisch Bureau* brochure on Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden for rice-import statistics.

## Significance

The 1918 *De rijstcultuur* is significant for the encyclopedia of Aceh history on three grounds. First, it is the most detailed single Dutch account of the rice economy of Atjeh at the moment when the colonial state was beginning to consider the systematic agricultural development of the pacified territory. Second, its dense vocabulary of Acehnese-Gayo-Alas agricultural terms preserves the technical lexicon of highland Sumatra rice cultivation at the close of the long nineteenth century. Third, its closing "Rijstvraagstuk" section is the principal contemporary source for the colonial-era debate on the integration of the Atjeh and Deli regional economies — a debate that would be taken up again in the [[Atjèh Instituut|Atjèh Instituut]]'s *De Rijkdom van Atjeh* (1923) and in Joh. Langhout's *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* (1923).
