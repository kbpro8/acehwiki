**Treaty of London 1824** was an international diplomatic convention concluded between Great Britain and the Kingdom of the Netherlands on [[17 March]] [[1824]], which established European spheres of influence in Southeast Asia and guaranteed the independence of the [[Aceh Sultanate]]. This treaty served as the legal baseline for nineteenth-century Dutch colonial policy on Sumatra until its revision in [[1871]].

## Terms and Stipulations Regarding Aceh

Under the provisions of the [[Treaty of London 1824]], Great Britain ceded its Sumatran possessions, including Bencoolen, to the [[Dutch East Indies Government]]. In diplomatic notes appended to the convention, the Netherlands pledged to respect the sovereignty of the [[Aceh Sultanate]] and ensure maritime security along the northern Sumatran coast.

As documented by General [[G. G. Booms]], these guarantees legally prevented the [[Royal Netherlands East Indies Army]] from launching direct territorial invasions against [[Kraton of Aceh]] for nearly fifty years.

## Revision and Historical Significance

The protective framework of the [[Treaty of London 1824]] was formally dismantled by the Anglo-Dutch Sumatra Treaty of [[1871]], which withdrew British objections to Dutch extension of sovereignty in Sumatra. In the [[Parliamentary Note on Relations with Aceh 1872-1873]], Minister of Colonies [[I. D. Fransen van de Putte]] cited the commitments of [[1824]] to explain the long delay in military intervention prior to the [[First Aceh Expedition]] of [[1873]].
