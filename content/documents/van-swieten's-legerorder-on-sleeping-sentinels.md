**[[Van Swieten's legerorder on sleeping sentinels|Van Swieten's legerorder on sleeping sentinels]]** was the field order issued by [[Jan van Swieten|generaal van Swieten]] at [[Kota Radja|K. Radja]] in [[1874]] — the "legerorder" in which, instead of applying [[Crimineel Wetboek art. 92|article 92 of the Criminal Code]] to sentinels found asleep on post, he instructed the chiefs of corps to punish them disciplinarily, citing an anecdote of Napoleon I who once took the rifle of a sleeping sentinel and stood his post himself. Borel considers the order unlawful and highly damaging — a characteristic instance of the "overdreven zachte en humane" (excessively soft and humane) war that van Swieten waged and of the want of discipline that, in Borel's judgement, prolonged the Atjeh-oorlog.

## The Order and Its Rationale

The legerorder was issued in response to the recurring discovery of Dutch sentinels asleep on post at K. Radja and the surrounding bivouaks — a problem that became acute after the [[Departure of Jan van Swieten from Atjeh (April 1874)|departure of the hoofdmacht]] on [[26 April]] [[1874]], when the [[Bezettingskorps in Atjeh|bezettingskorps]] under [[Generaal Pel|kolonel Pel]] was reduced to a fraction of its nominal strength and the troops were exhausted by malaria, cholera, the kwade moesson and the constant alarm. Instead of applying [[Crimineel Wetboek art. 92|article 92 of the Criminal Code]] — which criminalised sleeping on post and prescribed court-martial (krijgsraad) — van Swieten instructed the chiefs of corps to punish sleeping sentinels disciplinarily. To justify the order he cited an anecdote of Napoleon I, who, coming upon a sleeping sentinel, is said to have taken the man's rifle and stood his post himself rather than have him punished. The anecdote — apocryphal or not — was, in van Swieten's reasoning, an example of the humane indulgence that a great commander might extend to a tired soldier. The order was issued in writing and distributed to the corps.

## Borel's Critique

Borel's critique of the legerorder is twofold. First, he considers it unlawful: the [[Crimineel Wetboek art. 92|Crimineel Wetboek, article 92]] was the statutory provision governing sleeping on post, and a field order could not lawfully withdraw its application. To do so was to substitute the commander's personal discretion for the law of the land. Second, he considers it highly damaging: the *padi-snijden* concession to [[Langkroek]], the refusal to shell kampongs, the prohibition on burning kampongs or cutting standing padi, the gifts to spies — all these "overdreven zachte en humane" measures had their counterpart in the legerorder's indulgence of sleeping sentinels. The consequence was a relaxation of discipline at the very moment when the bezettingskorps was besieged, exhausted and reduced to a shadow of its strength. The night attack on Longbatta-Mesigit of [[22 November]] [[1875]] — when a band of ~50 Atjehneezen climbed the low borstwering and "met de blanke [[Klewang|klewang]]" hacked among sleeping manschappen, wounding 13 including 2 officers — was, Borel suspected, "hoogst waarschijnlijk" the fault of schildwachten who had neglected their post. The legerorder was, in Borel's reading, an invitation to such disasters.

## The Broader Pattern

The legerorder on sleeping sentinels is, in Borel's account, one of a series of documents and acts by which van Swieten conducted what Borel calls an "overdreven zachte en humane" war. The [[Padi-snijden on the Langkroek sawah (1874)|padi-snijden]] concession, the prohibition on burning kampongs, the gifts to spies, the refusal to shell kampongs within range of the Kraton — all were of a piece with the legerorder. The result was a war fought with "schop en spade" rather than with the kracht der wapens, and a bezettingskorps that was, by the end of May [[1874]], on the brink of collapse. [[Generaal Pel|Pel]] — who inherited the bezettingskorps on [[26 April]] [[1874]] — reversed most of these policies: from [[28 April]] [[1874]] he ordered the daily bombardment of kampongs within range; he ordered the cutting of standing padi; he burned kampongs from which the Dutch were fired on. The legerorder on sleeping sentinels was, however, not formally withdrawn — and the want of discipline it had licensed continued, in Borel's judgement, to cost Dutch lives through the attacks on Longbatta-Mesigit ([[22 November]] [[1875]]), on Lemboe-N.O. ([[23 January|23]]–[[24 January]] [[1876]]) and on the Atoeh detachment ([[13 February]] [[1876]]). It stands, in Borel's account, as a small but instructive document of the war.
