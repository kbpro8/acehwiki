A religious pandit of Arab-Gujarati origin who played an important role in Acehnese religious and literary life, arriving in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]] and resolving the disputed mystical questions of the day by opposing [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]'s pantheism. At the command of [[Iskandar Thani|Sultan Iskandar Thani]], he wrote the *Sirat al-mustaqim* ("The Straight Path"), begun in [[1634]] but only completed in [[1644]], as well as the *Bad' khalq as-samawat wal-arwah*. He is the author of the two Malay treatises edited in facsimile by [[P. Voorhoeve]] in [[1955]]: the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]] and the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq*]].

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Pandit Raniri

**Pandit Raniri** — referred to by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] as "the pandit Raniri" — was a Gujerati-born Malay-Muslim scholar and author whose treatise supplies one of the few firm chronological anchor-points in the early history of the [[Acheh|Achehnese]] Sultanate. He is best known in the present work as the authority for the year [[1636]] as the date of the death of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Meukuta Alam]] (Iskandar Muda).

Raniri states in his treatise — printed at [[Mecca]] in 1281 Hegira on the margin of another edition — that he came to Acheh in [[1637]] while [[Iskandar Thani]] was king. Since Iskandar Thani succeeded Meukuta Alam, this testimony fixes the death of the great Sultan at 1636, a date that "is confirmed as the correct date" against the uncertainties of native chronicle and tradition. Raniri's arrival thus falls in the very first year of the new reign, and his presence in Acheh places him at the centre of the learned and religious life of the capital under the wealthy sultans of the seventeenth century.

Snouck Hurgronje's reference to Raniri occurs in a passage distinguishing the strangers who wrote works of theology and law under the wealthy sultans in [[Banda Acheh]] from the native-born ulamas of later times. The pandit belongs emphatically to the former category: an immigrant man of letters patronised at the Achehnese court, whose influence "outside Acheh was at least as noticeable as within it." He is the most celebrated of the Indian-Arab scholars who, during Acheh's golden age, made the capital a centre of Islamic learning for the entire [[East Indian Archipelago|Indian Archipelago]].

Raniri is also remembered in the wider historiography of the Malay world as a rigorous opponent of the mystical doctrines that had flourished at the Achehnese court in the preceding generation. His arrival under Iskandar Thani marks the beginning of a reaction against the [[Wujudiyyah|wujudiyyah mysticism]] associated with [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and his school. The new Sultan is said to have put the disciples of the heretical mystic to death and to have ordered his books burned — an episode of religious persecution in the generation after [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]]'s reign in which Raniri's puritanical influence is commonly reckoned to have played a decisive part.

Beyond the bare facts cited by Snouck Hurgronje in Vol. I, Raniri's own writings — including the [[Bustan al-Salatin|*Bustan al-Salatin*]] and his legal and dogmatic works in Malay — remain fundamental sources for the religious and political history of seventeenth-century Acheh and the wider Malay-Indonesian world.

## Expanded account from *Twee Maleise geschriften van Nūruddin ar-Rānīrī* (Voorhoeve, 1955) — Biography

The full form of Raniri's name, as given by [[P. Voorhoeve|Voorhoeve]] on the authority of the colophon of the [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*]], was **Nūruddīn Muḥammad b. ʿAlī b. Ḥasanji b. Muḥammad Ḥamīd ar-Rānīrī al-Kurashī ash-Shāfiʿī** — a nisba indicating Arab (Qurayshī) descent and Shāfiʿite legal school, attached to the town of [[Ranir]] near [[Surat]] in [[Gujarat]] (Gudjarāt). Voorhoeve notes that the identification of Ranir with the town near Surat — once contested — has been placed beyond doubt by the researches of [[G. W. J. Drewes|Drewes]]: "er kan thans geen twijfel meer aan bestaan, dat zijn vaderstad Rānīr bij Sūrat in Gudjarāt was." His family was part of the network of Indian Arabs of mixed blood who maintained contact with the motherland by correspondence and study journeys, and who in turn maintained relations with Indonesia — that is, in this period, especially with [[Acheh]].

Neither Raniri's year of birth nor that of his death has been preserved. Voorhoeve fixes, however, the firm chronological pegs of his career:

- He arrived in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]], the first year of the reign of [[Iskandar Thani]].
- In [[1641]] he is found, together with a number of high functionaries of the Achehnese court, taking part in the ceremony of erecting the tombstone for Sultan [[Iskandar Thani|Iskandar II]].
- The dates of his Malay works, so far as they are reliably attested, fall between [[1634]] and [[1644]].
- In [[1644]] he returned to [[Ranir]].
- He must have died before [[1666]].

This chronology was established by [[H. Djajadiningrat|Djajadiningrat]] in *TBG* 69 (pp. 109–111), a study which Voorhoeve candidly confesses he had overlooked when composing his earlier notes in *BKI* 107 (p. 353 ff.). Among the corrections imposed by Djajadiningrat's research is the redating of the [[Sirat al-mustaqim|*Ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*]]: although begun in [[1634]] (that is, before Raniri's arrival in Acheh), the work was — as Djajadiningrat showed — only completed in [[1644]]. The knowledge of Malay letters displayed in it need not, therefore, have been acquired before [[1637]].

## The Gujarati milieu and family connections

The coastal towns of Gujarat in this period had a very international population. Ships regularly sailed thence to the [[Straits of Malacca|Strait of Malaka]], and among the mercenary troops of the sixteenth century were "Javanese" — that is, in this context, Indonesians. The Malay language was certainly not unfamiliar in the harbour towns; and the family of Raniri had, even before his own time, maintained relations with [[Acheh]]. Between [[1580]] and [[1583]] his uncle [[Muhammad Djailani Hamid]] arrived in Acheh. He wished to teach logic, rhetoric, dogmatics, and the doctrine of duties (*plichtenleer*), but found little interest. There was a great influx only for teachers of mysticism. So the scholar sailed on to [[Mecca]]. Some years later he returned, provided with the *venia docendi* as a teacher of mystical theology, and then had great success. Raniri learned from his uncle's experiences: when he himself left for Acheh he was thoroughly prepared. He knew the *[[Bahasa Djawi]]*, the Malay that was the language of the court in Acheh, and had already written two works in it, while a third was in progress.

## Sufi initiation: the Rifaʿiyya and the Bā Shaybān silsila

Raniri had also penetrated into mysticism. He was initiated into the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya order]], founded by the saint [[Ahmad Rifa'i]], whose tomb at [[Mocha]] is reverenced. His shaikh, of Hadrami descent but born in India, was **Sayyid Abū Ḥafṣ ʿUmar b. ʿAbdallāh Bā Shaybān** ([[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban]]). Bā Shaybān had studied in Arabia but later settled again in India, where he was received into the order by [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]] at [[Surat]]. He lived chiefly at [[Bijapur|Bījāpūr]], where he enjoyed the protection of the ruler Muḥammad ʿĀdilshāh; he died in [[1656]] at [[Bilgram|Bilgrām]]. The celebrated **Shaikh Yūsuf al-Makassar** ([[Yusuf Makasar]]), who in [[1644]] left Celebes for [[Mecca]], studied with the same Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān; he also numbers Raniri among his teachers.

The teacher of Bā Shaybān — and Raniri's spiritual grandfather — was [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]], born in [[1561]] at [[Tarim]] in South Arabia. He came to Gujarat at the age of nineteen, where he succeeded his famous grandfather as religious teacher, first in the capital [[Ahmadabad|Ahmadābād]] and later at [[Surat]]. He died at Surat in [[1620]]. Better known as an author is his uncle, **ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Aydārūs** ([[Abd al-Qadir al-Aidaros]]), who died in [[1628]] at Ahmadabad. Many of his writings are preserved, including correspondence exchanged with a scholar at [[Mocha]]. ʿAbd al-Qādir's father — Muḥammad's grandfather — had come from South Arabia in [[1551]]; ʿAbd al-Qādir's mother was an Indian. This, Voorhoeve concludes, was the milieu from which Raniri sprang: "Indische Arabieren van gemengden bloede, die door correspondentie en studiereizen het contact met het moederland onderhielden, en die anderzijds in verkeer stonden met Indonesië, dat wil in deze tijd in het bijzonder zeggen: Atjèh."

## The religious climate in Acheh and Raniri's polemical mission

In Acheh, Raniri as an orthodox mystic had during the reign of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] ([[1607]]–[[1636]]) nothing to expect. The "heterodox mystic" [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] dominated the spiritual life of the court, and even after his death in [[1630]] his disciples remained in honour with the sultan. But after Iskandar Muda died in [[1636]] the spiritual climate in Acheh changed. Shortly after the accession of [[Iskandar Thani|Iskandar II]], Raniri arrived in the country; in the presence of the sultan he refuted the adherents of the extreme [[Wujudiyyah|Wujūdiyyah mysticism]] in a public debate. From that time forth he enjoyed royal favour, both from Iskandar II and from his successor [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Ṣalīyatu'd-Dīn]] (Taj al-Ālam). This public disputation — the [[Raniri's public debate against the Wujudiyyah in Acheh|confrontation at the Achehnese court]] — is the dramatic hinge of Raniri's Achehnese career.

## Raniri's Malay writings before his arrival in Acheh

An unresolved question is where Raniri composed the two Malay works which he had completed before his arrival in Acheh in [[1637]]: a work on the articles of faith (the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]]) and a collection of sacred traditions in Arabic with Malay translation. Voorhoeve had once supposed, on the strength of a passage in the *Sajarah Banten*, that Raniri sojourned in Mecca before his departure for Acheh, and that he might be the scholar referred to as "Ibn ʿAlī" or "Ibn ʿAlam" who refused to return with the Banten envoys to their country. On further reflection Voorhoeve withdrew this hypothesis: it seems much more probable that the "Ibn ʿAlam" of the *Sajarah Banten* is in fact **Ibn ʿAlān** — that is, Muḥammad ʿAlī Ibn ʿAlān as-Siddīqī (d. 1057 H / [[1648]]), of whom we know for certain that he was repeatedly consulted from Banten: by Sultan Abū 'l-Mafāḥir ʿAbd al-Qādir on the *Naṣīḥat al-mulūk* of al-Ghazālī (on which Ibn ʿAlān had written a commentary), and by Abū 'l-Maʿālī Aḥmad on the *Tuḥfa al-mursala* of Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh. Among the Arabic sources used by Raniri, Voorhoeve found not only famous and widely diffused books but works of Indian authors and literature current in India, and not a single writing by older contemporaries in Mecca. To learn Malay, Raniri had no need of distant travel: there were Malays enough in Gujarat, and his uncle will have spoken that language too. Whether he wrote his first Malay books there nonetheless remains an open question.

## Works composed in Acheh

Of the works Raniri composed in Acheh, most are directed against the [[Wujudiyyah]]. This side of Raniri's activity has been treated at length by [[C.A.O. van Nieuwenhuijze|C. A. O. van Nieuwenhuijze]] in his Leiden dissertation *Sjamsu'l-Dīn van Pasai* (1945) and in a special study in *BKI* 104. In those studies two short texts and a portion of Raniri's "book of sects" — the [[Tibjan fi ma'rifat al-adyan|*Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*]] — were already edited. Voorhoeve's [[1955]] edition gives the complete *Tibjān* in facsimile from the Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 3291, together with the shorter [[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|*Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq*]] — itself directed entirely against the Wujūdiyyah — reproduced from the manuscript in the Maxwell collection of the [[Royal Asiatic Society]] in London. The *Tibjān* enumerates the pre-Islamic religions and the sects within Islam, with the aim of warning the faithful against all these aberrations and in particular against the doctrine of the heretical Wujūdiyyah.

In addition to the works already named, Raniri's writings include the [[Hall al-zill|*Ḥall aẓ-ẓill*]], the [[Djawahir al-'ulum|*Jawāhir al-ʿulūm*]] (which survives in a London manuscript and which cites al-Jīlī's [[al-Insan al-Kamil|*al-Insān al-kāmil*]] repeatedly, as does the *Ḥall aẓ-ẓill*), the lost [[Lata'if al-asrar|*Laṭāʾif al-asrār*]] (already cited in the *Tibjān* as a source for the refutation of the Wujūdiyyah), and the *Badʾ khalq as-samāwāt wal-arwāḥ*.

## Sources of the *Tibjān*

Voorhoeve's examination of Raniri's sources is itself a substantial contribution to Malay religious philology. The principal source of the *Tibjān* is the *at-Tamhīd fī bayān at-tawḥīd* of [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|Abū Shakūr as-Sālimī]], a Persian Shāfiʿite of the second half of the fifth century H. More than a quarter of the text of the *Tibjān* is translated literally from the *Tamhid*, especially its last chapter on orthodoxy and the rejection of innovation (*bidʿa*). The Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 5664 — a Banten copy sent to Leiden by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in [[1906]] — preserves a text practically identical to Raniri's own exemplar, including shared errors such as "Kubtiyah" (Copts) for "al-Muʿattila" and a lacuna that caused Raniri to announce four groups of the Tanāsuḥiyyah while treating only three.

A second source was the [[al-Insan al-Kamil|*al-Insān al-kāmil*]] of [[Abd al-Karim al-Jili|ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī]], from which Raniri drew his account of the Barāhima. The schematic overview of the seventy-two sects — six groups of twelve — comes from the widely diffused popular tract known as the *Maʿrifat al-madhāhib*, sometimes attributed to [[Abu Hanifa|Abū Ḥanīfa]], of which an Arabic exemplar in Leiden (Cod. Or. 1255) shows a strikingly close correspondence with the *Tibjān*, even in peculiar variants. The section on the quasi-Sufi sects is drawn from the small tract *Kitāb fī bayān madhāhib al-mutaṣawwifa* of [[Najm ad-Din an-Nasafi|Najm ad-Dīn an-Nasafī]].

## Raniri's polemical method in the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq*

The *[[Hudjdjat as-siddiq li-daf az-zindiq|Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq li-dafʿ az-zindīq]]* — "the argument of the pious one for the refutation of the heretic" — sets out, in Voorhoeve's analysis, four positions on the relation of God's being to the world: (1) that of the dialectical theologians (*mutakallimūn*), who distinguish two beings, the necessary being of God and the contingent being of the created world; (2) that of the Sufis, who accept the theologians' doctrine but employ a different terminology in which "being" (*wujūd*) signifies only the divine essence, while the world is mere manifestation (*maẓhar*) and shadow (*ẓill*); (3) that of the philosophers (*falāsifa*), who hold God's being and the world to be co-eternal; and (4) that of the heretical Wujūdiyyah, who teach that being is one and identical with God, who is "enclosed in all that is created" — so that "the world = God, God = the world." Raniri's method is to distinguish sharply the orthodox Sufi position from the heretical Wujūdiyyah by arguing that the latter, in saying "God and world are one," contradicts both revelation and reason; he cites in support authorities including [[Ibn Arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] (in the *Futūḥāt Makkīya*), [[Ahmad al-Mahaimi|ʿAlī b. Aḥmad al-Mahāʾimī]] (in the *Ithbād an-naṣīḥa* and *Irāʾat ad-daqāʾiq*), Muḥammad b. Faḍl Allāh (in the *Tuḥfa al-mursala*), and the lost *Laṭāʾif al-iʿlām*. He argues, finally, that ecstatic utterances (*shathīyāt* — [[Shathihiyat|shathihiyat]]) such as *anā al-ḥaqq* ("I am the Truth") of [[al-Hallaj]] are to be regarded as latah-like trance states, not blasphemous self-deification, when uttered by entranced mystics; but that the same utterances, when pronounced by one in his senses, deserve death and eternal punishment.

## Significance

Raniri occupies a singular position in the religious history of Acheh: he is at once the agent of the orthodox reaction that closed Acheh's "golden age" of mystical poetry, and the author whose works preserve, even in their polemic, the names, doctrines, and very words of the writers whose books were burned at his instigation. Without the *Tibjān* and the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq* our knowledge of [[Hamzah Pansuri]]'s *[[Asrar al-'arifin|Asrār al-ʿārifīn*]] and *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] and of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]]'s *[[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn*]] would be far scantier than it is. The Voorhoeve edition of [[1955]] gave scholars, for the first time, a complete facsimile of the *Tibjān* and of the *Ḥujjat aṣ-ṣiddīq* — placing Raniri's Malay polemics, and the lost literature they alone preserve, at the centre of any future history of Achehnese religious thought.
