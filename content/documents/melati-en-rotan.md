***Melati en rotan: Gedichten van F. Kunst, pentekeningen van Johanna Roberti*** ("Jasmine and Rattan: Poems by F. Kunst, pen-drawings by Johanna Roberti") was a Dutch-colonial poetry collection published at [[Bandoeng|Bandoeng]] in [[1947]] — comprising poems by **F. Kunst** with pen-drawings by **Johanna Roberti** — that contains two poems specifically concerned with [[Acheh|Atjeh]]: "*Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)*" ("West Monsoon (Coast of Atjeh)"), a landscape-poem on the Atjehnese coast in the west-monsoon rains, and "*[[Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh|Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh]]*" ("On the Death of General Swart, the Pacifier of Atjeh"), an elegy for the *Pacificator van Atjeh*. The collection — published in the immediate aftermath of the Japanese occupation and the early phase of the Indonesian National Revolution — is the principal literary source for the Dutch-colonial poetic memory of Atjeh in the mid-twentieth century.

## Publication

The collection was published at [[Bandoeng|Bandoeng]] in [[1947]] — the year of the [[Linggadjati Agreement|Linggadjati Agreement]] and the early Dutch military actions of the Indonesian National Revolution — by the Vacuüm-Handel Maatschappij Vortex and the Uitgeverij en Boekhandel W. Mellink. The collection comprises poems by **F. Kunst** with pen-drawings by **Johanna Roberti**, and is preserved in the [[Koninklijke Bibliotheek|Koninklijke Bibliotheek]] under the shelfmark 1105 AR2.

The collection's *Opdracht* (dedication) — addressed to those "*schandelijk verlaten en vergeten*" ("shamefully abandoned and forgotten") in the secret camps of the interior — fixes the collection's occasion in the Japanese occupation and the Indonesian National Revolution: the poems are dedicated to the Dutch internees who, in the editor's framing, had been abandoned by the motherland. The collection's literary-political horizon is therefore that of the late-colonial Dutch community in the Indies, in the moment of its destruction.

## The Atjeh-related poems

The collection contains two poems specifically concerned with [[Acheh|Atjeh]]:

1. ***Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)*** — a landscape-poem on the Atjehnese coast in the west-monsoon rains, depicting the rain on the coconut-plantations, the swollen rivers carrying trees to the sea, the smell of rotting leaves and overripe fruit, and the sudden late-sun breaking through the clouds. The poem is recorded in the [[Westmoesson (Kust van Atjeh)|separate article]] on the poem.
2. ***[[Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh|Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh]]*** — an elegy for the *Pacificator van Atjeh*, lamenting not the general's death but the post-war destruction of his life's work — the reduction of the country's well-being to chaos, the raging of fanatic hatred where Swart had brought peace, and "*Holland in blindheids nacht*" ("Holland in night of blindness"). The poem is recorded in the [[Bij den dood van Generaal Swart, den pacificator van Atjeh|separate article]] on the poem.

## The "Anak Laoet" Sabang poem

The collection also contains the poem "*Anak Laoet*" ("Child of the Sea"), whose second stanza fixes the bay of [[Sabang|Sabang]] — the colonial haven of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — as a place of resort for ships' passengers on the Europe–East-Asia route, with the lake *Kin der Zee* near "*Sabangs schoon belijnde bocht*" ("Sabang's beautifully contoured bay"). The poem is the literary document of the scenic quality of the Sabang bay in the colonial imagination, and is recorded in the [[Sabang|Sabang]] article.

## Significance

The *Melati en rotan* collection is significant for the encyclopedia of [[Acheh|Achehnese]] history as the principal literary source for the Dutch-colonial poetic memory of Atjeh in the mid-twentieth century. The collection's two Atjeh poems — the landscape-poem of the west-monsoon coast and the elegy for the *Pacificator van Atjeh* — together fix the two poles of the Dutch-colonial poetic imagination of Atjeh: the scenic landscape of the coast (the rain, the coconut-plantations, the swollen rivers) and the political memory of the pacification (the general who brought peace, the destruction of his work). The collection's publication in [[1947]] — in the moment of the destruction of the colonial order — gives the poems an elegiac quality that extends from the general to the colonial project itself.
