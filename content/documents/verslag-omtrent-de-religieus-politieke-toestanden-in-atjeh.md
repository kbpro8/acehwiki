**The Verslag omtrent de religieus-politieke toestanden in Atjeh** ("Report on the religio-political conditions in Atjeh") is the secret report of [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]], closed on [[23 May]] [[23 May]] [[1892]] after his seven-month residence at [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjèh]] from [[July 1891|July]] [[1891]] to [[February 1892|February]] [[1892]], that gave — in [[H. T. Damsté|Damsté]]'s words — "*een glasheldere verklaring van het Atjèh-zeer: bodem, kiem, groei, woekering, mogelijke genezing — alles werd er in behandeld*" (a crystal-clear explanation of the Atjèh malady: ground, germ, growth, infestation, possible cure — all was treated in it). The report is the foundational document of the modern Dutch understanding of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]] and the principal source of the strategic advice that [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] would execute, with the [[Corps Maréchaussée (Atjeh)|marechaussée]] campaigns and the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]], to bring the war to its military conclusion in [[1904]].

## The Composition

The report was the fruit of Snouck Hurgronje's seven-month residence at [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjèh]] from [[July 1891|July]] [[1891]] to [[February 1892|February]] [[1892]], during which he had been housed (through the good offices of the governor [[Pompe van Meerdervoort|Pompe van Meerdervoort]]) in a dwelling hired from an Arab at Peukan Atjeh, at some distance from the European society — exactly as he had wished. During those seven months "treasures were gathered on linguistic and ethnological ground, richer and fairer than ever yet won, anywhere in Indië; and also in religious and political respect the understanding of the Atjèher was completely achieved." The patient was examined, the clinical picture drawn, the diagnosis made, and the doctor ventured even to indicate the cure.

## The Structure

The report comprised four chapters:

1. **The first chapter** treated those life-expressions of the Atjèhers in which no Islamic influences were to be discerned.
2. **The second chapter** dealt with those life-expressions in which Islamic influences dominated.
3. **The third chapter** treated the principal moments of the war.
4. **The fourth and last chapter** gave the conclusions to be drawn from all the foregoing.

## The Diagnosis

The report gave a crystal-clear explanation of the Atjèh malady. The good way, when the Dutch conflict with Atjèh arose, would have been — under show of force — to make relations with the chiefs reachable from the coast, to inform oneself of their mutual relations, and to design a plan for a march through Groot-Atjèh. If there had been no time for that, then after the taking of the kraton one should have made a march through the three sagi's and occupied some commanding points, to cure the Atjèher of his "onoverwinnelijkheids-waanzin" (invincibility-madness).

Instead, the Dutch had made the mistake of overestimating the value of the Sultan's residence (and its inhabitants); and when they sat down there to "wait", the Atjèher saw that as weakness. The above-landers thought that it was through the cowardice of the below-landers that the *dalam* had fallen. And so there remained untouched: the national treasure of self-conceit and infidel-contempt, with on top of it a very special hatred against the Dutch upstarts; the consequence of the Dutch annexation of Atjèhse dependencies along the coasts of Sumatra, the opposition to the slave trade, the Dutch attempts to force the Sultan to promises, treaties, and compensations, and then the talk that went around about Dutch administrative methods elsewhere: masters- and culturist-institutions, soldier-recruiting, taxes, pass-compulsion, and other freedom-restrictions. The war was from the beginning a people's war, but badly led and without plan.

## The Oelama Period and the Holy War

When the Dutch went to "sit", bands soon formed that attacked them and made the Dutch waiting attitude impossible. The Dutch then stepped down from the Van Swieten system and tried another; and later they changed again, and then again. But the good lesson the Atjèher got from the "blinden generaal" Van der Heyden — the fighting spirit was extinguished and the entered rest was appreciated. Even a Teungkoe Tjhik (den sultan) submitted, and the Habib Abdoerrahman saw to it that he was home before the storm by a timely voluntary surrender. We were certainly on the right way, though still to be improved.

Then the one-eyed general was sent away — "verbannen" (banished), according to the Atjèher, who cites that as a sample of governmental unreliability. The new period proved a deceptive dream-image. Those who had submitted noticed that they had been mistaken; those who had hesitated withdrew and laughed at the quicker ones. The puppets went to dance again. And yet the lust to unite and to act, also outside the own territory, was no longer general among chiefs and population; it was no longer a true people's war, but general was the recognition that attacking the kafirs was pious sport. It were, however, more "who wanted and could" who led therein: and so one saw relatives of the imeum of Loeëng Bata, a Teukoe Adan and a Teukoe Oema, act as free band-leaders, who recruited *panglima's* (band-leaders), with as *rakan's* (band-members): scum who could now give rein to their robbing and murdering instincts in a respectable, even religion-sanctioning way; students who, urged by their teachers, wanted to become *tjahét* (blood-witnesses); and hot-headed youths, eager for fame.

The bands so formed were not large, but in the villages, where one recognised the piety of their striving, they were with their captured weapons irresistible; and against the Dutch too they could do something. By repeatedly taunting the Dutch in the positions to which they wished to limit themselves, it succeeded best to make it very unpleasant for them. The Dutch went over the top again and withdrew within the concentrated line. Now it was clear that the kafirs were not almighty! So there was no excuse either, when someone refrained. Teungkoe di Tiro sent word far and wide: with such weak means such great success had been achieved; how great would the success not be if the war were properly fought and no one withdrew! The chiefs were admonished, the population preached to, and the cooperation became general; it became a religious war under the leadership of Teungkoe di Tiro.

## The Prescription

What the Dutch had to do, according to Snouck Hurgronje:

1. **Keumala (the sultan c.s.) to be left alone** — as a wholly indifferent impotence, completely indifferent to the course of things.
2. **The actively hostile party not to be negotiated with, but to be beaten** — for their doctrine and their interest required that they would yield only to force; a number of commanding points in Groot-Atjèh would then inevitably have to be occupied for a considerable time.
3. **To strive for the restoration of trust in Dutch intentions**, by promoting agriculture, industry, and trade.

Of the application of those three principal rules the work of pacification depended; without them the rapporteur held it hopeless.

## The Supplementary Advice

Moreover, in the interest of continuity in the administration and to prevent errors as in the last years were industriously committed, the report advised:

a. **The establishment of an information bureau or civil staff** — whose officials would devote themselves exclusively to gathering and working up data for the increase of the knowledge of land and people of Atjeh.
b. **The improvement of the corps of native officials** and more continuous administrative care for the education of reachable young prominent Atjèhers.
c. **The observance of a better tone in dealing with Atjèhse chiefs and subordinates**; and written instruction to that effect for new-arrived officials and officers in Atjèh.
d. **Communication to the government of all letters of political significance received from and sent to Atjèhse chiefs.**

## The Fate of the Report

The report was offered to the government on [[28 May]] [[28 May]] [[1892]]. The *[[Raad van Indië|Raad van Indië]]* said that it was beautiful and that it must be printed. But to act according to the advice given therein, was not thought of! After admiring the report for two months, a copy was sent to the governor of Atjèh; but the governor-general wrote a note behind it, to moderate the impression it might make on the heer Deijkerhoff. His Excellency was willing to believe with the rapporteur that with merely peaceful means Groot-Atjèh would not soon be pacified; but whether Snouck Hurgronje's expectations of a resumed aggression would be realised?... And besides, there was no need to talk about that — it would cost too much. The Dutch had therefore better put the principal goal that brought them to Atjèh first: to render the Atjèher harmless towards the outside world, and not to break their heads with Atjèh-problems such as the restoration or not of the sultanate, the payment or not of the chiefs outside the line, and so on.

But the line — could that not be somewhat simplified? About that the governor should consult with the resident [[Scherer|Scherer]]! It was clear: the governor-general found the Atjèh-receipt of Snouck Hurgronje no speed-piece; and the governor of Atjèh accordingly waited a small year before preliminarily reacting to it. There was, assured the heer Deijkerhoff, very hard work going on at Atjèh, and for those hard workers it was rather a nuisance that they should also give time to the treatment of brochures and reports of "experts" from outside Atjèh, whose writings opened no new perspectives. Such the just-appeared brochure of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], and then the report of Dr. Snouck Hurgronje.

On [[17 July]] [[17 July]] [[1893]] the report got the promised Atjèhse turn: "*Een ieder berijdt zoo het een of andere stokpaardje*," wrote governor Deijkerhoff, "and Dr. Snouck Hurgronje rides the hobby-horse of Islam." That caused him to transfer all weight in the Atjèh matter to oelama's and moslemin; but he was mistaken in denying the influence of the Sultan's family; he was mistaken in representing the war as a people's war; and he was mistaken in thinking that the population thought to be waging the *prang sabil* or holy war. The population spoke of a "war on her purse" and of "Orang moslimin jang tjari ontong sendiri" (Muslims who seek their own profit). Since Teungkoe di Tiro and Habib Samalanga had died, the resistance had slackened, and looked nothing like a people's war! The resistance still presenting itself, which bore the character of *chouannerie*, one should let wear out by keeping it slumbering. The best means to make the bands run off was to prevent them from entering the field with the Dutch.

## The Publication as *De Atjèhers*

The *Raad van Indië* saw that it was beautiful and advised to publish the report. But in that report came fierce criticism of the work of [[Der Kinderen|Der Kinderen]] and [[L. W. C. van den Berg|Van den Berg]] with respect to Atjèh: on their regulation of the judiciary and on what they had said and written about Atjèh. The governor-general asked whether the author had objection to the publication desired by the Council. No, the author had no objection, but he had more material, especially on ethnological ground. Better it seemed to him to take up only what was worth knowing for the public. So it happened, and in [[1893]] the first volume of *[[De Atjehers (Snouck Hurgronje)|De Atjèhers]]* appeared from the Landsdrukkerij at Batavia. What stood therein printed to the disadvantage of Der Kinderen and Van den Berg was much milder than what the governor-general and the Raad van Indië had meant to publish. Yet those gentlemen felt themselves not flattered and went to audience with the minister [[Van Dedem|Van Dedem]] to protest against the publication by the government of a work in which such unsalted criticism occurred of their prestaties. The minister felt with them and wired to Indië that with spreading of the second volume must be waited until after receipt of a letter that would follow. The letter followed and charged the governor-general to give the author of *De Atjèhers* a talking-to and to add, as satisfaction for both criticised gentlemen, a government declaration to the second volume.

## The Teukoe Oema Footnote

It was in the report's note to advice no. 2 (with the actively hostile party not to be negotiated with, but to be beaten) that the famous footnote on Teukoe Oema appeared — the footnote that [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]] would distort into the Teukoe Oema policy that culminated in the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]]:

> "*Met sommige machtige avonturiers kan het wel zijn nut hebben, in onderhandeling te treden, daar zij niet, gelijk de oelama's, staan of vallen met de handhaving eener theorie. Een machtig man als Teukoe Oema zou, eens gewonnen, de geheele westkust en een deel der XXV Moekims voor ons toegankelijk kunnen maken. Het bestuur zou echter eerst volkomen overtuigd moeten zijn, dat zijne belangen met de onze samengingen; verder zou men, zoodra eene goede verstandhouding bereikt was, voortdurend moeten trachten die zoo te benutten, dat, wanneer Teukoe Oema kwam te sterven, het bestuur in zijne plaats kon treden, want eene uit zoo heterogene bestanddeelen samengestelde macht als die van Teukoe Oema valt dikwijls uiteen met den man, die haar schiep. Teukoe Oema is misschien de eenige, die door de sluitingsmaatregelen bij regelen getemd is, want hij heeft evenals de oelama's belang bij de verwarring, maar mits de peperhandel bloei. Verkreeg nu zijne dan krachtig geworden positie bevestiging van de kompeuni, dan zou hij wellicht haar bondgenoot willen zijn.*"

When the Teukoe Oema policy was attempted, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] advised: "*Steekt Oema zijn hand uit, neem die dan, maar houd die hand vast! gebruik Oema waar hij bruikbaar is, maar schenk hem geen werkelijk vertrouwen!*" (If Oema holds out his hand, take it, but hold that hand fast! use Oema where he is usable, but do not grant him real trust!). Deijkerhoff took the first half (taking Oema's hand) but neglected the second half (holding the hand fast); and the result was the catastrophe of [[March 1896|March]] [[1896]].

## Significance

The *Verslag omtrent de religieus-politieke toestanden in Atjeh* is the foundational document of the modern Dutch understanding of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]]. Its crystal-clear diagnosis of the Atjèh malady — ground, germ, growth, infestation, possible cure — and its prescription of the three principal rules (Keumala to be left alone; the actively hostile party to be beaten; trust to be restored by promoting agriculture, industry, and trade) together constitute the strategic analysis that [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] would execute, with the [[Corps Maréchaussée (Atjeh)|marechaussée]] campaigns and the [[Scheepvaartregeling|scheepvaartregeling]], to bring the war to its military conclusion in [[1904]]. The fate of the report — the admiration of the *Raad van Indië*, the reluctance of the governor-general, the contemptuous dismissal by [[Deijkerhoff|Deijkerhoff]] on [[17 July]] [[17 July]] [[1893]], and the distortion of the Teukoe Oema footnote into the policy that culminated in the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (March 1896)|defection of 29 March 1896]] — is one of the principal episodes in the history of Dutch colonial policy in Atjèh. The publication of the report, in modified form, as *[[De Atjehers (Snouck Hurgronje)|De Atjèhers]]* (1893–1894) — with the [[Bekendmaking Nederburgh|Bekendmaking Nederburgh]] prefatory note — made the substance of the diagnosis public.
