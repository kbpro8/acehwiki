**The Nisero affair** of [[1883]]–[[1885]] — in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s history, the *Nisero-quaestie* — was the international incident provoked by the wreck of the English ship *Nisero* in November [[1883]] off [[Panga|Panga]], in the territory of [[Teunom|Teunöm]] on [[Atjeh's Westkust|Atjèh's Westkust]], the seizure and plunder of the ship and the imprisonment of its crew by the Radja of Teunöm, and the protracted Dutch–British–Atjèhnese negotiations for the crew's release. The affair, in Kreemer's account, brought the Netherlands to the brink of international complications with Britain, exposed the limits of Dutch authority on the Westkust, and produced — through the disastrous employment of [[Teukoe Oema|Teukoe Oema]] as a Dutch agent — the conversion of Oema from uncertain ally to irreconcilable enemy. In [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s [[1893]] tract *[[De onderwerping van Atjeh (Van Heutsz, 1893)|De onderwerping van Atjeh]]*, the *Nisero-quaestie* — *de treurige Nisero-quaestie* — is cited as one of the events of the [[1884]]–[[1885]] policy context that exposed Dutch weakness on the Atjehnese coast and that the Atjehnese drew on when they concluded that the Dutch would prove "*weder spoedig te zwak*" — too weak again — to carry the [[Concentratiebesluit (19 Augustus 1884)|Concentratie]] and the [[Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884|rigorous blokkade]] through.

## The wreck and the seizure

"Het Engelsche schip Nisero strandde in November 1883 voor Panga, in het gebied van Teunöm op Atjèh's Westkust. De bemanning werd door den Radja van Teunöm gevankelijk medegevoerd en het schip uitgeplunderd. Tegenover onzen aandrang tot uitlevering van het scheepsvolk stelde de Radja zulke overdreven eischen, dat ons bestuur daarin niet kon treden. Vooral was dit het geval, toen een Engelsch oorlogsschip Pegasus in de wateren van Atjèh verscheen, daar de Radja er openlijk voor uitkwam, dat hij door zijn hooge eischen ons in oorlog met Engeland wikkelen wilde" — "The English ship Nisero stranded in November 1883 off Panga, in the territory of Teunöm on Atjèh's West Coast. The crew was taken captive by the Radja of Teunöm and the ship plundered. Against our demand for the delivery of the ship's people, the Radja set such exaggerated demands that our administration could not enter into them. This was especially the case when an English warship Pegasus appeared in the waters of Atjèh, for the Radja openly declared that he wished, by his high demands, to embroil us in war with England."

## The British intervention

The British commander of the *Pegasus* received orders from the British supreme authority to proceed to Teunöm and directly press the Radja for the release of the prisoners. The Atjèh governor — "zich niet verhelende, dat deze opdracht kwalijk overeen te brengen was met de volkenrechtelijke gebruiken" — "not unaware that this assignment was difficult to reconcile with the usages of international law" — nevertheless supported the proposed step, both to avoid difficulties with the British government and to avoid the reproach that not everything had been done or endured on our side for the liberation of the prisoners. "Toen ook deze onderhandelingen mislukten, werd door ons den 4en Januari een expeditie naar Teunöm gezonden; maar ook deze tuchtiging leidde niet tot de bevrijding der gevangenen, die inmiddels verder in het binnenland waren vervoerd" — "When these negotiations also failed, an expedition was sent to Teunöm on 4 January; but this chastisement also did not lead to the liberation of the prisoners, who had in the meantime been transported further inland."

## The Maxwell mission

The mission of Mr. [[Maxwell|Maxwell]], member of the colonial council at [[Singapore|Singapore]], to make a further direct attempt at the liberation of the prisoners, also failed. "De eischen van den Radja waren zóó hoog, dat van inwilliging geen sprake kon zijn" — "The Radja's demands were so high that of compliance there could be no question."

## The employment of Teukoe Oema and the slaughter of the boat-rowers

The governor of Atjèh then sought to achieve the goal with the help of [[Teukoe Oema|Teukoe Oema]]. "Hiertoe werd deze den 3en juli 1884 met 32 volgelingen ingescheept; toen hij met zijn volk aan land werd gezet, werden de sloeproeiers plotseling met den klewang aangevallen en op twee na allen vermoord. Oema maakte zich meester van de wapens en munitie, welke in de sloep aanwezig waren en deed zich sedert weder als een onzer ijverigste vijanden kennen" — "For this purpose he was shipped on 3 July 1884 with 32 followers; when he and his people were put ashore, the boat-rowers were suddenly attacked with the klewang and all but two murdered. Oema took possession of the weapons and ammunition that were in the boat, and from then on made himself known as one of our most zealous enemies." See [[Teukoe Oema]].

## The resolution

"The Engelsche Regeering, waarschijnlijk overtuigd van onze onmacht, bood nu hare bemiddeling aan, om te trachten niet alleen de loslating van de schipbreukelingen van de Nisero, maar in het algemeen ook de beëindiging van den oorlogstoestand in Atjèh te verkrijgen. Deze bemiddeling werd door de Nederlandsche Regeering niet aangenomen, doch de verder gevoerde onderhandelingen leidden tot de slotsom, dat namens de beide regeeringen, aan den Radja van Teunöm een gemeenschappelijke eisch werd gesteld tot uitlevering der gevangenen. Met het overbrengen van dezen eisch werden de gouverneur [[Laging Tobias|Laging Tobias]] en de heer Maxwell belast. Eindelijk gaf de Radja toe. Tegen een losgeld van ƒ100.000 werden de gevangenen uitgeleverd en de sluiting der havens van Teunöm opgeheven" — "The English government, probably convinced of our impotence, now offered its mediation, to attempt not only the release of the Nisero's shipwrecked crew but in general also the ending of the war-state in Atjèh. This mediation was not accepted by the Netherlands government, but the subsequent negotiations led to the conclusion that, on behalf of both governments, a joint demand was made on the Radja of Teunöm for the delivery of the prisoners. The governor Laging Tobias and Mr. Maxwell were charged with conveying this demand. At last the Radja yielded. For a ransom of ƒ100,000 the prisoners were delivered and the closure of the harbours of Teunöm was lifted."

## Account from *De onderwerping van Atjeh* (Van Heutsz, 1893) — the *Nisero-quaestie* as diagnostic event

Van Heutsz cites the *Nisero-quaestie* in the [[1893]] tract as one of the events of the [[1884]]–[[1885]] policy context that, in his diagnosis, had taught the Atjehnese to expect the colony's collapse. The catalogue is set out in the historical movement of the tract, in the chapter on the *cajoleering*-decade of [[1884]]–[[1886]]; the *Nisero-quaestie* appears there alongside the [[Concentratiebesluit (19 Augustus 1884)|Concentratie]] and the [[Demmeni]] *besluit* of [[13 October]] [[1884]] as one of the events that, taken together, "*onze zwakheid maar al te zichtbaar*" — made our weakness all too visible. Van Heutsz's text records the catalogue and the inference the Atjehnese drew from it:

> "Ook in de treurige Nisero-quaestie was onze zwakheid maar al te zichtbaar geweest".

The Atjehnese inference, Van Heutsz continues, was that the Dutch would prove "*weder spoedig te zwak*" — too weak again — to carry the rigorous blokkade through, and that they had "*het nut hadden leeren inzien te trekken uit Nisero-quaesties*" — they had learned to appreciate the advantage to be drawn from *Nisero-quaesties*. The plural — *Nisero-quaesties* — is the operative form: the *Nisero-quaestie* of [[1884]]–[[1885]] was, in Van Heutsz's reading, not a singular event but a *type* of event — the type of diplomatic incident in which the Dutch, confronted with a foreign power's claim on an Atjehnese coastal statelet, would prove too weak to enforce their own sovereignty and would, in the event, retreat. The Atjehnese had, in Van Heutsz's diagnosis, learned to provoke such incidents and to draw political advantage from the Dutch inability to manage them.

### The *Nisero-quaestie* and the policy debate of 1884-1885

The *Nisero-quaestie* was, in the chronology of the [[1893]] tract, contemporaneous with the [[Concentratiebesluit (19 Augustus 1884)|Concentratiebesluit of 19 August 1884]] and the [[Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884|Staatsblad No. 105 of 1884]] *rigoureuse blokkade*. The incident was, in Van Heutsz's reading, part of the policy context of the [[1884]]–[[1885]] crisis — the context in which the metropolitan *opperbestuur* imposed the Concentratie on the colony over the objections of [[Laging Tobias|Gouverneur Laging Tobias]], [[Scherer|resident Scherer]] and [[Van Langen|assistent-resident van Langen]], and in which the colony's own civil-administrative authorities warned that "*een dergelijk vertoon van zwakheid, ten zeerste zou schaden aan het Nederlandsch gezag*" — such a display of weakness would greatly harm the Dutch authority. The *Nisero-quaestie* was, in this context, the empirical proof the colony's civil-administrative authorities had warned against: the Dutch, confronted with the diplomatic incident, had exhibited the very weakness the *Concentratie* was, in the metropolitan reading, designed to conceal.

The *Nisero-quaestie* also coincided with the [[Demmeni]] *besluit* of [[13 October]] [[1884]] — the hybrid *half sluiting / half scheepvaartregeling* that Van Heutsz denounces as the worst of both worlds — and with the [[Staatsblad No. 85 van 1885|Staatsblad No. 85 of 1885]] repeal of the [[Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884|Staatsblad No. 105 of 1884]] *rigoureuse blokkade*. The Dutch, having prescribed the rigorous blokkade in [[1884]], declined to apply it, and the *Nisero-quaestie* was, in Van Heutsz's reading, one of the events that exposed the gap between the prescription and the application.

### The *Nisero-quaestie* settlement and the Westkust harbour

The *Nisero-quaestie* was, in Van Heutsz's later policy-prescription, the diplomatic fact that bound the Dutch to keep a Westkust harbour open that he otherwise wished to close. In the policy-prescription half of the [[1893]] tract, where Van Heutsz works through the four-point plan [[Scherer]] had set out in his lecture of [[20 January]] [[1891]] to the [[Indisch Genootschap]], he notes that [[Telok Kroet]] — the Westkust harbour [[Scherer]] had proposed as one of his four open ports of the Westkust — had, by the *Nisero-quaestie* settlement, to remain open:

> "Wel moet dit open blijven voor den algemeenen handel ingevolge de overeenkomst bij de Nisero-quaestie, maar dit doet niets ter zake … eene vestiging moet er toch niet komen".

The *Nisero-quaestie* settlement had, in other words, bound the Dutch by an international agreement to keep [[Telok Kroet]] open to general trade; Van Heutsz concedes the obligation but argues that it "*niets ter zake*" — nothing to the point — does, since the obligation to keep the harbour open does not oblige the Dutch to establish a *vestiging* — a settlement — there. The argument is the operational expression of Van Heutsz's general principle: that the *scheepvaartregeling* he prescribed required the Dutch to retain only those ports that were necessary for the *contrôle op handel en scheepvaart*, and that the *Nisero-quaestie* settlement's obligation to keep [[Telok Kroet]] open to general trade did not oblige the Dutch to convert that obligation into a Dutch *vestiging*.

## Significance

The Nisero affair is, in Kreemer's narrative, the moment at which the Netherlands' inability to police its own Atjèhnese territory was exposed to an international audience. The Radja of Teunöm's open intention to embroil the Dutch with the British, the failure of the Dutch punitive expedition of [[4 January]] [[1884]], the failure of the Maxwell mission, and the conversion of [[Teukoe Oema|Teukoe Oema]] from ally to enemy — all of these revealed the hollowness of Dutch authority outside the concentrated line. The eventual resolution — a joint Anglo-Dutch demand backed by a ƒ100,000 ransom — was a diplomatic victory only in the narrowest sense; it confirmed that the Dutch could not act in Atjèh without British cooperation, and that the Atjèhnese chiefs could play the European powers against each other.

In Van Heutsz's [[1893]] diagnosis, the *Nisero-quaestie* was, additionally, one of the events of the [[1884]]–[[1885]] policy context that "*onze zwakheid maar al te zichtbaar*" — made our weakness all too visible — and that taught the Atjehnese to expect the colony's collapse and to "*het nut hadden leeren inzien te trekken uit Nisero-quaesties*" — to appreciate the advantage to be drawn from *Nisero-quaesties*. It was contemporaneous with the [[Concentratiebesluit (19 Augustus 1884)|Concentratiebesluit of 19 August 1884]] and the [[Staatsblad No. 105 van 1884|Staatsblad No. 105 of 1884]] *rigoureuse blokkade*, and was, in Van Heutsz's reading, part of the same [[1884 Concentratie and blokkade debate|1884 Concentratie and blokkade debate]] that produced the [[Demmeni]] *wankelen* and the [[Staatsblad No. 85 van 1885|Staatsblad No. 85 of 1885]] repeal. The *Nisero-quaestie* settlement had bound the Dutch, by an international agreement, to keep [[Telok Kroet]] open to general trade — an obligation Van Heutsz concedes in the policy-prescription half of the [[1893]] tract, while arguing that the obligation does not require the Dutch to establish a *vestiging* there. The *Nisero-quaestie* is, in Van Heutsz's narrative, the type-event of the diplomatic incident the Atjehnese had learned to provoke, and the empirical warrant for the [[1893]] prescription's insistence on a marine-enforced closure that would prevent such incidents from recurring.
