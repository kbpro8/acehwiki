**Conversion of Minangkabau via Atjeh (c. 1550)** is the date given in *[[Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk]]* ([[1946]]) for the rapid, wholesale conversion of the [[Minangkabau]] of central Sumatra to Islam, effected — in the account of both Prof. Dr. [[W. J. A. Kernkamp|Kernkamp]] and Dr. [[F. H. van Naerssen|Van Naerssen]] — under the direct influence of the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] sultanate. Kernkamp's wording in the chapter *De invloed van den Islam*: "the Minangkabauers, who around 1500 were still heathens, were converted from Atjeh, after the realm of that name had arisen there. About 1550 they took up Islam in rapid tempo and in their entirety, and have remained fervent Muslims since." Van Naerssen, in the chapter *De oudste cultuurstroomen*, gives the same date.

## Atjeh as the proximate source of Minangkabau Islam

The role assigned to Atjeh in the Minangkabau conversion is, in both Kernkamp's and Van Naerssen's accounts, the pivotal event of mid-sixteenth-century Sumatran religious history. Before the rise of Atjeh, the Minangkabau had been a Hindu-Javanese-influenced polity, "the descendants of an once-powerful Hindu-Sumatra realm"; after the rise of Atjeh, they became — within a few years around 1550 — "fervent Muslims" who would, in turn, become the principal agents of Islamisation for the southern Bataks. Kernkamp notes that "the southern Bataks, who in turn were converted from Minangkabau, long continued to resist. The action was accompanied by violence and plunder and this 'holy' war therefore failed." The Minangkabau–Batak proselytising frontier thus became, in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the southern continuation of the Atjeh-derived Islamisation of northern Sumatra.

The Minangkabau conversion also marks a turning-point in the wider Indonesian Islamic geography. Van Naerssen records that the [[1415]] Chinese reports confirm Islam "already spread over the whole north coast of Sumatra and also established on the east coast as far as the little realm of [[Aru]] with its own Sultan"; by 1550, with the conversion of the Minangkabau interior, the Islamisation of the Sumatran west coast was likewise secured. The [[Padri War on Sumatra's East Coast|Padri movement]] of the early nineteenth century, the [[1838]] Dutch expedition against the Padries, and the late-nineteenth-century Minangkabau role in the Islamic reformism of the [[Kaum Muda]] are all, in this long perspective, the afterlife of the mid-sixteenth-century Atjeh-derived conversion.

## Significance for Atjeh

For the [[Acheh|Atjeh]]-focused reader, the conversion of Minangkabau via Atjeh matters for two reasons. First, it establishes the Atjeh sultanate's religious-cultural reach beyond its own immediate territorial boundaries: by 1550, Atjeh was not only the dominant Muslim polity of the north Sumatran coast but the source from which the most populous Muslim interior people of Sumatra had received its Islam. Second, it fixes a specific date — c. 1550 — at which the Atjeh sultanate's wider religious influence is documented, some three centuries before the [[Aceh War|Dutch-Aceh war]] of [[1873]] and four centuries before the [[1946]] publication of *Wat Indië Ontving en Schonk*.
