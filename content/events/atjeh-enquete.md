The **Atjéh-enquête** was the formal investigation conducted by Governor-General [[J. B. van Heutsz|J. B. van Heutsz]] in [[November]]–[[December]] [[1907]] into the civil and military administration of the governor of [[Acheh|Atjèh]], [[G. C. E. van Daalen|G. C. E. van Daalen]]. Prompted by mounting press allegations — the [[Wekker articles|"Wekker" articles]] in *[[De Avondpost]]* — parliamentary criticism, and private complaints about Van Daalen's harsh rule, the inquiry led to Van Daalen's removal and a major political crisis for Van Heutsz's governorship. Its findings confirmed what advisers including [[Snouck Hurgronje]] and [[H. Colijn]] had warned in [[1904]]: that Van Daalen had "misunderstood the fundamental principle" of pacification, and that his methods had alienated the very chiefs on whose cooperation Dutch authority depended.

## Background

By [[1907]], Van Daalen had been governor of Aceh and its dependencies for over two years. Though he had completed the military conquest of the [[Gajoland|Gajo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas]] highlands in [[1904]]–[[1905]], his civil administration was increasingly criticised. Complaints focused on his arbitrary fines, imprisonment of chiefs, internment of women and children at [[Sabang]], and general distrust of the indigenous leadership. In [[October]] [[1907]], the [[Wekker articles|"Wekker" articles]] appeared in *De Avondpost*, accusing Van Daalen of cruelty and misconduct, and galvanising parliamentary opposition with members such as [[Van Kol]] calling for an inquiry. Van Heutsz had initially defended Van Daalen, but under pressure from Minister Fock — and after receiving credible reports of abuses — he agreed to visit Aceh in person.

## The investigation

Van Heutsz arrived in [[Koeta Radja|Koetaradja]] on 21 November [[1907]], accompanied by **[[Rost van Tonningen]]**, the Army Commander (tasked with examining military conduct), and **[[D. F. W. van Rees]]**, the Secretary-General of the Indies (assisting with administrative review). Van Heutsz personally investigated the political and civil aspects. The inquiry lasted several weeks and covered the main administrative centres of [[Great Acheh|Groot Atjèh]], [[Pidie|Pidië]], [[Lho' Seumawe|Lho' Seumawé]], and the west coast.

## Findings

Van Heutsz's final report documented numerous serious deviations from the pacification policy he had designed:

- **Arbitrary fines**: Van Daalen had imposed heavy penalties on chiefs, often without proper legal basis, depositing them into the road-works fund — a practice Van Heutsz had explicitly forbidden.
- **Unjustified internment**: Chiefs and their families were sent to [[Sabang]] and kept there indefinitely without judicial review, not based on the legal provisions of Article 47 of the *Regeeringsreglement*.
- **Neglect of local leadership**: In Pidië, several *oelèëbalangs* had been dismissed and not replaced, so that the civil administrator effectively took over their functions — "an absolute impossibility."
- **Inhumane treatment**: In Lho' Seumawé, the civil administrator was so incompetent that chiefs openly refused to cooperate and the interior had not seen a military patrol for months; in Tapak Tuan, women and children of a fugitive chief were imprisoned without any legal grounds.

Van Heutsz concluded that Van Daalen had "misunderstood the fundamental principle" on which the pacification rested — namely, that the chiefs should be supported, guided, and trusted, not punished and humiliated. His investigation revealed conditions he described as "meer dan ergerlijk" (more than reprehensible).

## The critical letter

On 24 December [[1907]], Van Heutsz sent a personal letter to Van Daalen, formally expressing his displeasure:

> "Het kán toch niet juist zijn de landshoofden en oelabalangs door een meer bestraffend dan leidend en vormend optreden in de noodzakelijkheid te brengen hun gedrag jegens ons alleen af te meten naar de mate van vrees en ontzag, welke wij hun door voortdurende 'gevoelige correctie' met straffe hand weten op te dringen."

That is, it could not be right to force chiefs to measure their conduct solely by fear of punishment, rather than by guidance and trust. He ordered the reversal of several specific measures.

## Aftermath

Van Daalen requested relief on 28 December [[1907]]; his resignation was accepted and he was formally relieved in [[May]] [[1908]]. The investigation's report was published in a redacted form in 1908, omitting some of the most damaging details to avoid fuelling press attacks, but had a sobering effect on public opinion and helped restore confidence in Van Heutsz's overall policy. [[H. N. A. Swart]] succeeded Van Daalen, immediately reversing the punitive policies and restoring the conciliatory approach that brought Aceh the period of relative stability described in *De Buitenbezittingen*.

## Significance

The Atjéh-enquête was a turning point in the [[Aceh War]]'s pacification phase. It demonstrated that military success alone could not secure lasting peace, and that a governor who alienated the indigenous elite would fail to consolidate Dutch authority. The inquiry also forced Van Heutsz to publicly acknowledge his misjudgment of Van Daalen's character — an act that, though personally painful, enhanced his reputation for integrity. The episode has since been cited as a classic case study in the tension between military temperament and civil governance in colonial administration.
