**The Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)** was the Dutch invasion force sent against [[Aceh|Atjeh]] after the failure of the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)]]. Under [[Jan van Swieten]], it landed on the east side of the [[Aceh River|Aceh River]] in [[December]] [[1873]], fought its way through the Acehnese outer defenses, and occupied the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] on [[24 January]] [[1874]]. Within a week, Van Swieten issued the [[Van Swieten's Proclamation of 31 January 1874]], declaring [[Groot-Atjeh]] annexed by right of conquest.

The expedition was a major Dutch military success, but it did not end the [[Aceh War]]. From the first months after the occupation of the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]], critics pointed out that the country had not submitted and that Acehnese resistance remained active.

## Preparation after the first defeat

The second expedition was prepared after the failure of the first Dutch attack in [[April]] [[1873]] and the death of [[J. H. R. Köhler|General Köhler]]. The Dutch gathered a much stronger force, improved the armament, and assembled better artillery, transport, medical support, bridging material, and supplies. Contemporary Dutch accounts gave the strength as 6,354 infantry besides officers, 75 cavalry, 656 artillerymen with 74 guns, 306 engineers, and about 250 free native auxiliaries. [[Jan van Swieten]] was recalled from retirement and placed in supreme command, with [[General Verspijck|General Verspijck]] as second-in-command.

The expedition also combined civil and military authority in one person, because Van Swieten acted both as military commander and as government commissioner.

## Landing east of the Aceh River

The landing plan differed from the first expedition. The Dutch decided to land east of the [[Aceh River|Aceh River]], both because resistance there was expected to be lighter and because they wanted to spare [[Marassa]], whose chief [[Toekoe Nek Radja Moeda Setia|Toekoe Nek]] had shown goodwill toward them. The landing at [[Kwala Loë]] on [[9 December]] [[1873]] met only light resistance.

From the beachhead the Dutch moved inland. [[Kota Moesapi]] was occupied on [[13 December]] [[1873]]. The next day the Acehnese line at [[Battle of Tiban (14 December 1873)|Tiban]] was stormed. On [[15 December]] [[1873]] the mouth of the river was reached, and on [[18 December]] [[1873]] a column under [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Pel]] reached [[Penajoeng]].

## The slow advance inland

From [[Penajoeng]] the expedition advanced toward the inner defenses of the Acehnese capital. During this phase Van Swieten still tried negotiation. He sent two letters to the sultan, one offering peace on Dutch terms and one proposing to conduct the war in the manner of civilized nations. The messenger, [[Mas Soemo Widikdjo]], a guide and interpreter who was described in Dutch sources as the son of a British-Indian officer, left on [[26 December]] [[1873]] and was later killed at [[Longbatta]].

Meanwhile the land campaign continued. The Dutch moved against [[Battle of Lemboe (25–26 December 1873)|Lemboe]], where the fighting was severe, and later accounts remembered the bravery of men such as adjutant-under-officer [[E. C. O. von Bredow|Von Bredow]], who planted the Dutch flag at the parapet, and Captain Van Daalen, who brought reinforcements forward. On [[6 January]] [[1874]] the Dutch captured the [[Third Conquest of the Great Missigit|Great Missigit]]. Advice from [[Toekoe Nek Radja Moeda Setia|Toekoe Nek]] then helped shape the next move. After a secret meeting with controller [[Kroesen]] on [[11 January]] [[1874]], Toekoe Nek advised the Dutch to seize [[Taman (Kota Goenoengan)|the Taman]], a fortified pleasure-garden southwest of the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]], and on [[12 January]] [[1874]] the Dutch stormed it. This made the royal position much harder to hold.

## Capture of the Kraton

On [[24 January]] [[1874]], Dutch troops entered the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] and found it abandoned. The Dutch flag was raised, and the fall of the royal center became the great symbolic success of the expedition. Two days later, on [[26 January]] [[1874]], the sultan died of cholera at [[Pager Ajer|Pagger Ajer]].

On [[31 January]] [[1874]], Van Swieten declared [[Groot-Atjeh]] annexed and invited chiefs to submit in writing. The Dutch now presented themselves as the new sovereign power.

## Support from the navy

The naval side of the campaign also mattered. When the ruler of [[Pedir]] was reported to have gone to help defend the capital, a Dutch squadron bombarded Pedir between [[29 December]] [[1873]] and [[31 December]] [[1873]]. Dutch accounts named the ships as the *Zeeland*, *Metalen Kruis*, *Citadel van Antwerpen*, *Borneo*, and *Banda*. The action was meant to punish support for the resistance and show that the Dutch could strike along the coast as well as inland.

## Why the expedition did not end the war

Although the expedition took the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]], it did not end the war. This was clear almost at once. The Dutch still had to fight actions after the fall of the capital, including the expeditions to [[Expedition to Bital (12 February 1874)]] and [[Expedition to Ketapang Doewa (15 February 1874)]], and the reverse at [[Battle of Lampoe Oek (16 April 1874)]].

This reality shaped the early printed debate about the campaign. [[General Knoop]] judged that the expedition had been only "slechts ten halve gelukt". In [[Open Letter by Brutus to General Knoop]], [[Brutus]] argued in detail that the Dutch had moved too slowly after each success and had relied too much on the hope that occupation of the capital would bring submission by itself. In [[General Van Swieten's letter in Het Vaderland]], Van Swieten answered that the expedition had fulfilled its program by taking the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] and securing a durable base.

## Significance

**The Second Aceh Expedition** changed the course of the war. It succeeded where the first expedition had failed, and it gave the Dutch possession of the old royal center. But it also created the central paradox of the early [[Aceh War]]: the Dutch could hold the capital and still fail to subdue the country. That is why the expedition remained both a military success and a political beginning rather than an end.
