**The Fire at Penajoeng** was a mysterious conflagration that broke out on [[19 December]] [[1873]] in the house of [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] (also known as [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil]]) in the kampong of [[Penajoeng]], shortly after the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|Dutch expeditionary force]] under [[Jan van Swieten|Lieutenant-General Van Swieten]] had occupied the position. The fire destroyed the house — described as a substantial plank-built dwelling — together with the Acehnese state archive stored within it, and drove [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]], one of the most promising Acehnese intermediaries for peace, into flight and permanent estrangement from the Dutch cause. [[Van Swieten]] regarded the event as one of the most consequential misfortunes of the campaign.

## Toengkoe Kali's Position

[[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] was the Sultan [[Sultan Aladin Mahmoed Shah|Aladin Mahmud Shah]]'s second father-in-law — having married the Sultan to his daughter in early [[1873]] — and the acting *bandhara* (treasurer and chief minister) of the Acehnese court. He was, by all accounts, favourably disposed toward the Dutch. During the negotiations of [[March 1873]], he had been among those who understood the advantages that a treaty with the Netherlands-India Government would bring to the Sultan and had been willing to advise acceptance. He had been restrained, however, by the opposition of the *sagie* chiefs, particularly [[Panglima Polim]], who saw clearly that Dutch sovereignty would extinguish their own quasi-independent authority.

During the landing and advance of the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|Second Expedition]], [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] had advised his associates to accept the Dutch presence and had encouraged peaceful contact. [[Toekoe Nek Radja Moeda Setia|Toekoe Nek]], the panglima of the [[XXV Moekims]] at [[Maraksa]], had similarly counselled [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] to approach the commanding general, promising a cordial reception.

## The Fire

On [[19 December]] [[1873]], as the Dutch column under [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] advanced through [[Penajoeng]] and established occupation of the position, the substantial plank house of [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] was set ablaze. The circumstances remain uncertain: it occurred after the column had already passed through, and no one was ever able to discover who set the fire or how it was ignited. [[Kolonel Wiggers van Kerchem|Colonel Wiggers van Kerchem]], who was the first to take possession of [[Penajoeng]], later recounted to Van Swieten that it had appeared to be a fine, well-built house.

Within the house, the Acehnese state archive — a collection of documents of great historical value — was stored. The archive went up in flames and was irretrievably lost. [[Van Swieten]] described this as the destruction of "a treasure of great historical worth."

## Consequences

The destruction of his home and the loss of his archives had an immediate and decisive effect on [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]]. When, during the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|Second Expedition]], the Dutch forces arrived at [[Penajoeng]], [[Toengkoe Kali]] had intended to come forward and join the Dutch side. But the burning of his house — whether by accident, by design of Acehnese patriots who suspected his sympathies, or by some other cause — destroyed his confidence. He fled into the interior and took no further part in the negotiations.

Later, when [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]] had submitted and was in [[Batavia]], he stayed for half a month at the home of [[Raad van Indië|Councillor of the Indies]] [[Levysohn Norman]], who gathered many interesting particulars from him. [[Levysohn Norman|Levysohn Norman]] asked why [[Toengkoe Kadli Malikoel Adil|Toengkoe Kali]], who was inclined to align with Dutch policy, had not joined the commanding general during the second expedition. His answer was: "Toekoe Nek also advised me to do so, and promised a favourable reception. I was also minded to do it; but when on [[19 December]], at the arrival of the troops at Penajoeng, my house was set on fire, my confidence was destroyed and I took flight."

## Van Swieten's Assessment

[[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]] regarded this event as a calamity of the first order. "We lost with Toengkoe Kali a means of contact with many chiefs who, like him, did not want the war and were inclined to accept our political arrangements," he wrote. He further observed that the house-burnings — a recurring practice during the campaign, whether ordered or merely tolerated — produced not only moral injury but struck at the very foundation of utility. By destroying the homes of those who might have been willing to cooperate, the Dutch alienated precisely the people whose cooperation was most needed.

"This is again proof," [[Van Swieten]] wrote, "of what incalculable evil those disastrous burnings cause, and how not only morality but also the principle of utility is thereby violated."

The fire at [[Penajoeng]] thus stands as one of those seemingly minor incidents whose consequences rippled far beyond their immediate cause. The loss of a single friendly chief, the destruction of a single archive, and the alienation of a single network of moderate Acehnese leaders may have prolonged the war by years.
