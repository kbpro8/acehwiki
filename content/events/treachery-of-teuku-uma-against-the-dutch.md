The **treachery of Teuku Uma against the Dutch**—known in Achehnese-Dutch annals simply as the "Uma episode"—was the open defection of the celebrated Achehnese chief [[Teuku Uma|Teuku Uma (Umar)]] from his nominal alliance with the Netherlands India Government in [[1896]]. After three years during which the Dutch, under the misjudged "concentration" policy, had armed and subsidized Uma as the linchpin of their influence over the West Coast and one of the three *sagis* of [[Great Acheh]], Uma judged that he had now sufficient money and weapons to play a leading part among the insurgents. In March [[1896]] he removed the scales from the eyes of the blindly-trusting [[General Deykerhoff|General Deykerhoff]], and his secret opposition to Dutch rule thus changed to open hostility.

## The "Uma period" preceding the defection

The three years before the defection had earned an unhappy notoriety under the name of the "Uma period". Since the [[Submission of Teuku Uma|submission of 1893]], the Dutch Governor—absolutely unacquainted with native affairs—had given his entire confidence to the adventurer, and in defiance of all warnings had persisted in strengthening him by generous subsidies of Dutch weapons and Dutch money. Thus assisted, Uma had overcome his own enemies in partisan struggles, and civil war had raged even after the Dutch power was established in Acheh. Under the guise of fighting against the foes of the Dutch regime, he had dealt out, as his own, plentiful subsidies to his friends in the War-party, and had tried by all means in his power to acquire the maximum of influence both over the Dutch and their opponents.

## The turning point

From the very first, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, Uma had set himself to winning substantial gains for the War-party and only nominal advantages for the Dutch—which last advantages, however seriously taken, had no real value whatever. The only apparent gain which Uma, appointed a commander of friendly Achehnese forces and considered by the Governor as a trusty councillor, brought to his protector, was an extension of the Dutch "line" of greater extent than the original "concentrated" line; yet this could effect nothing whatever in the way of terminating an insurrection that had its centres and its granaries outside Great Acheh, in the dependencies. The extension of the line was due to, and continued to depend on, the slender thread of Uma's good-will. Of course this could not go on for long. As soon as it ceased to be possible for Uma to combine the satisfaction of the wishes of the War-party with the appearance of advancing the interests of the Government, the barrel would burst; and there was not much doubt on which side Uma would elect to be.

## The consequences in Great Acheh

In [[1896]] the Nanta family—Uma's in-laws, since he had married Chut Din, the daughter of [[Teuku Nanta Seutia|Teuku Nanta]]—having taken a prominent part in the treachery of Teuku Uma against the Dutch, the Ne' family was restored to power. When the Nantas were expelled, the Dutch Government appointed as chief **Teuku Raja Itam**, a son of the Teuku Ne' who had been uleëbalang of [[Meura'sa|Meura'sa]] at the beginning of the Dutch operations in Acheh. Since the "defection" of Teuku Uma, the Nantas were declared deposed from the government of the [[VI Mukims|VI Mukims]], and the native authority in that place was exercised by the young Teuku Raja Itam, son of the late Teuku Ne' of Meura'sa. The defection thus had direct political consequences within Great Acheh itself, redistributing the balance of power among the great uleëbalang families of the sagi.

## Significance for the Aceh War

The Uma episode, however melancholy in itself, indirectly exercised a healthy influence upon the management of Achehnese affairs. Its termination opened all eyes to the need of vigorous action if one did not wish to abandon altogether the subjugation of Acheh. In [[1896]], the war against the Achehnese—which had been allowed to be suspended since [[1881]] without any real resultant gain—was resumed. Under the vigorous governorship of [[Governor Van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]], less than six years sufficed to bring the Dutch to what even the most sceptical had to admit was the beginning of the end. The traitor Uma, with numbers of adventurers, fanatical leaders and guerilla chiefs, perished; the chiefs of dependent states, the members of the Sultan's family, and finally their head himself, submitted almost without exception; and thousands of refugees came back to their gampongs.
