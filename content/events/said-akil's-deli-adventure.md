The **Said Akil's Deli adventure of [[1824]]–[[1828]]** — referred to by Van der Kemp as the *Deli affair* — was the four-year attempt by [[Said Akil]] (Syed Akil), third son of the [[Penang]] trader [[Said Hussein]] and younger brother of the Achehnese pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]], to make himself master of the East-Coast Sumatran state of [[Deli]]. Married to a daughter of the [[Sultan of Deli]] c. [[1824]] and adopted by the Sultan as a son, Said Akil returned after his father-in-law's death, raised a force from Penang, and installed himself as *Raja Mooda* (deputy ruler) over the young successor. He then plundered the country and its traders until a chorus of complaints from the [[Shahbandar of Deli]], the [[Sultan Alaeddin Muhammad Shah of Acheh|Sultan of Acheh]] and the traders of [[Batu Bara]] forced the British government of [[Penang]] to read him out of its protection in [[1828]]. The episode is preserved in the Binkhorst collection of correspondence printed by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]].

## Marriage and adoption (1824)

About [[1824]] Said Akil became engaged, or was married, to a daughter of the [[Sultan of Deli]]. The Sultan himself announced the adoption in his [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|letter to Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of 27 August 1824]] (Van der Kemp's Appendix 6), begging at the same time for British help against the in-pressing [[Padri War|Padries]] (Rinchis) and reminding the British of the friendship that had existed with the late Governor [[W. E. Phillips|Phillips]]. The marriage thus brought Akil into the Deli royal house as son-in-law and adoptive son, opening the way to his later usurpation.

## Raising of a force and the "Raja Mooda" (1826)

When his father-in-law died, he was succeeded by "an insignificant boy of 14 to 16 years". Said Akil, "as brother-in-law", considered that he might profit from the situation, and in [[1826]] he fitted out "a fairly considerable force" at [[Penang]] to go to [[Deli]] and play the master. The Penang government, through its Malay translator [[John Anderson]], warned him by letter of [[11 April]] [[1826]] that he must leave Deli alone; but when Akil replied that his expedition had no other aim than to fetch his Deli wife "in a glorious manner", the government let him depart. The young Sultan, the correspondence drily notes, "seemed to take this rather well", sending back a polite acknowledgement dated 25 June 1826.

## Disorder and complaints (October 1826 – early 1828)

Once established at [[Deli]], Akil made himself master of the government. The [[Sultan Alaeddin Muhammad Shah of Acheh|Sultan of Acheh]] complained in a brusque letter of [[13 October]] [[1826]] to the Governor of [[Penang]] that this was a violation of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] (article 1 was probably meant), since Deli was a dependency of [[Acheh]] and was now being endangered by a subject of the British government. [[John Anderson|Anderson]] again wrote, on [[2 November]] [[1826]], asking Akil to return to Penang "with as little delay as possible", but this was talking to deaf ears.

By early [[1828]] the [[Shahbandar of Deli]] was writing to the Governor of Penang begging him to "deliver the land from the robber Said Akil", describing how all the traders' property had been plundered by Akil, how traders at [[Batu Bara]] had been ruined, and how the chiefs of Batu Bara were about to seek satisfaction by force. Anderson wrote again on [[7 February]] [[1828]] requiring Akil to abstain from all interference and to return to his former residence at Penang as a "peaceable British subject." The young Sultan (presumably writing under Akil's dictation) replied that it was all lies and slander; Akil himself wrote on 7 August 1828 that he was doing nobody any harm and was only waiting to collect some 60,000 dollars owed to him before returning to Penang.

## Removal by British pressure (1828)

Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] finally wrote to the young Sultan on [[27 August]] [[1828]] that the numerous complaints against Akil had given rise to the admonitions, and that the British government, "wishing nothing else than to see quiet trade", could not countenance the doings of one of its own subjects in another country. Akil was informed that, having proceeded to Deli contrary to the injunctions of the government of Prince of Wales Island, he was placed without the protection of the British Government in the event of his infringing the laws or injuring the inhabitants of that country. The Bengal government, on being informed, approved the Penang government's conduct by dispatch of [[24 October]] [[1828]].

With this final intervention Akil's Deli adventure came to an end. Van der Kemp warns the reader not to confuse this comparatively insignificant personage with the contemporaneous crown pretender [[Raja Akil of Sukadana|Raja Akil of Siak/Sukadana]] on [[Borneo]], whose career was described by [[Pieter Johannes Veth|Professor Veth]]. The episode is illustrative of the looseness of British control over its Malay subjects and of the practical impotence of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh treaty of 1819]] in securing order on Sumatra's East Coast — a region now falling within the Dutch sphere under the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]] but effectively unpoliced by either power.
