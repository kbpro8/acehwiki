The **Jinamee** (also spelled *jinamec* or *jinamë*) was the ceremonial "wedding gift" presented by a newly installed [[Sultan of Acheh]] to each of the three [[panglima sagi]] on the occasion of his accession to the throne. By later tradition the sum had risen to $500 per chief, and its payment symbolised the metaphorical marriage-contract by which the sultan was joined to the federated dominions of [[Acheh]] proper. The transaction thus enacted, in ritual form, the principle that the three sagis (the [[XXII Mukims]], [[XXVI Mukims]] and [[XXV Mukims]]) were the collective guardians of the sultanate and that the new sovereign took office as their "bridegroom".

The metaphor of marriage between sultan and realm was no mere poetic flourish. As Snouck Hurgronje observes, the three guardians—the panglima sagi—did not long succeed in retaining the supremacy over the federate dominions which they had possessed when this metaphorical marriage-contract was first devised, yet the outward form of the institution survived. The bridegroom was required to pay to each of the chiefs a sum of five hundred dollars as a wedding gift (*jinamec*), and the payment constituted one of the visible signs that the new ruler acknowledged the protecting authority of the sagi confederations.

In later times the jinamee became intertwined with the reciprocal payment that a newly constituted panglima sagi was expected to make to the sultan for the [[letters patent with the ninefold seal]] confirming his appointment. Against the $500 received as jinamee, it became the established rule for a new panglima sagi to pay at least an equal sum to the sultan on receipt of his patent. The flow of ceremonial gift and counter-gift thus formalised the personal bond between sovereign and the three "guardians" of the realm, even as real power slipped away from the Dalam towards the sagi chiefs themselves.

The jinamee was thus one of the principal revenues accruing to the panglima sagi from their office, alongside the fines, fees and shares of inheritance that flowed through their hands. It belonged to the same order of ceremonial payments as the [[langgeh umong]] and the [[adat meulangga]]—institutions through which the relations of chiefs to one another and to their dependants were periodically reaffirmed. Together with the [[coronation of the sultan]] and the various [[installation of a new sultan]] rites, the jinamee marked out the sultanate as a federation in form as well as in fact, however nominal the sultan's authority might have become in the closing decades of the nineteenth century.
