The **Dutch military measures against Atjehse piracy** of [[1872]] were the decision of the Dutch colonial government, after repeated urging by the British government in London, to abandon the policy of *niet-inmenging* (non-intervention) and to take military measures against the [[Achehnese|Atjehse]] piracy and slave trade that had been the subject of the [[American bombardment of the Atjehse coast (1831)|American bombardment of 1831]] and the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]]. The decision, made possible by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-contract of 1871]], which gave the Netherlands a free hand over the whole of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]] in exchange for an undertaking to suppress the slave trade and piracy, led directly to the [[First Aceh Expedition (1873)|first Aceh expedition]] of [[April 1873|April]] [[1873]].

## Context

The [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-contract of 1871]] (the *Sumatra-contract*) gave the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] a free hand for the whole of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], but at the same time laid on it the obligation to make a definitive end to the Atjehse slave trade and piracy. The [[Achehnese|Atjehers]] carried on a busy slave trade and were notorious pirates; numerous ships had fallen victim to them. After fruitless attempts to settle the matter by diplomatic means alone, the government "ging in 1873 over tot militaire maatregelen..... tot 'beperkte' militaire maatregelen" — went over in 1873 to military measures, to *limited* military measures. "Zekere militaire doelen moesten worden bereikt...... dan zouden de Atjehers wel tot rede gebracht zijn......"

## The Decision of 1872

Frijling dates the turn to military measures to [[1872]]: "Met die gedachte richtte het zich ook in 1872, na herhaald aandringen van Londen, tegen de Atjehse zeeroverij en slavenhandel." The British government had repeatedly pressed the Dutch to act; the Sumatra-contract of the previous year had given the Dutch both the right and the obligation to do so. The decision was taken in the spirit of the existing policy of *niet-inmenging*: "beperkte" — limited — military objectives were to be set, and it was assumed that, once these were achieved, the Atjehers "wel tot rede gebracht" — would be brought to reason.

## Sequel

The sequel is recorded by Frijling with characteristic irony. "Na een aanvankelijk échec van onze troepen onder Generaal Köhler gelukte het aan Generaal van Swieten de Kraton van de Sultan van Atjeh te nemen. De Kraton heet sindsdien Kotta Radja." The government, believing that the military events had made a deep impression on the Atjehers, wished to adhere to its policy of non-intervention and recalled the troops to Batavia. The result was that a year later troops had to be sent again: "De offers aan mensenlevens waren vergeefs gebracht. Slavenhandel en zeeroverij werden op de oude voet voortgezet." The political failure of 1872 — the assumption that "limited" military measures would suffice — inaugurated the protracted "schier eindeloos gesukkel" of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], which only [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] would bring to an end around the turn of the century.

## Significance

The [[1872]] decision is the formal beginning of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. Its premise — that "limited" military measures would suffice to bring the Atjehers to reason — would be repeatedly falsified over the next thirty years and would be the standing reproach to the Dutch political authorities that Frijling formulates in his sketch. The decision is also the moment at which the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] took upon itself, at British insistence, the active suppression of Atjehese piracy and slavery — the obligation it had assumed in [[1814]] and had failed to discharge for fifty-eight years.
