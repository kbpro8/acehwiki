**The concentration decision of August 1884** was the pivotal moment when the Governor-General of Netherlands Indies ordered the withdrawal of Dutch military forces from scattered outposts throughout [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot Atjeh]] to a consolidated fortified line surrounding [[Kota-Radja|Kota Radja]] and [[Olehleh]]. This strategic retreat marked a fundamental shift in Dutch colonial policy after years of costly offensive operations against Acehnese resistance fighters.

## Background

By August 1884, the Dutch colonial administration in [[Atjeh]] faced an untenable military situation. The versterkingen (fortifications) established during the conquest expeditions of [[Generaal Pel|Generaal Pel]] in the [[IV Moekims]] and the campaigns of [[General van der Heyden]] in 1878–1879 in the [[XXII Moekims]] and [[XXVI Moekims]] had expanded far beyond defensible limits. The post at [[Pakan-Kroeng-Tjoet]], [[Lamjong]], [[Oleh-Karang]], [[Lampermej]], [[Lambaroe]], [[Tjot-Goe]], [[Ketapan-Doea]], and [[Pakan-Badak]] formed a discontinuous line that did not enclose any meaningful territory.

The communicationslijn (communication line) through the [[XXII Moekims]] to [[Djerir]] stretched 121 palen (approximately 24 kilometers) without protection on either flank. As the Acehnese population increasingly joined the oorlogspartij (war party) advocating *wang sabil* (holy war funds), these exposed supply routes became death traps for Dutch convoys. Every transport along the line from [[Anagaloeng]] to [[Glé-Kabiig]] in early 1884 required fighting.

The rapid succession of administrative stelsels (systems) had compounded the problem. First, the civiel bestuur (civil administration) was introduced with military influence deliberately marginalized. Then, the prematurely proclaimed peace collapsed as hostilities escalated. By 1881–1884, the Dutch found themselves holding indefensible positions while the enemy held all strategic advantages.

## The Decision

On [[19 August]] [[1884]], the Governor-General issued several decisions that transformed Aceh's governance:

1. The civil bestuur that had persisted for over three years despite repeated criticism was abolished
2. Civil and military authority was reunited under a single [[Militair Gouverneur]] (Military Governor)
3. The kontroleurs (controllers) in the sub-districts of Groot-Atjeh were abolished
4. The Assistant-Resident on the West Coast (stationed at [[Melaboeh]]) and the controller at [[Segli]] were replaced by military commanders
5. The Assistant-Resident on the North and East Coast (stationed at [[Telok-Semawé]]) and the controller of [[Samalanga]] were abolished along with their military garrisons
6. A new geconcentreerde stelling (concentrated position) in Groot-Atjeh was defined
7. Posts outside the new line would be gradually evacuated
8. Several field battalions would return to Java

The deadline for completing the concentration was [[1 March]] [[1885]].

## Three Strategic Options

The military planners considered three stelsels (options) for the new line:

**First Option:** Extend the eastern boundary to [[Toengkoeb]]–[[Anagaloeng]], retaining the extensive installations at both locations. This was rejected as it would require heavy casualties to fortify through hostile [[XXVI Moekims]], demand enormous resources, and maintain dangerously long communication lines.

**Second Option (Chosen):** Follow approximately the existing line of [[Pakan-Kroeng-Tjoet]], [[Lamjong]], [[Roempit]], [[Lampermej]], [[Lambaroe]], [[Ketapan-Doea]], extending toward [[Salang]] to the sea. This preserved several existing posts and maintained a moderate perimeter.

**Third Option:** Draw the line as tightly as possible around [[Kota-Radja]] and [[Olehleh]], abandoning more territory but reducing costs and garrison requirements. This was rejected because further retreat would bolster enemy morale and signal weakness.

## Significance

The concentration decision represented a strategic retreat from the offensive posture that had characterized Dutch operations since [[1873]]. By consolidating around the main population centers, the Dutch aimed to achieve what they could not through expansion: genuine control over a smaller, defensible territory. Whether this choice was optimal remained debated—the future might yet force reconquest of abandoned areas at fresh cost. However, the decision at least represented a coherent stelsel (system) after years of stelselloosheid (policy without system).
