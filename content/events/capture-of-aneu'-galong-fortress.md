The **capture of the Aneu' Galong fortress** by the Dutch troops in [[1896]] was the engagement in which the guerrilla leader **Mat Amin**, the son and successor of [[Teungku Tiro]], was killed in action. The event is recorded by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in a bracketed footnote to his account of [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]]: "[Mat Amin was killed in action in 1896, when the Dutch troops captured the fortress of Aneu' Galong. Two other sons of Teungku Tiro, [[Teungku Beb|Teungku Beb]] (died 1900) and [[Teungku Mahidin|Teungku Mahidin or Ma' Et]], together with their kinsman [[Teungku Chot Plieng|Teungku Chot Plieng]] (died 1901) much respected for his piety and learning, continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the *sabil* contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]. [[Great Acheh]] no longer furnishes a field for the labour of these men and their fellows.]"

## Background: Mat Amin's inheritance

Teungku Tiro — the most prominent *ulama* of the Achehnese war, who had been recognised as commander-in-chief of the [[Achehnese War|holy war against the Dutch]] — had died in January [[1891]] after a sudden illness, expressing the conviction that poison had been administered to him by some agent of his son Mat Amin. Notwithstanding this, Teungku Tiro had Mat Amin summoned to his bedside when dying, and solemnly warned him to forsake his evil ways and to seek the counsel of devout *ulamas*. "He seemed however to realize how little chance there was of his son's ever proving his true successor in any respect." Mat Amin "was not wont to hearken to advice. In his dress he assumed the character of an *uleebalang*, and while his father, in receiving distinguished guests, always rose to meet them, the young man would remain sitting until they came up to him. Even the Achehnese consider Mat Amin proud, ignorant and headstrong, and when after his father's death he gave the rein more than ever to his evil passions, the great body of the adherents of Teungku Tiro at once melted away."

## The fortress at Aneu' Galong

The fortress of Aneu' Galong was one of the strongholds to which the remnant of Mat Amin's following had retired after his father's death. As a guerrilla leader and *teungku* by title, Mat Amin carried on the collections of the *haqq sabil* and the leadership of such bands as still adhered to the name of Teungku Tiro, but without his father's combination of religious authority and military command. The bands under his leadership were, in Hurgronje's account, "a great burden to the people", and his life was "in other respects also far from harmonizing with the teaching of his father". The story goes that during the last years of the latter's life Mat Amin lived openly with another man's wife, and that Teungku Tiro, to give him a severe lesson, forbade him access to his presence; and this appears to have so enraged the young man, that he brooded over some device to rid him of this tiresome father.

## The capture and the death

The Dutch capture of Aneu' Galong in 1896 thus brought to a close, in military terms, the line of Teungku Tiro in Great Acheh. With Mat Amin's death in action, the remaining sons of Teungku Tiro — Teungku Beb (died 1900) and Teungku Mahidin or Ma' Et — together with their kinsman Teungku Chot Plieng (died 1901), continued to do their utmost to maintain the collections of the *sabil* contributions in the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]. But Great Acheh no longer furnished a field for their labour. The capture of Aneu' Galong is therefore, in the military history of the Achehnese War, one of the engagements by which the Dutch offensive, resumed in [[1896]] after the [[Defection of Teuku Uma|defection of Teuku Uma]], began to break up the organised centres of the Achehnese war-party in Great Acheh itself.

## Significance

The fall of Aneu' Galong, in the wider perspective of the war, marks the close of the succession of Teungku Tiro in Great Acheh, and the consequent shift of the remaining religious leadership of the war to the dependencies on the [[North Coast (of Acheh)|North Coast]]. It belongs to the same series of Dutch successes of 1896 — the year in which the offensive was resumed after fifteen years of suspended action — that opened the way for the methodical close of the war under [[Van Heutsz's governorship|Van Heutsz's governorship]] from [[1898]]. The capture thus stands, in Hurgronje's account, at one of the turning-points of the Achehnese War.
