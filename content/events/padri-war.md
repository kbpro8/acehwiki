The **Padri War** (c. [[1821]]–[[1837]]) was a religious-military conflict that unfolded on the island of [[Sumatra]] in the early nineteenth century, pitting the reformist *Padri* movement — Islamic reformers strongly influenced by *Wahhabi* ideas then spreading through the archipelago after the Mecca pilgrimage — against both the traditional Minangkabau authorities and the [[Netherlands Indies|Dutch colonial government]], including the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]] (Dutch Marine Corps). The eastward spread of the movement to Sumatra's East Coast states — including [[Deli]], [[Batu Bara]], [[Tanah Putih]], [[Balah]] and [[Panei]] — is documented in the Dutch sources printed by [[P. H. van der Kemp|P. H. Van der Kemp]] in connection with the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|Sultan of Deli's appeal]] of [[27 August]] [[1824]], and forms the immediate background to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Deli affair]] and to the growing Dutch intervention on Sumatra's East Coast that would, after Van der Kemp's period, culminate in the Dutch conquest of the coast and the long [[Aceh War]].

## The Padri movement

The Padris (Dutch: *Padries*; Malay: *Rinchis*) were Islamic reformers, strongly influenced by *Wahhabi* ideas then spreading through the archipelago after the Mecca pilgrimage, who in the early years of the nineteenth century had launched a religious war in the Minangkabau highlands of west-central Sumatra. Their movement, inspired by a strict interpretation of Islam, sought to purge local customs deemed un-Islamic. By the early 1820s their activity had begun to spill eastward towards the [[Straits of Malacca]], threatening the small Malay principalities of the East Coast that had hitherto lived under the loose suzerainty of [[Acheh]] and in commercial contact with the British settlement of [[Penang]].

## The East-Coast theatre (1824–1828)

The principal notice of the East-Coast Padri movement in Van der Kemp's study is contained in the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|letter of the Sultan of Deli]] to Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of [[27 August]] [[1824]] (printed as Appendix 6). The Sultan reported that "many of the districts of Pulo Puchee" (i.e. [[Sumatra]]) were in a very disturbed state, "for the Rinchis ([[\|Padries]]) have determined to attack all the different states and render them tributary, to become obedient to all their peculiar laws and orders." Among the states already reduced to subjection and rendered tributary were [[Tanah Putih]], [[Balah]] and [[Panei]] — and the Padries had now expressed their determination to attack [[Batu Bara]] and [[Deli]] itself.

The Sultan of Deli's letter shows that the Padri advance had, by mid-[[1824]], reached the very threshold of the principal East-Coast states. The Sultan declared that "none of us Rajahs in this quarter will consent to obey them," and that he would proceed to [[Batu Bara]] himself if hostilities commenced. He begged the British Governor for instructions as to how assistance might be obtained — a request that, given the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch treaty of 17 March 1824]] recently signed at London, the British were in no position to grant, the whole of Sumatra having been declared outside the British sphere.

The Padri threat forms the immediate background to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Deli affair]] of [[1824]]–[[1828]]. [[Said Akil]]'s marriage to the [[Sultan of Deli]]'s daughter c. 1824, and his installation as *Raja Mooda* (deputy ruler) of Deli after the Sultan's death, took place while the state was already menaced by the Padris. Akil's depredations, of which the [[Shahbandar of Deli]] complained to Governor Fullerton in early [[1828]], compounded the disorder produced by the Padri advance, and the inability of either the British government of [[Penang]] or the Dutch colonial government at [[Batavia]] to police the East Coast left the Malay traders of [[Batu Bara]] and the surrounding states to fend for themselves.

## Dutch military involvement

Dutch military response to the Padri rising included regular army units and specialised marine detachments. As recorded in the *De Geschiedenis van het Nederlandsche Korps Mariniers* (pp. 102-104), a detachment of the [[Royal Netherlands Marine Corps|Korps Mariniers]] took part in an **[[Expedition against the Padri's (1838)|expedition against the Padri's (1838)]]**, demonstrating the Crown's willingness to project force beyond the immediate theatre of the war in the Minangkabau highlands. Within a few years of the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|Sultan of Deli's appeal of 1824]] the Dutch colonial government had launched military expeditions against the Padris, both in the Minangkabau highlands (the Padri War proper, c. [[1821]]–[[1837]]) and on the East Coast, where Dutch posts were established at [[Batu Bara]], [[Deli]] and elsewhere.

## The West-Coast dimension

The Padri War was also the subject of a [[1939]] retrospective by **[[S. da Costa|S. da Costa]]**, *[[De Padrie-oorlog (Costa, 1939)|De Padrie-oorlog; haar oorsprong, strijd en verloop ter Sumatra's Westkust]]* ("The Padri War; its origin, struggle, and course on Sumatra's West Coast"), published in the *Orgaan van de Nederlandsch-Indische Officiersvereeniging*, No. 24, p. 149 *e.v.*, and reprinted in *Ons Aller Belang*, No. 33, p. 318 *e.v.* The article is recorded as No. 558 in [[H. van Meurs|Van Meurs]]'s *[[Literatuur-Overzicht over het jaar 1939 (Van Meurs, 1940)|Literatuur-Overzicht over het jaar 1939]]* (The Hague, [[1940]]), in the Sumatra chapter.

Da Costa's survey treated the Padri War in three aspects — its *oorsprong* (origin), *strijd* (struggle), and *verloop* (course) — as fought on [[Sumatra's Westkust|Sumatra's Westkust]] (Sumatra's West Coast), the western seaboard of the island. The restriction to the west coast is the article's distinctive contribution: where Van der Kemp's study treats the Padri War from the perspective of the east-coast theatre, da Costa's survey treats the war as it played out on the western seaboard, where the colonial engagement with the Padries intersected with the longer history of Dutch involvement in the west-coast ports and with the geopolitics of the [[Acheh|Atjehnese]] sphere of influence on the same coast. The double publication — in the officers' journal and in the general colonial periodical — marks the article as one judged to be of interest to both military and civilian colonial readerships.

## Connection to Aceh

Although the main theatre of the Padri War lay in west-central [[Sumatra]], the conflict affected the broader political landscape of the northern tip of the island. The [[Aceh|Aceh Sultanate]], wary of Dutch encroachment, monitored the war closely and at times offered covert support to Padri factions to counterbalance Dutch influence. The Padri movement was one of the several threads — together with the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Achehnese succession dispute]], the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Raffles-Acheh convention]] of 1819, and the subsequent [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty]] — that drew the colonial powers deeper into Sumatran affairs and ultimately set the stage for the long Aceh conflict of the late nineteenth century.

## Outcome and significance

The Dutch ultimately subdued the Padri resistance, consolidating their control over the interior of [[Sumatra]]. The war's conclusion paved the way for further Dutch expansion toward the Aceh coast, setting the stage for the later [[Aceh War]] ([[1873]]–[[1904]]). Van der Kemp's mention of the Padri movement is brief but pointed: the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|letter of 27 August 1824]] demonstrates that already in [[1824]] the East-Coast states were begging for European intervention against the Padries, and that the British — having just signed the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London]] — were unable or unwilling to provide it. The way was thus opened for the Dutch to take up the task, with consequences that would culminate before the century's end in the [[Aceh War|Aceh conflict]].
