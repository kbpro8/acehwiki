**The Acheh Demographic and Economic Survey of 1935** was the comprehensive demographic and economic reassessment of the residency of [[Acheh (Atjeh)|Atjeh]] undertaken by the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]] in [[1935]], at the depth of the [[1930s|depression]] that had collapsed the export economy of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] and in the wake of the [[1930|1930]] Netherlands Indies census (Dutch: *Volkstelling*) whose Atjeh returns had first systematically charted the population of the residency. The survey consolidated the demographic data of the [[1930|1930 census]] with the economic data collected by the [[Bureau van Financieele Aangelegenheden|Bureau of Financial Affairs]] and the agricultural and trade statistics of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|colonial administration]], and it supplied the empirical foundation for the late-colonial state's planning of relief, taxation, and the regulation of the plantation sector during the depression years. As an episode in the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial state's]] knowledge-project, the survey belongs to the same late-colonial documentary tradition as [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer's *Atjeh* (1922-23)]] and the surveys of the [[Encyclopaedisch Bureau|Encyclopaedisch Bureau]], and it stands as the principal statistical portrait of Atjeh on the eve of the dislocations of the late [[1930s|1930s]] and the [[1942|Japanese invasion]] of [[1942]].

## The 1930 census and the demographic baseline

The demographic baseline of the 1935 survey was the [[1930|1930]] Netherlands Indies census, the *Volkstelling van 1930*, whose Atjeh returns had given the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] its first systematic population count of the residency. The census had recorded the population of the sub-division of [[Koeta-Radja|Koetaradja]] at approximately 80,000 inhabitants — against some 45,000 in the sub-division of [[Seulimeum|Seulimeum]] — together forming the division of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] with its almost 125,000 inhabitants. The division of [[Pidie|Pidië]], with its sub-divisional headquarters at [[Sigli|Sigli]] and its nearly 135,000 inhabitants, was recorded as the most populous of all Atjeh, while the [[Gajolanden|Gayo Lands]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]] of the interior were charted for the first time in their post-[[Construction of the Gajoweg|Gajoweg]] demographic configuration. The census had also recorded the confessional and ethnographic composition of the population — the Acehnese majority, the Gayo and Alas minorities of the interior, the Javanese and Sundanese contract-labourers of the plantation belt, the Chinese commercial community of [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta Radja]] and [[Sabang|Sabang]], and the European administrative and military population — and the 1935 survey took these returns as its baseline for the reassessment of the residency's demographic trajectory. The 1935 survey was, in this sense, a five-year-on update of the [[1930|1930]] census rather than an independent enumeration, and its principal demographic task was to estimate the impact of the depression on the population distribution.

## Economic context: the depression and the export economy

The economic content of the 1935 survey was determined by the collapse of the Atjeh export economy that had begun with the [[1930|1930]] fall in commodity prices and had deepened through the early [[1930s|1930s]]. The [[latex-aceh|rubber]] exports of the Atjeh interior — both the estate-produced *pale crêpe* and the smallholder *inlandse-crepe|inlandse crêpe* — had been hit by the Stevenson restriction scheme of the [[1920s]] and the [[International Rubber Regulation Agreement|International Rubber Regulation Agreement]] of [[1934]], and the smallholder economy of the residency had contracted in parallel. The [[palm-oil|oil-palm]] estates of the East Coast, the [[arabica-coffee|arabica coffee]] gardens of the [[Gajolanden|Gayo highlands]], the [[copra|copra]] economy of the coastal coconut groves, the [[tea-aceh|Gayo tea]] estates, and the [[sisal|sisal]] fibre plantations had all been driven into retrenchment or abandonment by the collapse of the world commodity prices, and the [[Sabang|free port of Sabang]] — the great transit entrepôt of the straits — had seen its transit trade sharply reduced. The 1935 survey accordingly concentrated on the measurement of this contraction: the acreage and output of the plantation sector, the volume and value of the smallholder export crops, the trade figures of the residency's ports, the revenue of the customs houses, and the fiscal position of the *landschapskassen* (Dutch: *landschapskassen*, the treasuries of the indigenous self-governing lands). The survey's economic returns gave the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] the empirical basis for the depression-era regulation of the Atjeh economy.

## Methodology and the colonial knowledge-project

The 1935 survey was conducted by the regular administrative staff of the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] — the *controleurs* and *assistent-residenten* of the sub-divisions, working under the direction of the resident — and by the statistical bureaux of the central [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] at [[Batavia|Buitenzorg]] and [[Batavia|Batavia]]. Its methodology combined the direct enumeration of the European administrative population with the indirect estimation of the indigenous population through the agency of the *uleebalang* and the indigenous heads of village, and it drew on the customs-house records, the plantation returns, and the agricultural statistics compiled by the [[Bureau van Financieele Aangelegenheden|Bureau of Financial Affairs]] and the agricultural service. The survey belongs, in this sense, to the late-colonial knowledge-project that had produced [[Kreemer's Atjeh (1922-23)|Kreemer's encyclopaedic *Atjeh*]] in [[1922]]–[[1923]], the surveys of the [[Encyclopaedisch Bureau|Encyclopaedisch Bureau]], and the regular statistical reports of the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]], and it was the immediate successor to these earlier compilations in the documentary record of the residency. Its results were intended to inform the late-colonial state's depression-era planning — the regulation of the plantation sector, the relief of the smallholder economy, the restructuring of the *landschapskassen*, and the projected educational and infrastructural investments of the [[Ethische Politiek|Ethical Policy]] in its late, retrenchment-driven phase.

## Significance and legacy

The 1935 survey is significant as the principal statistical portrait of Atjeh at the end of the depression decade and on the eve of the dislocations of the late [[1930s|1930s]] and the [[1942|Japanese invasion]] of [[1942]]. It recorded the Atjeh economy at the nadir of its inter-war contraction — the smallholder economy under the pressure of the international restriction schemes, the plantation sector in retrenchment, the transit trade of [[Sabang|Sabang]] diminished — and it gave the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] the empirical baseline against which the subsequent recovery of the late [[1930s|1930s]] and the dislocations of the Japanese occupation would be measured. The survey's demographic returns, building on the [[1930|1930]] census, supplied the population figures that would inform the wartime and post-war planning of the residency, and its economic returns supplied the empirical foundation for the post-[[1945|1945]] surveys of the plantation sector that the [[Survey Parties to Atjeh (February 1950)|survey parties of February 1950]] would use as their pre-war baseline. The 1935 survey is, in this sense, the principal documentary bridge between the late-colonial statistical record of Atjeh and the post-colonial record that would succeed it, and its figures remain the principal source for the demographic and economic history of the residency in the depression decade.
