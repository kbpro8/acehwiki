A mystical devotional performance combining recited Arabic dikr with synchronized body movements, founded by the 18th-century Medina-based teacher Muhammad Samman and transmitted to Acheh, where it became one of the most popular forms of devotional recreation, despite later disciples replacing much of the original meaningful Arabic text with rhythmic but meaningless sounds.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Rateb Samman (ratib Samman)

The **rateb Samman** (Achehnese, from the Arabic *ratib Samman*) was the simultaneous intoning of a terribly noisy litany named after [[Shaikh Samman|Shaikh Samman]] (also called [[Cheh Saman|Cheh Saman]]), the sainted founder of a *tariqah* or mystic order who lived at [[Medina|Medina]] a couple of centuries ago. The litany was one of the religious exercises of the Sammaniyyah order, and was popular among the Mohammedans of the [[East Indian Archipelago|East Indian Archipelago]] of the old stamp. [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] notes that it might be heard almost every week in many of the kampongs of [[Batavia|Batavia]].

### Place in the meunasah

The rateb Samman was one of the religious observances that gave the [[Meunasah|meunasah]] its character as a house of prayer as well as a club and sleeping-place for the men of the gampong. Among the furniture of the meunasah were the *rapai* or kettledrums, and the wooden rings known as *pulet*, which were required not only for the [[Rateb pulet and rateb sadati|rateb pulet and rateb sadati]] — the secular or quasi-secular chants that borrowed the name and noise of religious litanies — but also for the genuine mystical exercises such as the rateb Samman. The litany thus belonged to that part of Achehnese religious life in which the popular amusements of the gampong shaded into the genuine practices of the mystic orders.

### The form of the recitation

The rateb Samman consisted of formulas drawn from the *dikr* of the Sammaniyyah order, recited in unison by a seated company under the lead of the [[Teungku|teungku]]. The chant rose in volume and speed until, in its more ecstatic forms, the participants — overcome by what they regarded as the violence of their spiritual transport — rose up, sat down again, leapt, danced, and finally fell to the floor in sheer exhaustion. This culminating state of trance was called *do'* by the Achehnese — a term derived from the Arabic *dauq* ("taste"), which in mystical vocabulary denotes the tasting of higher spiritual enjoyments, but which in Achehnese usage had narrowed to the meaning "trance." The performance forms part of the wider Achehnese culture of [[Kanduri|kanduri]] feasts and night-long religious observances that punctuate the [[Ramadhan (fasting month)|fasting month]].

### The Peutamat daroh and the Samman

The rateb Samman was most characteristically performed on the night of the [[Peutamat daroh|peutamat daroh]], the feast that marked the conclusion of a complete recitation of the Quran during the [[Ramadhan (fasting month)|Puasa]]. On that occasion the ordinary [[Traweh (Tarawih)|traweh]] prayers were followed not by the usual *meudaroih* (silent listening to a solo chanter) but by this clamorous rite. The combination placed the rateb Samman at the very summit of Achehnese religious enthusiasm: the solemn completion of the Sacred Book, the noisy chanting of the Sammaniyyah formulas, and the trance that followed, all joined to make the peutamat daroh one of the great nights of the Achehnese year.

### Popularity and condemnation

Although the rateb Samman was understood by the [[Ulema|ulamas]] to belong properly to the sphere of supererogatory devotion, its vitality in Achehnese life came from popular enthusiasm rather than from the learned tradition. Its blend of vocal chant, rhythmic movement, and induced trance placed it, in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s classification, among those Achehnese observances in which orthodox Shafi'ite practice and popular mysticism shaded into one another. The stricter ulamas looked askance at the transports of the Sammaniyyah, but the popularity of the rite was too great to be set aside; the young people, in particular, delighted more in the chanting of the [[Rateb pulet and rateb sadati|rateb pulet and rateb sadati]], which had in common with the religious litanies only the name and the noise.

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Rateb Saman

The **Rateb Saman** (also called **rateb meuse** or **kuluhut** in Achehnese) is a particularly noisy and ecstatic form of the *rateb Saman*, a communal mystical chant named after [[Samman|Shaikh Samman]] (Cheh Saman), the founder of a *tariqah* (mystic order) who died at [[Medina]] in 1152 Hijrah. The practice was especially popular among native Mohammedans of the old stamp throughout the East Indian Archipelago and held a recognised place in Achehnese religious life at the close of the nineteenth century.

In Acheh the rateb Saman was most characteristically performed on the night of the **peutamat daroh**, the feast that marks the conclusion of a complete recitation of the Quran during the [[Puasa (Ramadhan fasting month)|Puasa]] (Ramadhan). On that occasion the ordinary [[Traweh (Tarawih)|traweh]] prayers were followed not by the usual *meudaroih* (silent listening to a solo chanter) but by this clamorous rite. The chant consists of formulas drawn from the *dikr* of the Sammaniyyah order, recited in unison by a seated company under the lead of the teungku. The formulas grow progressively shorter and the tempo faster until the participants, overcome by what they regard as the violence of their spiritual transport, rise up, sit down again, leap, dance, and finally fall to the floor in sheer exhaustion from the ecstasy arising from their contemplation of the divine.

This culminating state of trance is called *do'* by the Achehnese — a term derived from the Arabic *dauq* ("taste"), which in mystical vocabulary denotes the tasting of higher spiritual enjoyments but which in Achehnese usage has narrowed to the meaning "trance." It is from this feature that the most clamorous form of the rite takes its alternative names *rateb meuse* and *kuluhut*. The performance forms part of the wider Achehnese culture of [[Kanduri|kanduri]] feasts and night-long religious observances that punctuate the fasting month, alongside the traweh prayers, the *meudaroih* Quranic recitations, and the special [[Kanduri Mo'lot|kanduri]] associated with the prophet's birth.

Snouck Hurgronje notes that the rateb Saman was understood by the ulamas to belong properly to the sphere of supererogatory devotion, yet in practice it drew its vitality from popular enthusiasm rather than from the learned tradition. Its blend of vocal chant, rhythmic movement, and induced trance placed it, in his classification, among those Achehnese observances in which orthodox Shafi'ite practice and popular mysticism shaded into one another.
