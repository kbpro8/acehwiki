**The Gold Coast cession and the Atjeh-Oorlog context of 1872** is the closing Atjeh-related reference in Dr. F. W. Stapel's *Geschiedkundige Atlas van Nederland*, by which the [[The Netherlands|Netherlands]]' cession of its Gold Coast possessions to [[British Empire|Great Britain]] — ratified on [[17 February]] [[1872]] and formalised as the Wet of [[20 January]] [[1872]] (Staatsblad No. 6) — freed Dutch hands for the coming [[Aceh War|Atjeh-Oorlog]] while Britain confronted the [[Ashanti Empire|Asjantijnen]] in West Africa. In Stapel's text it is the explicit linkage between the end of the Dutch Atlantic empire and the opening of the largest Dutch colonial war of the nineteenth century.

## Background

The Dutch possessions on the [[Gold Coast|Gold Coast]] of West Africa — a string of forts from [[Axim|Axem]] to [[Chama|Chama]] — had been a feature of Dutch overseas presence since the seventeenth century, but by the late nineteenth century they had become commercially marginal. "The trade relations also meant little more: the firm of Rijckevorsel had withdrawn, while the Rotterdam firm of Muller saw more advantage in its relations with Mozambique and Liberia, so that only one of its ships once a year called at the Coast" (*de handelsrelaties beteekenden ook weinig meer: de firma van Rijckevorsel had zich teruggetrokken, terwijl de Rotterdamsche firma Muller meer voordeel zag in hare betrekkingen met Moçambique en Liberia, zoodat slechts één harer schepen eenmaal per jaar de Kust aanliep*).

The Dutch government concluded "that it would be in everyone's interest if the coast came under the authority of England alone; she may thereby have harboured the secondary thought that a transfer to England could be played out to obtain a free hand in North Sumatra" (*dat het in ieders belang zou zijn zoo de kust onder het gezag kwam van Engeland alleen; zij mag daarbij de nevengedachte hebben gekoesterd dat een overgave aan Engeland kon worden uitgespeeld om de vrije hand in Noord-Sumatra te verkrijgen*).

## The 1871-1872 treaty

"On 25 February 1871 a tractate tending to the cession was concluded. Ratified on 17 February 1872, it has been promulgated as the Wet of 20 January 1872 (Staatsblad No. 6)" (*Den 25 Februari 1871 is een traktaat strekkende tot den afstand gesloten. Geratificeerd den 17 Februari 1872 is het afgekondigd als Wet van 20 Januari 1872 (Staatsblad No. 6)*). The Dutch public "had left itself rather unprotesting; the deliberations in the First Chamber had however been broadly set up, they also penetrated deeper to the core than in the Second Chamber. Watched was that the transfer was complete, so that there could be no question of a 'sale'" (*Het Nederlandsch publick had zich tamelijk onbetuigd gelaten; de beraadslagingen in de Eerste Kamer waren niettemin breed opgezet geweest, zij drongen ook dieper tot de kern door dan in de Tweede Kamer. Gewaakt werd, dat de overdracht volkomen was, zóó dat er niet zou kunnen worden gesproken van een 'verkoop'*).

## The 6 April 1872 transfer

"In the morning of 6 April 1872 was, on the Castle S. Jorge, simultaneously with and next to the Netherlands flag, an English one hoisted. Then the acting Netherlands Governor Ferguson handed over the colony to the Governor-General of the English Possessions on the West Coast of Africa, Sir J. Pope Henessy. At the setting of the sun both flags were taken down, the tricolour for ever, the cross of St. George to take up with the beginning of the next day the sole rule over the landscape" (*In den morgen van den 6den April 1872 werd, op het Kasteel S. Jorge, gelijktijdig met en naast de Nederlandsche vlag eene Engelsche geheschen. Toen gaf de waarnemend Nederlandsch Gouverneur Ferguson de kolonie over aan den Gouverneur-Generaal der Engelsche Bezittingen ter Westkust van Afrika, Sir J. Pope Henessy. Bij het ondergaan der zon werden beide vlaggen neergehaald, de driekleur voor altijd, het kruis van St. George om met het begin van den volgenden dag de alleenheerschappij over het landschap te voeren*).

## The Atjeh-Oorlog context

The closing Atjeh reference is in Stapel's next sentence: "The Netherlands could now undisturbed prepare for the Atjeh-Oorlog: England got the war against the Asjantijnen in prospect, which, if in less acute phases, would also come to last until about the year 1900" (*Nederland kon zich nu ongestoord opmaken tot den Atjeh-Oorlog: Engeland kreeg den oorlog tegen de Asjantijnen in het vooruitzicht, die, zij het in minder acute phasen, ook tot omstreeks het jaar 1900 zou komen te duren*).

The parallel is exact: the Dutch Atjeh War (1873–c. 1904) and the British Ashanti wars (1873–1900) are presented as the simultaneous colonial commitments that the Gold Coast cession enabled on both sides. The cession released the Dutch from West African entanglement at the cost of opening them to the largest colonial war in their nineteenth-century history; the British assumption of the Gold Coast brought them into the long Ashanti contest that would close the century.

## Significance

The Gold Coast cession is the closing Atjeh-related event in Stapel's *De Koloniën*, and the explicit linkage between the end of the Dutch Atlantic empire and the opening of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-Oorlog]] is the strongest single statement of the connection between the Atlantic and Indian Ocean theatres of Dutch colonial policy. The cession is also the moment at which the long Dutch West African presence — opened in the seventeenth century — finally ends, and the moment at which the long Dutch Acehnese presence — opened in 1598 with the [[First Dutch voyage to Atjeh (1598-1599)|Houtman brothers' voyage]] — enters its largest and most costly phase.

The reference to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-Oorlog]] is the only explicit mention of the nineteenth-century Aceh War in Stapel's *De Koloniën*, and it appears as the closing bookend of a narrative that has followed the Dutch-Acehnese relationship from its 1598 opening. The Gold Coast cession is therefore the structural pivot between the early modern Dutch-Acehnese story that is the subject of the Westkust chapters and the modern Dutch-Acehnese story that would dominate the nineteenth century.
