The **Musem Barat** ("west season") was the Achehnese name for the south-west monsoon, the season of winds that blew from the south-west across the Achehnese coast. Its commencement fell within the month of [[Ramadhan]], in [[keunong tujoh blah]] (the 7th of the keunong series in its "blah" or intercalary form), which in the year 1310 of the Mohammedan era corresponded to the 4th of April. With the musem barat came the maximum altitude of the sun at noon (*seunang mata uròë*), which actually occurred on the 5th of April; the two events—the onset of the south-west wind and the highest point of the sun—thus marked the transition from the calmer weather of the early year to the season of storm and rain.

The rhythm of the two monsoons governed the whole of Achehnese navigation and much of its agriculture. The two winds were not, however, of equal force at all times: there were periods in which each was said to "slumber" for five or seven days, during which the otherwise dangerous voyage from the capital to the [[West Coast]] could be safely undertaken. Just as the north-east wind (*musem tiniu*) slumbered for five to seven days in [[keunong 23]], so the south-west wind slumbered in [[keunong siblah]] (the 7th of the keunong series in the month of Dul-hijjah), and during these days it was safe to sail from the capital to the West Coast. Such intervals of calm were of the greatest practical importance to trade, correspondence, and the seasonal movement of pilgrims and pepper-planters between the two coasts.

The musem barat was the season of stormy weather at sea. In [[keunong limong blah]] (the 5th of the keunong series, corresponding to about the 2nd of May), some began ploughing, and stormy weather prevailed at sea. This was the season in which maritime trade contracted, and in which the seine-fishers held their first division's [[kanduri la'ot]] at [[Ulee Lheue]] in keunong 17 (April). The 100-day interval of the musem barat—the wet and stormy season that followed the sowing of the padi—was the period during which access to the common rice-fields was most strictly regulated, since the growing crop had to be protected from cattle. With the close of the musem barat and the onset of the [[musem tiniu]] (north-east monsoon) in keunong limong, the second division of the seine-fishers celebrated their kanduri la'ot, and the most favourable time for the voyage from the capital to the West Coast began.

The two monsoons thus formed the great bi-polar rhythm of the Achehnese year, dividing it into a season of storm and a season of relative calm, of wet and dry, of restricted and of open navigation. Their alternation was recorded not by the months of the Mohammedan lunar calendar but by the [[keunong]] series, which marked the conjunctions of moon and Scorpion against the background of the solar year. Through this reckoning the Achehnese were able to predict, with reasonable accuracy, the onset of the musem barat and the operations—ploughing, sowing, the celebration of the [[kanduri blang]]—that belonged to it. The musem barat thus stood in a close and reciprocal relation to the agricultural and maritime calendar of Achehnese life, no less than to the cycle of its religious observances.
