The **me tanda kong narit** — literally "the bringing of a token that the agreement has been ratified" — is the Achehnese ceremony of betrothal, by which the preliminary agreement between the families of a prospective bride and bridegroom is solemnised by the formal presentation of a *tanda* or token of good faith. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] describes it in his chapter on proposal, betrothal and marriage, the *me tanda kong narit* is the central act of the betrothal, the ceremony in which the agreement between the two families passes from a verbal understanding to a binding contract. It is, in Hurgronje's phrase, "a betrothal gift in fact."

The ceremony is preceded by a preliminary arrangement. The symbolic language of the proposal having been dropped, "a preliminary agreement is concluded" between the parents or guardians of the prospective couple, "and a deputation from the bridegroom's side proceeds to the house of the bride's parents, where the girl resides, in order to fix a day for the *me tanda kong narit*." The best day for the *me tanda kong narit* is in its turn carefully chosen — "generally on the 14th (full moon) of the month," a preference which reflects the wider Achehnese regard for the full moon as an auspicious time for beginnings. The deputation is composed of the *wali*, or representative, of the bridegroom's side and other intermediaries "who undertake the presentation of the gift of betrothal." Besides the betrothal-gift, they bring what is called the *ranub dòëng* — the betel set presented on all formal Achehnese occasions, and the indispensable vehicle of courtesy and ceremony in Achehnese social life.

The betrothal-gift itself — the *tanda* — is a valuable object, usually of gold, which the gampong of the bride now produces in token of good faith. The legal consequences of the *tanda* are defined by the Achehnese adat: "If the engagement be broken off later through the man's fault, the *tanda* ..." (becomes the property of the bride's family); and the converse holds where the breach is the woman's. The institution of the *tanda* is one of the principal means by which Achehnese adat regulates the relations between the families, and it sits at the centre of the chapter on betrothal in the Achehnese marriage cycle. The family of the bridegroom, having given the *tanda*, acquires thereby a recognised standing in the family of the bride: "the father of the bridegroom is now ... in social conditions, [which] commences from the time of the betrothal. Neither [party] cross the threshold of the girl's parents [for certain purposes] until the continuance of the betrothal" has been solemnised by the marriage itself.

The *me tanda kong narit* thus marks the formal opening of that complex of mutual obligations which culminates in the wedding. The presentation of the *tanda* is itself a religiously sanctioned act — the teungku is present, and the *fatihah* is recited — but the legal weight of the ceremony lies in the adat, which fixes the consequences of breach and the standing of the parties between betrothal and marriage. The ceremony is one of the principal life-cycle events of Achehnese society, and its observance was universal, even among families which could afford only a modest *tanda*. The most ceremonious form, with its full complement of intermediaries, its *ranub dòëng*, its careful choice of the 14th of the month, and its binding legal consequences, exemplifies the Achehnese genius for surrounding every important transition of life with a double apparatus of adat and religious observance.
