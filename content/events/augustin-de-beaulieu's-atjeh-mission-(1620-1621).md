**Augustin de Beaulieu's Atjeh mission of 1620**–**1621** was the French attempt to break into the pepper trade of the Sultan of [[Acheh|Atjeh]]. Driven past Cape Guardafui and unable to make the intended coast, De Beaulieu landed at [[Tikoe]] on Sumatra's Westkust — a pepper-port dependent on the Sultan of Atjeh — and from there came to Atjeh itself in early [[1621]] for difficult negotiations with the Sultan and the *Rijksgrooten*. The mission ended in failure: no pepper-trade permission was obtained, the Vice-Admiral [[Robert Gravé|Robert Gravé]] died and was buried in Atjeh, and the French claim for damages against the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] that arose out of the related loss of the *l'Espérance* was eventually settled at 550,000 *livres*.

## The expedition

The Saint-Malo expedition, led by [[Augustin de Beaulieu|Augustin de Beaulieu]], consisted of several ships: the *Montmorency* (De Beaulieu's flagship), the *l'Hermitage*, the *l'Espérance* (under the Vice-Admiral [[Robert Gravé|Robert Gravé]]), and the *Leiden*. The expedition's purpose was to break into the pepper trade that the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]] was consolidating in the Indies.

## Landfall at Tikoe

Driven past Cape Guardafui, De Beaulieu could not make the intended coast and landed with the *Montmorency* and *l'Hermitage* at [[Tikoe]] on Sumatra's Westkust — "een centrum van peperhandel, afhankelijk van den Sultan van Atjeh" — a centre of pepper trade, dependent on the Sultan of Atjeh. [[Tikoe]]'s dependency on Atjeh meant that any pepper trade there required the Sultan's permission.

## Atjeh negotiations, early 1621

From [[Tikoe]] De Beaulieu came in early [[1621]] to Atjeh itself, where he had difficult negotiations with the Sultan and the *Rijksgrooten* (great officers) to obtain permission for the pepper trade. He particularly complained about the extortion of gifts during the negotiations: "vooral klaagt De Beaulieu over het afpersen van geschenken bij de besprekingen" — De Beaulieu particularly complained about the extortion of gifts during the discussions. The complaint is a recurrent feature of European accounts of the Atjeh court, and would be echoed by other visitors.

## The English ship with the sick Gravé, November 1621

Suddenly, in November [[1621]], while the *Montmorency* lay in a bay to be cleaned of the shell-layer that was impeding her sailing, an English ship appeared aboard which was the gravely ill Vice-Admiral [[Robert Gravé|Gravé]]. He, together with one De Parc of the *l'Espérance*'s crew, told the story of that ship — a tale full of inaccuracies that later appeared in print in De Beaulieu's travel-adventures. The French had not reckoned on the Dutch carefully checking and recording their doings.

## The loss of the *l'Espérance*

The truth was that before Tikoe the *l'Espérance* had lain twelve days waiting for a sloop sent ashore with fifteen sick sailors to fetch help. The sloop never reappeared. Ten of the sailors died; the five survivors took a Dutch yacht to Atjeh to seek their Admiral. On the great ship were 74 souls; [[Robert Gravé|Gravé]] and three others were still on their feet, but the ship was unmanageable for want of hands. The Dutch ship *Leiden* found the French "gants machteloos" — entirely powerless — with only two casks of water remaining aboard.

Some of the sick were taken aboard the *Leiden*; the *l'Espérance* was given a crew of 50 Dutchmen. At the island Nassau it was hoped the sick would recover, but many found their grave there. When the *Leiden* and the *l'Espérance* reached Jakatra, 95 persons had died and 29 survived. The French printed account claims the ship was taken by force and brought to Java — "Tijdgenooten wisten beter" — contemporaries knew better. The Dutch ambassador in Paris was thanked by French nobles interested in the Dieppe expedition for the help given to the *l'Espérance* — for which favour they waived complaints about the lifting of Dutch sailors from French ships.

## The *Raad van Defensie* and French inaccuracies

The French had not reckoned on the possibility that their doings were being carefully checked and recorded in writing, "zoodat het niet moeilijk was voor den uit Engelschen en Hollanders bestaanden 'Raad van Defensie' om om de onjuistheid der Fransche beweringen met de stukken aan te toonen" — so that it was not difficult for the "Raad van Defensie" (Council of Defence), composed of English and Dutch, to demonstrate the inaccuracy of the French assertions with the documents in hand.

## The death and burial of Gravé at Atjeh

[[Robert Gravé|Gravé]] went in the bark *l'Hermitage* to Atjeh, where De Beaulieu had already departed. By English ship he set out, happened to meet his chief, and told him, deathly ill, of his troubles in the presence of witnesses. In the night of [[9 November]] [[1621]], two hours before daybreak, Gravé died of a violent fever "causée de facherie" — caused by vexation — arising "uit ergernis" (from chagrin). He was buried in Atjeh. See [[Robert Gravé]].

## Aftermath and the French claim

When [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] was in Holland in [[1623]] he wrote that the English and French questions together had cost the Company some sixteen tonnes of gold. The French shipowners eventually received their claimed damages — 550,000 *livres* — paid out by the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]]. Le Tellier's grave is in Bantam. Gravé's grave is in Atjeh.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, De Beaulieu's mission is the principal French attempt of the early seventeenth century to enter the Atjeh pepper trade directly — at the Sultan's court, in formal negotiation with the *Rijksgrooten*, and through the Westkust dependency of [[Tikoe]]. Its failure, and the related loss of the *l'Espérance* with 95 dead, defined the limits of French commercial intrusion into a trade that the VOC was successfully consolidating. The death and burial of [[Robert Gravé|Gravé]] at Atjeh left a French grave in the sultanate and a long-running French diplomatic case against the Company.
