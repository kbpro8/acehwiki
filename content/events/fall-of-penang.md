**The fall of Penang on [[19 December]] [[1941]]** was the Japanese capture of the British free port of [[Penang|Penang]] — the great entrepôt whose "lively trade relations, which had been maintained with the [[Overwal|Overwal]] since time immemorial," were concentrated on the island — and the event which enabled [[Major Fujiwara|Major Fujiwara]] to begin the construction of the [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]], the fifth-column organisation that would prepare the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of Atjèh]] of [[12 March]] [[1942]]. Piekaar's *Atjèh and the War with Japan* treats the loss of Penang as the strategic turning point which placed Atjèh, "considered safe behind strong Singapore, in a strategically advanced position."

## Strategic consequences

Penang's fall was the first of the cascade of British defeats that exposed the Overwal to Japanese attack: "19 December 1941 fall of Penang, 28 December Ipoh, 11 January 1942 Kuala Lumpur, 15 February Singapore." From the moment of its capture, "regular air reconnaissance flights took place over Aceh," and "the strategically important [[Sabang|Sabang]] was repeatedly bombed, most heavily on [[22 January]] [[1942]]." Radio-Penang became the principal Japanese propaganda instrument in northern Sumatra: "the propaganda conducted by radio-Penang, which was listened to much more generally than we had desired, contributed to" the Indonesian population's realisation "earlier than the Dutch, who against better knowledge continued to believe in the miracle, the inevitability of a Japanese occupation."

## Founding of the Fujiwara-kikan

The fall of Penang was the indispensable precondition for the construction of the [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] — literally "the Fujiwara organisation," named for its founder. "Already after the fall of Penang on 19 December 1941, the construction of this organization could be begun. For on Penang, the lively trade relations, which had been maintained with the Overwal since time immemorial, were concentrated." The free port hosted a large Atjèhnese colony of merchants and seamen, "besides many merchants and seamen, also numerous people who, for political or other reasons, voluntarily or not, preferred an existence abroad." These men, recruited by [[Major Fujiwara|Fujiwara]] and placed under the operational leadership of [[Masubuchi|Masubuchi]], became the F-propagandists who, in the weeks before the invasion, slipped back into Atjèh as "refugees" to make contact with [[Poesa|Poesa]] and to lay the network that would be activated on the night of [[11 March|11]]–[[12 March]] [[1942]].

## Significance

Penang's fall transformed the [[Fujiwara-kikan|Fujiwara-kikan]] from a concept into an operational intelligence apparatus. Without the commercial and personal networks concentrated on Penang, the fifth-column preparation of Atjèh — which gave the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion]] its "dangerous character" — could not have been undertaken. The loss of Penang thus stands as the precondition of the loss of Atjèh.
