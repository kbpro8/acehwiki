**The *Tibjān fī maʿrifat al-adyān*** ("Elucidation concerning the knowledge of religions") is the first of the two Malay treatises of [[Nur al-Din al-Raniri|Nuruddin ar-Raniri]] edited in facsimile by [[P. Voorhoeve|P. Voorhoeve]] in the [[1955]] volume of the *Uitgaven van de Stichting De Goeje*. Composed before Raniri's arrival in [[Acheh]] in [[1637]] and probably completed in [[1637]] or shortly thereafter, it enumerates the pre-Islamic religions and the sects within Islam with the avowed aim of warning the faithful against these aberrations and — above all — against the doctrine of the heretical [[Wujudiyyah]] that flourished at the Achehnese court of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] under the influence of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] and the school of [[Hamzah Pansuri]]. The Voorhoeve edition reproduces the Leiden manuscript Cod. Or. 3291 from the legacy of [[Herman Neubronner van der Tuuk|Van der Tuuk]], a copy made about [[1855]] for Van der Tuuk from a good older exemplair of Achehnese provenance.

## Background and purpose

Voorhoeve's introduction to the [[1955]] edition places the *Tibjān* in the context of Raniri's polemical mission in Acheh. As an orthodox mystic of the [[Rifa'iyah order|Rifāʿiyya]] who had absorbed from his teacher [[Sajjid Abu Hafs Umar Ba Sjaiban|Sayyid ʿUmar Bā Shaybān]] and his spiritual grandfather [[Muhammad al-Addars|Muḥammad al-ʿAddārs]] the scholastic apparatus of Gujarati Arabian Islam, Raniri came to Acheh with a text already in hand by which the doctrines of the Wujūdiyyah could be set within the wider catalog of heresies — Muslim and pre-Muslim — against which a faithful Shāfiʿite must defend himself. The *Tibjān* is, in this sense, the manifesto of the [[Raniri's public debate against the Wujudiyyah in Acheh|public disputation]] that won Raniri the favour of [[Iskandar Thani]] in the first year of his reign.

The work is divided into two great divisions. *Part I* treats the pre-Muslim religions from [[Adam]] down to ʿĪsā. *Part II* treats the sects of Islam — the seventy-two sects (over and against the one saved sect) said by tradition to have sprung from six original groups, each subdivided into twelve.

## Part I — The pre-Muslim religions

The first part of the *Tibjān* opens with [[Adam]] and the beginning of idol-worship after his death, the five idols of the people of Idrīs, the deluge in the time of Nūḥ, the re-excavation of the idols in the time of Ismāʿīl and their veneration by the Banū Ghatafān, and the three classes of *mushrikūn*. It treats the distinction between *kāfir* and *mushrik*; the Tabiʿūn (or Ṭabāʿ) who worshipped the four elemental qualities; the *ḥukamāʾ munajjimūn* or Falakīyah, who worshipped the planets; the Thanāwiyyah (dualists), Zamzamiyyah, Shamsāniyyah, and Sāmaniyyah; and — notably — [[Hamzah Pansuri]], of whose teaching Raniri says "their doctrine resembles that of the Tanāsuḥiyyah, the Falāsifa, the Barāhima-Wathaniyya, the Sāmaniyya in Tibet, and the Ḥulūliyya." The Madjus (Zoroastrians) are treated with their sub-divisions, and the *Dahriyya* or *Mulhid* (atheists) who believe in metempsychosis, some of whom dwell in [[Gujarat]] and are called the *Śrāwaka* — a Sanskrit term meaning Buddhist, as Professor [[G. W. J. Drewes|Drewes]] identified. The *Ḥukamāʾ Falāsifa* (philosophers) are refuted for their inappropriate comparison of God with the sun. The "Copts" (in fact the *al-Muʿattila*, the sect that denies all divine attributes, misnamed in Raniri's source) and the Sophists are surveyed, and the Tanāsuḥiyyah — metempsychosists — are subdivided into four groups; the *Barāhima* in India in the first; groups akin to the Manawiyya, Ḥulūliyya, and Ghālibiyya among the Rāfiḍīs in the second; reincarnationists in the third; the fourth group is silently omitted by reason of a lacuna in Raniri's exemplar of the *Tamhīd*. Tanāsuḥiyyah are even found, Raniri notes, on [[Java]], on the slopes of Mount [[Merapi]]. The first part closes with the "ten scriptuary groups," of which the first is the Barāhima (so-called followers of Ibrāhīm), the second the Jews (*ʿUzairiyya* and *Sāmiriyya*), and the third the Christians (*Malkāniyya*, *Mastūriyya*, *Mār Yaʿqūbiyya*).

## Part II — The sects of Islam

The second part of the *Tibjān* opens with the tradition of the seventy-three sects — that is, the seventy-two doomed plus one saved. The seventy-two have sprung from six sects, each again divided into twelve: (1) the Rāfiḍiyya or Sabāʾiyya; (2) the Khārijīyya or Ḥarūriyya; (3) the Jabriyya; (4) the Qadariyya; (5) the Jahmiyya; and (6) the Murjiʾa. The sects are then surveyed sect by sect, each with a brief statement of its dogma followed by Raniri's refutation. The survey closes with the heretical Ṣūfīs — thirteen sects enumerated according to [[al-Ghazali|Imām Ghazālī]], [[Abu'n-Nadjib as-Suhrawardi|Abū 'n-Najīb Suhrawardī]], and [[Najm ad-Din an-Nasafi|Najmuddīn ʿUmar an-Nasafī]] — the Musjabbiha or Mujassima, the doctrine of anthropomorphic traditions, and the *bidʿa* (innovation) divided into five kinds according to ʿAlī b. Aḥmad al-Mahāʾimī's *Ithbād an-naṣīḥa*, into two according to Ghazālī, and into five according to [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|Abū Shakūr as-Sālimī]]. The work closes with the true Ṣūfīs, with renewed warnings against the *bidʿa*, and with a closing prayer.

The longest and most historically important section is the survey of the Wujūdiyyah — that is, the *Ittiḥādiyya* or *Mulhid* or *Zindīq*, divided into the upper-wind (bovenwindse) and lower-wind (benedenwindse) Wujūdiyya. To the lower-wind Wujūdiyya belongs [[Hamzah Pansuri]], whose *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muntahī]]* Raniri refutes sect by sect; and [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Sjamsuddin of Pasai]], whose *[[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn]]* and *[[Chirkah|Chirkah]]* (Van Nieuwenhuijze prefers the reading *Harakah*) are likewise taken to task. Raniri explicitly refers to his own lost *[[Lata'if al-asrar|Laṭāʾif al-asrār*]] for a fuller refutation. This portion of the *Tibjān* was edited in part by [[C.A.O. van Nieuwenhuijze|C. A. O. van Nieuwenhuijze]] in *BKI* 104 (1948), pp. 326–370, with a brief translation in his dissertation *Sjamsu'l-Dīn van Pasai* (Leiden, 1945), pp. 206–213.

## Sources of the *Tibjān*

Voorhoeve's examination of the *Tibjān*'s sources is the most original contribution of his edition. Four sources are identified:

1. **The *at-Tamhīd fī bayān at-tawḥīd* of [[Abu Syakur as-Salimi|Abū Shakūr as-Sālimī]]** — a Persian Shāfiʿite of the second half of the fifth century H. (Brockelmann *GAL* I, 419). More than a quarter of the *Tibjān* is translated literally from the *Tamhīd*, especially its last chapter on orthodoxy and the rejection of innovation. Raniri used a manuscript practically identical with the Leiden Cod. Or. 5664 — a Banten manuscript sent to Leiden by [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] in [[1906]] — sharing with it the strange error "Kubtiya" (Copts) for *al-Muʿattila* and a lacuna that caused Raniri to announce four groups of the Tanāsuḥiyya while treating only three. Voorhoeve collated this Leiden manuscript with three Indian copies (India Office Library Loth 384, Levy 1888 and 1890) and with KBG Arab. CCCXI in Jakarta, and was able to recover almost the entire *Tamhīd* substrate of the *Tibjān*.
2. **The *al-Insān al-kāmil* of [[Abd al-Karim al-Jili|ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī]]** — from which Raniri drew, in particular, his account of the Barāhima (which al-Jīlī, uniquely among the sect-writers, classes among the scriptuaries). Raniri's notice on the Barāhima (*Tibjān*, p. 31 r. 4–5) is a literal translation of *al-Insān al-kāmil*, ed. Cairo 1293, II, p. 81 r. 33–82 r. 1.
3. **The *Maʿrifat al-madhāhib*** — a popular schematic overview of the seventy-two sects, with the six groups of the *Tibjān* in the same order, each in twelves, widely diffused in Arabic and Persian manuscripts (sometimes attributed to [[Abu Hanifa|Abū Ḥanīfa]]), of which Voorhoeve identified nine Arabic and five Persian exemplars. The most striking witness is Leiden Cod. Or. 1255 f. 3v–15v, a Moluccan manuscript with interlinear Malay translation — written on paper watermarked with the arms of Thann in Alsace (in use from [[1612]]), and bearing on f. 1v the note that [[Pulo Ai]] was destroyed on Monday 3 Rabīʿ I 1023 / [[14 April]] [[1614]]. The Moluccan manuscript agrees with the *Tibjān* not only in the names of the sects but in the dogmas attributed to them — including, in the case of the *Khaufiyya* of the Jabriyya, the bizarre doctrine that "there are two gods, one on land and one at sea," which Voorhoeve finds in no other witness.
4. **The *Kitāb fī bayān madhāhib al-mutaṣawwifa* of [[Najm ad-Din an-Nasafi|Najm ad-Dīn ʿUmar an-Nasafī]]** — a small tract of seven folios in a manuscript at Rome (Vatican, Borg. Arab. 254, f. 56), from which Raniri took the first eleven of his thirteen heretical Ṣūfī sects in the same order. The Persian verse of the *Wākiʿiyya* quoted in the *Tibjān* is found in this same tract.

## Manuscripts and apparatus criticus

The facsimile in the Voorhoeve edition is taken from the only complete manuscript, Leiden Cod. Or. 3291 from the legacy of [[Herman Neubronner van der Tuuk|Van der Tuuk]] — here designated **B** — a not-very-careful copy, probably made about [[1855]] for Van der Tuuk from a good older exemplar that came from [[Acheh]]. Its Achehnese provenance is betrayed, Voorhoeve notes, by such forms as *sidjuk* (Ach. *sidjueʾ*) for *sedjuk*, and by the consistent replacement of *kesembilan* with *kesemilan* (cf. Ach. *seumah*, Mal. *sembah*). Voorhoeve collated B with three other manuscripts:

- **A** — Koninklijk Instituut voor de Tropen, Amsterdam, Anthropol. Afd. no. 0/271, catalogued by [[Ph. S. van Ronkel|Van Ronkel]] in *Meded. Afd. Volkenkunde* no. 7 (1935), p. 158; on paper watermarked Leveratto (Churchill no. 573: 1794); a slightly more careful copy ending at p. 105 r. 7 of B.
- **C** — from the collection of [[William Marsden]], now in the School of Oriental and African Studies, London, no. 12210 (microfilm at Leiden Or. A. 18); probably the oldest manuscript, more closely related to B than to A; ends at p. 92 r. 14 of B; the later pages added by a later hand; the Arabic fully vocalised, unlike A and B; carries Achehnese marginal glosses.
- **D** — the Achehnese manuscript of the Koninklijk Instituut voor Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde at The Hague, mentioned by Van Ronkel in *BKI* 103, p. 599. The author of this adaptation, [[Teungku Haron|Teungku Harõn]], composed about [[1929]] — using a manuscript of the *Tibjān* — an Achehnese treatise on the history of Islam in Acheh. An extensive summary, almost a translation, was made by **H. T. Damsté**. With the help of this reliable guide it was possible to recover the passages of the *Tibjān* in Achehnese; but the Achehnese author did not carry his *Tibjān* beyond the first sect of the Jabriyya (p. 57 of the Voorhoeve text). He did not accept the text uncritically: he used, among other things, the *al-Farq baina al-firaq* of ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Baghdādī (probably in the printed edition) to correct corrupt names of sects.

For the section in which Raniri refutes the *[[Kitab al-Muntahi|Kitāb al-Muttahī]]* of [[Hamzah Pansuri]], Voorhoeve also collated: **F** — the Malay text (Cod. Or. 7291(3)); **G** — a fragment of the same text (Cod. Or. 1952); **J** — the Javanese translation (Cod. Or. 5716(2) and 7736(1)).

## Significance

The *Tibjān* is, after the [[Sirat al-Mustaqim|*Ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*]] and alongside the [[Bustan al-Salatin|*Bustan al-Salāṭīn*]], one of the foundational works of Malay scholastic prose. It preserves, in its polemic, the only extended quotations from the [[Kitab al-Muntahi|*Kitāb al-Muntahī*]] of [[Hamzah Pansuri]] and the [[Mir'at al-muhakkikin|*Mirʾāt al-muḥaqqiqīn*]] of [[Shaikh Shamsuddin of Sumatra|Shamsuddin of Pasai]] — works whose own manuscripts have survived only in fragments. Its catalog of sects is the chief vehicle by which the scholastic classification of Muslim heresies was transmitted to the Malay-Indonesian world. And it is the principal witness to the religious and intellectual constellation of [[Acheh]] in the years [[1636]]–[[1641]]: a court in transition from the [[Wujudiyyah]] mysticism of the age of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskandar Muda]] to the orthodox reaction of [[Iskandar Thani]] and [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Taj al-Ālam]], a transition in which Raniri himself was the principal actor.
