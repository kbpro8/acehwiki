The **Holy War against the Dutch** (*jihad* or *prang sabi*, "war in the path of God") was the religiously organised phase of Achehnese resistance to the Dutch invasion that began in [[1873]]. While the early years of the [[Achehnese War|Achehnese-Dutch war]] were conducted largely by the sultan's officials and the uleebalangs, the so-called Dutch policy of concentration — by which the colonial forces withdrew behind a fixed line of forts and left the interior to its own devices — opened the way for a new leadership drawn from the ulamas. Snouck Hurgronje marks this point as the beginning of the period of the ulamas: hitherto they had remained somewhat in the background, henceforward they became the real leaders of the war.

The teaching of Islam in regard to the holy war, as it had moulded itself by degrees in Mohammedan jurisprudence, comprised a twofold obligation: a joint and several obligation of the community at large to spread the faith by force of arms at the bidding of the Chief of the Believers, and an individual obligation incumbent on each believer when the infidel actually attacked the territory of Islam. In Acheh, Snouck Hurgronje observes, real use was made of the fanatical doctrine of the jihad, which was readily exaggerated to the detriment of the kafirs, while those gentler tenets which the "believer" found too difficult of attainment were simply set on one side. The doctrine found a strong support both in the character of the people and in the most popular ingredients of their creed.

The pre-eminent leader of this religious war was [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] (Muhamat Amin) of [[Tiro]] in [[Pidie]], who from the time of the concentration until his death in December [[1885]] directed the jihad in his sphere of action with almost unchallenged authority. As the representative of religious law he could assume full powers, and none dared show him open hostility. Each energetic ulama travelled to and fro in the sphere where he possessed or hoped to acquire influence, proclaiming with renewed vehemence the principles of the holy war, repairing ruined mosques, and ensuring that religious worship was held in greater regularity than before. Teungku Tiro knew that money is the life and soul even of the holy war, and pushed on with the utmost zeal the collections of the *hui sabi* (*haqq sabil*), the "way of God" share of the [[Jakeuet (zakat)|jakeuet]] or zakat that was now the mainstay of the ulama party.

The popular enthusiasm kindled by this teaching showed itself most terribly in the *ateuë* attacks, in which youths inflamed to fanaticism threw themselves, practically unarmed, upon the bayonets of the Dutch troops in the certainty of martyrdom and the boundless recompense hereafter awaiting the martyr to his creed. The leaders of the bands of volunteers, however, fell short of the conditions which must be fulfilled by commanders in the holy war; and on this ground the ulamas claimed for themselves the duty of regulating the conduct of the jihad. Where reverses were met with, the representatives of religion were ever ready with their explanation: "He that will carry on a holy war with assurance of victory must begin," said they, "by turning from all his iniquities." Among Teungku Tiro's cooperators were ulamas of foreign extraction such as [[Habib Abdurrahman|the Habib]], who came to Great Acheh to take part in the direction of the jihad, especially in the [[XXVI Mukims]], and gathered in the *ka sadi* and other contributions to meet the expenses of the war.

## Additional account from *De Islaam in Indonesië* ([[1947]]) — the zakaat war-chest and the Haddji title

[[R. L. Mellema|Mellema]], in his comparative ethnological survey *[[De Islaam in Indonesië (Mellema, 1947)|De Islaam in Indonesië (in het bijzonder op Java)]]* (Amsterdam, [[1947]]), records the fiscal-military base of the holy war from the canonical-institutional side, framing it as a diversion of the *zakaat* (the alms-tax, the third pillar of the Muslim law) by the *[[oelama|oelama]]*'s of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — whom he glosses as "godsdienstleiders (eigenlijk: wetgeleerden)" (religious leaders, properly legal scholars) — into a *particuliere krijgskas* (private war-chest) during the [[Aceh War|Atjehoorlog]]. The canonical category underlying the diversion is the *ḥaqq al-sabīl* — the share of the zakaat assigned to "those who fight in the path of God" — which is one of the eight canonical heads of distribution of the alms-tax. Mellema compares the Atjehnese volunteers funded by this canonical category to the underground resistance workers in the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] during the German occupation — a comparison that, in a work published in [[1947]], carries the immediate force of a recent and lived analogy. The account is the Mellema-source counterpart to the Snouckian narrative of [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]]'s *lui sabi* collections given above, and it is treated at length in [[Oelama zakaat war-chest in the Atjehoorlog (Mellema 1947)]] and in [[Sabil Contributions|sabil contributions]].

Mellema further records that the ascendancy of the oelama's over the official chiefs — the *officiele hoofden*, i.e. the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]]s — was reinforced when the oelama could in addition assume the title of *Haddji*, on the ground that he had accomplished the pilgrimage to [[Mecca]]. "Wanneer zij dan daarenboven nog de titel van Haddji konden aannemen," Mellema writes, "kon dit slechts het aanzien, dat zij in de ogen van het volk genoten, verhogen" — when, in addition, they could assume the title of Haddji, this could only enhance the prestige they enjoyed in the eyes of the people. The Haddji title is thus, in Mellema's account, the religious-cultural complement to the fiscal-military base supplied by the zakaat: the one gave the oelama the material means to pay a following, the other gave him the charismatic authority to command one. Together, the two made the oelama's the effective rivals of the uleebalangs in wartime Atjeh — a rivalry that would, after the [[Japanese invasion of Atjeh|Japanese surrender]] of [[August 1945|August]] [[1945]], culminate in the [[ulama-uleebalang social revolution in Atjeh (1945-1946)|social revolution in Atjeh]] of [[1945]]–[[1946]]. The Haddji title's prestige is treated at length in [[Haddji title prestige in Atjeh (Mellema 1947)]].
