The **Adat Meukuta Alam** — also known as the *Makota Alam* or *Adat Kanun* — is the body of written ordinances ascribed to Sultan [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Meukuta Alam]] (Iskandar Muda, reigned [[1607]]–[[1636]]), and regarded by the Achehnese as the chief monument of royal legislation in their history. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] explains, the sultan "was himself called Meukuta Alam after his death, and his ordinances are thus known as Adat Meukuta Alam." Together with the [[Sarakata|sarakatas]] of later sultans, they constitute the only attempts at centralisation of authority or reform — social, political, or religious — that can be discerned in the whole course of Achehnese history.

The Adat Meukuta Alam is preserved, along with other Achehnese sarakatas, in manuscript documents of varying age and authority, and was published in part by [[K. F. H. van Langen|Van Langen]] in his *Atjehsch Staatsbestuur* (1888). They contain, besides ceremonial of court and festival and the collection and disposal of taxes on imports, certain regulations touching the court of the sultan and maritime commerce. The new elements which they introduce may be classified, in Hurgronje's summary, under three heads: first, attempts at an extension of the authority of the Sultan by allotting to him — the "king of the port" — a certain control over the succession of the other chieftains of the land, over the disputes of these chiefs with one another or those between the subjects of different chiefs, and over the interests of strangers; second, certain rules intended to bring about a stricter observance of Mohammedan law; and third, regulations dealing with trade then confined to the capital, the shares of certain officials established in the capital in the profits drawn from this trade by the king of the port, the court ceremonial, and the celebration of great religious festivals.

The practical fate of these ordinances was very limited. The rules dealing with trade and ceremonial exercised, during the reign of their promulgator at least, some degree of authority over life and trade in the seaport town. Of the other innovations, however, "hardly a single one has passed from document into actuality, but simply the state of things they reveal as already in being has continued its existence." Perpetual dynastic struggles leading to the death or downfall of the rulers, the unstable character of the rulers themselves, the endeavours of chiefs and officials even in the capital to promote their own authority and profit, the want of a proper machinery of government based on other principles than "might is right" — all this ensures that no period can be pointed out in which even the regulations affecting the capital passed current as the living law of the land.

The Adat Meukuta Alam has nonetheless exercised a deep influence on the Achehnese political imagination, since "the general tendency is to refer all that has become customary law to the Sultans in general (*adat pôteu meureuhom*) and to Meukuta Alam in particular." How little reliance can be placed on Achehnese data in respect to the origin of such written laws may be seen from the mention of the tomb of [[Teungku Anjong]] (died [[1782]]) in the so-called laws of Meukuta Alam — a sultan who, as Hurgronje drily notes, "was not even born in the reign of Meukuta Alam († 1636)." The ascription of all customary law to a single name has thus become a kind of constitutional myth: the Adat Meukuta Alam is less a code than a fiction of legitimacy, by which the Achehnese explain to themselves the existence of the customs under which they live. The same process may be observed in other Malayan countries, where the *Hukum Kanun* of Malacca and similar codes "are expressive not of decay, but of a temporary desire for order and reform. Of these the same holds true as of the Adat Meukuta Alam in Acheh."
