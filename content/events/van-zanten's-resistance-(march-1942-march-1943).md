**Van Zanten's resistance** — the post-[[Sumatra capitulation (28 March 1942)|capitulation]] Dutch guerrilla conducted by Lt [[H. van Zanten|lieutenant H. van Zanten]] and a small force of European volunteers and released Maréchaussee from a *bosbivak* north-east of [[Takengon|Takengon]] from [[March 1942|March 1942]] until Van Zanten's capture on [[10 March]] [[10 March 1943|10 March 1943]] — was the longest sustained Dutch resistance to the Japanese in occupied [[Atjeh|Atjeh]]. In the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), the Van Zanten resistance is the principal narrative of the *Bijzondere verrichtingen na de capitulatie* and the staff history's exemplar of the Dutch officer's duty to continue resistance after a formal surrender.

## The Redelong base (March – November 1942)

When [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|majoor Palmer van den Broek]] ordered the capitulation of the Takengon garrison on [[19 March]] [[19 March 1942|19 March 1942]], the *theeonderneming Redelong* — recently vacated as a stafkwartier by the Dutch troops — still contained a handful of officers, Van Zanten among them. Although his older colleagues "probeererden hem hiervan terug te houden," Van Zanten "zette evenwel door" and announced he would hold out "zo lang mogelijk." Released indigenous militairen and a number of European volunteers joined him, bringing his strength eventually to about 70 men with 55 karabijnen.

Van Zanten established a *bosbivak* "enige tientallen kilometers ten noordoosten van Takengon," with the intention of attacking the Japanese in the rear should an Allied landing take place. To acquire intelligence he detached soldiers in civilian clothes to the *theeonderneming Redelong* and to Takengon, where clandestine radios were available; he also made contact with the *ondergrondse actie* that had been set up in [[Medan|Medan]]. He obtained much help from the *administrateur* of Redelong, [[S. A. Liezenberg|S. A. Liezenberg]], and from the *geneesheer* of the Takengon hospital, with whom he corresponded via an Ambonese sergeant named **Kalatu**. The chain — Kalatu, then the *vrouw van een Menadonees militair* who worked in the Dutch hospital — was eventually detected by the Japanese.

## The Japanese counter-operation (November 1942 – March 1943)

The Japanese began to take the Van Zanten group seriously in November [[1942]]. On [[24 November]] [[24 November 1942|24 November 1942]] they arrested fourteen released militairen working on the *theeonderneming*, "vastgebonden, waarna zij aan de bekende Japanse mishandelingen werden onderworpen." On [[26 November]] [[26 November 1942|26 November 1942]] a Japanese force of about 100 men attempted to take Van Zanten's bivak but was foiled by an informer. The bivak was burned. From this point Van Zanten was "voortdurend achtervolgd" and was forced to change hiding-place constantly; the supply of food and medicines from Redelong was cut off.

The European sergeant **Andringa** and two Maréchaussees were captured in February [[1943]] "ten gevolge van ziekte en uitputting." The staff history records the gradual collapse of the group's logistical and intelligence position through the early months of [[1943]].

## Capture, court-martial and execution (10 March 1943)

On [[10 March]] [[10 March 1943|10 March 1943]] Van Zanten and the five Maréchaussees remaining with him were captured when they emerged from the forest to *fourageren*. The staff history records that they were "op beestachtige wijze mishandeld en verhoord," with the Japanese repeatedly offering the lieutenant *lijfsbehoud* if he would act as their spy. He refused. He was eventually transferred to [[Fort de Rock|Fort de Rock]] in [[Medan|Medan]], where a *zogenaamde krijgsraad* condemned him to death.

The compilers record his bearing: "Luitenant Van Zanten bleef tot het laatste toe, ondanks alle vernederingen en mishandelingen, waardig en fier, niettegenstaande hij zeer goed begreep geëxecuteerd te zullen worden." The date and place of execution are not given in the staff history; the entry closes the Van Zanten episode.

## Significance

The Van Zanten resistance is, in the staff history's Atjeh chapter, the only post-capitulation Dutch guerrilla treated at length, and it is presented as the moral counter-weight to the mass surrenders of March [[1942]]. The compilers' emphasis on Van Zanten's "waardig en fier" bearing in captivity, and on his repeated refusal of the Japanese offer of *lijfsbehoud* in exchange for espionage, frames him as the staff history's exemplar of the Dutch officer's duty to continue resistance after capitulation — even where the resistance was, by the staff history's own account, hopeless. The episode also closes the Atjeh chapter's narrative of the *Jappentijd*: with Van Zanten's execution, the last organised Dutch military presence in Atjeh was extinguished.
