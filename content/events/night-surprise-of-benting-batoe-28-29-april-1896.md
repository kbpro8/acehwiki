**The Night Surprise of Benting Batoe (28–29 April 1896)** was the night assault by a [[Dutch]] detachment under [[1e Luit. M. Neelmeijer|1e Luit. M. Neelmeijer]] on the Achehnese fort of [[Batoe]] — a fortified work near [[Toengkoeb]] approximately 400 m west of the crossroads, held by the *bendehoofd* [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]] — in which Sergeant [[Panman]] (No. 21620) opened the first gate of the outwork from the inside and Sergeant [[Vogel]] (No. 22210) opened the second gate and killed [[Leh-Oe]], [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb|Tjoet Toengkoeb]]'s confidant, in hand-to-hand combat. As described in [[A. Kruisheer|Kruisheer]]'s *ATJEH '96*, the night surprise was one of the most celebrated small actions of the spring of [[1896]] — a textbalso example of Achehnese-style infiltration tactics used against the Achehnese themselves.

## Background

By the last week of [[April]] [[1896]] the [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Regeeringscommissaris]], [[Lt.-Gen. Vetter]], had completed the [[Evacuation of Kroeng-Gloempang, Tjot-Rang, Toengkoeb (20 April 1896)|evacuation of the buitenlinie]] and had issued his [[Proclamation of Vetter (27 April 1896)|proclamation of 27 April]] announcing military operations against [[Teuku Umar]]. The [[Bombardment of Lampisang (27 April – 23 May 1896)|bombardment of Lampisang]] had begun on the same day. The Dutch were on the offensive, but the Achehnese were not passive: harassment of the consolidated line continued, and outlying Achehnese *bentings* — including the *benting* at [[Batoe]] near [[Toengkoeb]] — maintained their pressure on the southern approaches to the Dutch position.

The *benting* at [[Batoe]] was a substantial work. Held by the *bendehoofd* [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]] — one of the principal Achehnese war-leaders in the southern mukims — it occupied a position approximately 400 m west of the crossroads at [[Toengkoeb]], commanding the road to the south. Its construction was the standard Achehnese pattern: an earthen wall pierced for rifle-fire, surrounded by a *bamboedoeri* abatis, with a outwork protecting the main gate. The garrison was substantial, the works were strong, and a frontal assault would have been costly. The Dutch command resolved, instead, on a night surprise.

## The Plan and the Approach

The detachment was placed under the command of [[1e Luit. M. Neelmeijer|1e Luit. M. Neelmeijer]] — the same officer who had brought the first report of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]] to [[Lamkoenjit]] on [[30 March]] [[1896]] around 7:30 p.m. Neelmeijer's plan was audacious: a small party would approach the *benting* at night, infiltrate the *voorwerk*, open the gates from the inside, and admit the main assault party. The plan required individual courage of the highest order: the men who opened the gates would be alone, in the dark, inside an enemy work, with no possibility of reinforcement until the gates were opened.

The detachment approached [[Batoe]] under cover of darkness on the night of [[28 April]]–[[29 April]] [[1896]]. The approach was made in silence, the men moving across the open ground in single file. The Achehnese sentinels — if any were posted — failed to detect the approach. The detachment reached the *voorwerk* without alarm.

## The Storming

The first critical act fell to Sergeant [[Panman]] (No. 21620). Panman, working his way to the gate of the *voorwerk*, succeeded in opening it from the inside — whether by scaling the wall and unbaring the gate, or by some other means, Kruisheer does not specify. The *voorwerk* was entered. The second critical act fell to Sergeant [[Vogel]] (No. 22210). Vogel, advancing to the second gate — that of the main *benting* — succeeded in opening it. In the course of opening the gate he encountered [[Leh-Oe]], the confidant of [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]], whom he killed in hand-to-hand combat. The gates were now open, and the main assault party — waiting in the darkness outside — poured into the *benting*.

The garrison, taken completely by surprise, was driven out of the work after a brief and confused fight in the darkness. The *benting* was occupied. The capture of [[Batoe]] — achieved at negligible cost and by individual heroism rather than by frontal assault — was a sharp reverse for [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]], who lost his confidant [[Leh-Oe]] and his fortified base. The Achehnese *bendehoofd* himself escaped, but his prestige was dented; he would be heard of again, ultimately withdrawing to [[Lam-Tobah]] after the [[Ladoeng Landing and Glé Broeë Attack (22–23 June 1896)|Ladoeng landing and Glé Broeë attack]] of [[22 June]]–[[23 June]] [[1896]].

## Significance

The Night Surprise of Benting Batoe on [[28 April]]–[[29 April]] [[1896]] was, in tactical terms, a model operation: a small detachment, a bold plan, individual heroism, and a complete success at negligible cost. The actions of Sergeant [[Panman]] and Sergeant [[Vogel]] were celebrated in the Dutch colonial press and were held up as examples of the quality of the European NCO — the *onderofficier* — who, as the [[Siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe (28–29 March 1896)|siege of Aloer Oe]] had already shown, was the backbone of the Dutch army in Atjeh. In strategic terms the night surprise was a minor operation, but it served to keep the Achehnese off-balance in the southern mukims during the preparatory phase of the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive]], and it denied [[T. Tjoet Toengkoeb]] the use of an important fortified base. The operation also demonstrated, in its way, that the Dutch could match the Achehnese at their own game of infiltration and night attack — a lesson that would be repeated, on a larger scale, in the [[Ladoeng Landing and Glé Broeë Attack (22–23 June 1896)|Ladoeng landing]] of [[22 June]]–[[23 June]] [[1896]].
