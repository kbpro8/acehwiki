The **pigskin parchment letter incident at Atjeh of 1600** was a diplomatic fiasco during the visit of [[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]]'s fleet to [[Acheh|Atjeh]] at the end of [[1600]]. At the solemn reception of the Sultan by Van Caerden, who was to deliver gifts and a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]], a Portuguese monk present at the court pointed out to the Sultan that the letter — written on parchment made from the skin of the unclean pig — could not lawfully be touched by a Mohammedan. "Zoo miste's Prinsen brief zijn doel" — So the Prince's letter missed its mark. The incident is the source of the chapter-title of chapter XXXII of [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s *Tijdens de O.I. Compagnie*: "Het Kasteel van Verre. De brief op varkensvel in Atjeh."

## Background: the second Amsterdam fleet, 1599

At the end of [[1599]] two Amsterdam fleets sailed out, independently of each other: one under [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] with Van Caerden as Vice-Admiral, the other under [[Jacob Wilkens|Jacob Wilkens]] with [[Cornelis van Heemskerck|Cornelis van Heemskerck]] as his second. Both founded a factory at [[Bantam]]. Van Caerden then visited Sumatra's Westkust (West Coast) and, at the end of [[1600]], Atjeh.

## The audience and the Portuguese monk

At the solemn reception of the Sultan [[Sultan Aloeddin of Atjeh|Aloeddin]] by Van Caerden, who was to deliver gifts and a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]], a Portuguese monk was present. The monk drew attention to the fact that the letter was written on parchment made from the skin of the unclean pig — "het vel van het onreine varken" — which a Mohammedan might not lawfully touch.

The intervention was a classic act of Iberian diplomatic sabotage: the Portuguese, hostile to the Dutch intrusion in the Asian trade, used their familiarity with Islamic law to turn the Sultan's religious scruples against the Dutch embassy. The Sultan, naturally, could not accept the letter. "Zoo miste's Prinsen brief zijn doel."

## The De Houtman warning

The pepper trade likewise did not go smoothly. [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] — long a prisoner in Atjeh since the [[Death of Cornelis de Houtman at Atjeh (1599)|disaster of 1599]] — came aboard Van Caerden's ship to warn him of an impending attack. He then returned to shore, hoping to obtain the release of his fellow-prisoners, to deliver a cargo to Van Caerden, and to negotiate a contract between the Sultan and the Dutch. All in vain — De Houtman remained a prisoner.

## Van Caerden's reckless conduct

Van Caerden's expedition was further disfigured "door roekeloos optreden tegenover de inlanders" — by reckless behaviour towards the natives — in particular over the recovery of an advance paid to the Sultan. His conduct, Godee Molsbergen writes, made "den Hollandschen naam in den Archipel gehaat" — the Dutch name hated in the Archipelago.

The consequences were concrete. Neutral Moors (*Mooren*) whom Van Caerden had wronged obtained, after a lawsuit, 50,000 *realen* in damages — a staggering sum. The case set a precedent the Dutch would later have to live down.

## The 1601 Zeeland ships and the resolution

When the four Zeeland ships returned to Atjeh in August [[1601]] bearing [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]]'s letter (this time in Spanish, and presumably not on pigskin), the Sultan was conciliatory. Van Caerden's clumsy conduct was "naar genoegen met den Sultan besproken" — satisfactorily discussed with the Sultan — and the Sultan sent three ambassadors on the returning ships: [[Abdoel Hamid]], [[Sri Mohammed]] and [[Mir Hassan]]. See [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|Atjeh embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)]].

## Significance

The pigskin parchment letter incident of [[1600]] is the first recorded European diplomatic fiasco at the Atjeh court — a textbook example of how a small material detail (the parchment's origin in pig-skin) could, in the hands of a hostile third party (here a Portuguese monk), defeat an entire embassy. The incident is also a measure of how thin European knowledge of Islamic sensibilities still was at the turn of the seventeenth century: the Dutch had sent a letter from their Prince to a Mohammedan Sultan on the very material most calculated to give offence. The [[Aceh Embassy to Prince Maurits at Grave (1602)|1602 embassy]] that resolved the affair would be far more carefully choreographed.
