**Both lanchang** (literally "setting afloat of a *lanchang*," a small boat or raft) is an Achehnese apotropaic ceremony employed both in cases of individual illness and — formerly, on a grand scale — as a public rite to combat epidemic disease. The custom is described by C. Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* in his chapter on superstitions connected with sickness and disease. A parallel rite exists in the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay peninsula]], where the same word *lanchang* is used in the north for the boat; in the south it is called *raung*.

## The Small Lanchang for Individual Illness

Lanchangs on a small scale are set afloat in case of individual illnesses. The process is as follows. A rectangular piece is cut from a betelnut spathe, folded and stitched tight on three sides, and kept open by means of a cross-piece attached to its upper edges, so that it roughly resembles a boat; a sort of mast is also fixed in it. In this little vessel are placed various sweetmeats (*peugajah*), sirih, a live hen, two raw eggs, boiled glutinous rice, flowers, and a piece of white cotton. It is then taken to the river or to the padi-fields — in which last case it is deposited at a spot where three or four ways meet (*seumpang lhe'è* or *peunèt*). All this is carried out under the orders of a woman skilled in medicinal art (*inòng ulèë*) who herself conveys the lanchang to its destination.

She first causes the patient to eat some of the sweetmeats; the other objects are moved backwards and forwards over the patient's head with solemn reckoning ending in a long-drawn *tujōh bōlih!* ("seven!"), and then placed in the boat. The *bōlih* cry — "seven" being a number of peculiar ritual significance — punctuates the act of separation, by which the illness is symbolically loaded into the boat and sent away.

## The Great Lanchang against Epidemics

Another preventive of epidemics, employed "so to speak officially" in Acheh in earlier times, was the *both lanchang* on a grand scale. When an epidemic prevailed, the [[Sultan of Acheh|Sultan]] would command a great *lanchang* built of wood or bamboo and furnished with a quantity of rice and meat. A pretended human sacrifice to the sea was also made: some poor man who was willing to play the part of victim for a few dollars was placed on board the boat and set off to drift down the [[Aceh River|Acheh river]] and out of the *kuala* (the river-mouth). At the river's mouth he would manage to regain the shore, leaving the rice and meat to their strife with the waves. The *bantu* or evil spirit which caused the epidemic was supposed to sail away in the boat, taking the pestilence with it.

## Place in Achehnese Folk Religion

The *both lanchang* belongs to that stratum of Achehnese folk religion in which Islamic formulae overlay an older substratum of animistic and Hindu-Javanese practice. It stands alongside other curious methods of preventing and resisting epidemics: on [[Pulo Breueh|Pulo Breueh]] (Bras) an old *karieng*-tree — the "Ja Karieng" — was believed to have the power of enchaining epidemics and letting them loose upon the people at will. Once a year the inhabitants of the island held there a great *[[kanduri]]*, offering up flowers and incense, and taking sea-baths close by; were this festival not observed, it was thought that the coming year would surely be marked by pestilence.
