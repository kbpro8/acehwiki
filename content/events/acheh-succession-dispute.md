The **Acheh succession dispute of 1802–1819** — referred to by Van der Kemp as the *burgerkrijg in Atjeh* — was the civil war in the sultanate of [[Acheh]] that began with the accession of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] in [[1802]] and continued, with fluctuating fortunes, until the conclusion of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] of 22 April 1819. It set the recognised sultan [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] (Johor Alum) against the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Saif ul-Alum]] (Syf), son of the Penang-based merchant [[Said Hussein|Said Hussein]], and drew in the [[Three Sagis]] of the [[XXII Mukims|XXII]], [[XXV Mukims|XXV]] and [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]] at the capital, the powerful coastal chief [[Tunku Pakih|Tunku Pakih of Pedir]], and ultimately the British governments of [[Penang]] and [[Bengal|Bengal]].

## Origins

Since 1802 [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] had reigned "in so far as circumstances permitted" (Van der Kemp). As always in Acheh, the sultan confronted a powerful opposition party. After a ten-year reign, that party judged itself strong enough to seek a replacement, and fixed its eye upon a certain [[Said Hussein|Said Hussein]] — a remote kinsman of the royal house, but more importantly a very rich merchant residing at [[Penang]]. Hussein declined the throne for himself, pleading age, but accepted it on behalf of his second son [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]], who had hitherto served as boatswain on his father's proas. To win the throne, an expedition was fitted out at [[Penang]].

## The Hussein Expedition and the Two Sultans

In mid-1815 [[Said Hussein|Hussein]] and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] sailed for Acheh (see [[Said Hussein's expedition to Acheh (1815)]]), and Syf was there proclaimed sultan as *Sil-ul-illum Shak* by the assembled chiefs. Among his supporters was the relatively powerful potentate of [[Pedir]], [[Tunku Pakih|Tunku Pakie]], who had contributed much to the success of the revolution. Before long, however, [[Tunku Pakih|Packie]] fell out with [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] and reverted to the party of the dispossessed [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]], whose seat he established at [[Pedir]]. For [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] Acheh itself soon became too hot; he too withdrew to the east coast, fixing his residence at [[Telok Semawé]], between [[Pedir]] and [[Penang]]. The capital [[Banda Acheh]] fell under the de facto rule of the federation of the [[Three Sagis]].

There were thus two sultans — [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]] (referred to in the documents as "the old sultan," and dismissively as the former "boatswain" by the Penang party), and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé]] — while at [[Banda Acheh]] itself the [[Three Sagis]] held effective power. The civil war continued for several years with such varying fortune that at length the government of [[Penang]] did not know which of the two pretenders should be recognised.

## Earlier British Contacts

The dispute unfolded against a background of intermittent British contact with Acheh. In [[1786]] the [[Sultan of Acheh's letter to Warren Hastings|insulting letter of Sultan Alauddin Muhammad Shah]] to Governor-General [[Warren Hastings|Warren Hastings]] had shown how little respect the Achenese court had for British power. In [[1814]] [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh (1814)|Captain Canning's visit]] to Acheh on behalf of the Calcutta government had met with a highly insulting reception from [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] and his court, which deterred the [[Penang]] authorities from any active intervention in favour of the legitimate sultan. In late 1817 or early 1818 [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|Captain Coombs's first mission]] was sent to Acheh to identify the rightful sultan; the chiefs declared that they had forever abjured [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] and recognised [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] as their sultan. [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] left Acheh in February 1818 convinced that [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] was the recognised ruler but that the British would have to support him with a thousand men to enable him to maintain his authority.

## The British Commission

It was the conflict between [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] over which pretender to recognise — and [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s alarm at rumours of Dutch overtures to [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]] — that led to the dispatch of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs commission]] of 1819. After seven unproductive weeks at the roadstead of [[Banda Acheh]], during which [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] of the [[XXII Mukims]] made plain his "undisguised supremacy at the capital" and treated both pretenders with contempt, the commission proceeded to [[Pedir]] and concluded the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]]. [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé]] was induced to renounce his claims — a submission that proved worthless, for in July 1819 he was again writing to the governor of [[Penang]] as sultan of Acheh and continuing his "predatory life" unpunished.

## Aftermath

The 1819 treaty did not end the dispute; it merely overlaid it. In April 1822 [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] was still at [[Pedir]], writing to [[Penang]] that he lacked the means to move to the ruined capital. The [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|letter of the Three Sagis to Governor Phillips of 4 December 1822]] declared that they would have no one but [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]]. The death of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] in December 1823 left the matter still unsettled. [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]]'s [[Panglima Polim's supremacy at Acheh|supremacy at Acheh]] continued, and the question of who was the rightful sultan was overtaken by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]] and the resulting paralysis of Dutch policy on Sumatra.
