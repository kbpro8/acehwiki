**Raffles' detour to the [[Karimon Islands]]** in January [[1819]] was the unauthorised diversion of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] from the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]], then preparing at [[Penang]] for its mission to [[Acheh]], to inspect the [[Karimon Islands]] with [[Major William Farquhar|Major Farquhar]] of [[Malacca]]. The episode is examined by Van der Kemp because it led, indirectly, to the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]] and because the high-handed manner in which it was executed typifies the difficulties that plagued the commission from its inception and that would culminate in the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] and the [[Paper war|paper war]] between Raffles and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]].

## Background

The [[Karimon Islands]] had long attracted British attention as a possible site for a settlement commanding the southern entrance to the [[Straits of Malacca|Straits of Malacca]]. [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] had formed a favourable opinion of them during his recent voyages to [[Pontianak]] (where he had been rebuffed by [[Van Boekholz|Van Boekholz]]) and to [[Lingga]] (where he had concluded a treaty subsequently set aside by [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]]). The Dutch were aware of these British designs: as the Dutch governor [[Timmerman Thyssen|Timmerman Thyssen]] wrote to [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]] on 11 November 1818, after the surrender of [[Malacca]], "the Major still hopes to take possession of the Karimon islands; at Pulo Pinang it seems to be a fixed plan to take possession of them and of the kingdom of Acheen on Sumatra."

## The Detour

On the afternoon of 19 January 1819 [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] wrote from [[Malacca]] to [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]], then at [[Penang]] preparing for the Acheh mission, to propose a joint inspection of the Karimons. [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] judged the invitation of such importance that he not only postponed the entire Acheh journey — which, he had insisted, could not be delayed — but actually sailed without taking leave of either Governor [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] or his co-commissioner [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]. Worse, he took with him the artillery detachment that [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] believed was destined for the Acheh expedition, and left behind only a written notification for both gentlemen. The Bengal government's later report to London of 7 October 1820 described the proceeding as "contrary to the spirit of our instructions; and independently of that consideration was obviously improper without previous communication with his colleague."

## The Dispatch of the Minto

Once at sea [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] reflected ("It occurred to me for the first time") that the Dutch might profit by his absence to conclude their suspected treaty with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]]. Without consulting either [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] or [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]], he therefore ordered the Company's ship *[[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir|Minto]]* under [[Lieutenant Criddle|Lieutenant Criddle]] to make for Pedir and inform the sultan of the commission's approach. [[Lieutenant Criddle|Criddle]] dutifully called upon [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] at Pedir, communicated the message that the commission would arrive within three or four weeks, and received the sultan's reply that the Dutch had made him no overtures. By this device [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] had his way after all: the commission, though not actually visiting Pedir first, had notified [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] in advance.

## Aftermath and the Founding of Singapore

[[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] returned to [[Penang]] on 16 February 1819 after an absence of only a fortnight, having — as Van der Kemp concedes — laid the foundation of an important settlement and "an anti-Batavia." The Dutch at [[Malacca]] meanwhile feared he was preparing a new attack on the [[Karimon Islands]]. [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] protested to the Bengal government against his arbitrary interruption of the Acheh commission and his unauthorised dispatch of the *Minto*; [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] replied in a lengthy defence addressed to the government secretary at Calcutta on 30 September 1819, included by Van der Kemp as Appendix 5.

The diversion established a pattern that would recur throughout the commission: [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] acting on his own authority, his colleagues protesting, and the Bengal government ultimately siding — though with reservations — with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]. It was the same pattern that would lead to the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] being concluded with [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] rather than [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]], and the same disposition towards unilateral action that had produced — and would continue to produce — friction with the Dutch throughout the archipelago. The [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]] was, in this sense, an incidental gain from an undertaking whose formal purpose had been quite different.
