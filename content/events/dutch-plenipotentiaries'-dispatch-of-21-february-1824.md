The **Dutch plenipotentiaries' dispatch of [[21 February]] [[1824]]** — referred to by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] as the *Londensche depêche* of 21 February 1824 — was the despatch sent by the Dutch plenipotentiaries [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] and [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] from London to the Netherlands government reporting the [[Raising of the Acheh treaty in London negotiations|sudden raising by the British plenipotentiaries]] of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 British-Acheh convention]] concluded by [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]. The dispatch recorded that, on the reading of the convention, the parties had "mutually recognised" it as incompatible with the new state of affairs and as not susceptible of express maintenance; reported the British concern that, should the sultans of [[Acheh]] be left to themselves, the United States of America would seek to establish themselves there; and suggested that the "readiest means" of averting this evil was for the Netherlands to stipulate with the Achehnese that they would never admit any establishment of a foreign power. The dispatch is printed by Van der Kemp from the *Bijdragen* of Elout (1863).

## Background

By mid-February [[1824]] the Dutch plenipotentiaries had reached agreement with the British on a [[First draft of 1824 Anglo-Dutch treaty|first draft of the treaty]], dated [[1 February]] [[1824]]. The draft contained no provision concerning [[Acheh]]. While [[Captain Elout|Captain Elout]] was on the point of departing for The Hague with the draft, the British plenipotentiaries [[George Canning|Canning]] and [[Charles Watkin Williams Wynn|Wynn]] unexpectedly disclosed the existence of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 Acheh convention]]. Captain Elout was *verbally* instructed to bring this new incident personally to the King's attention and to seek authorisation for the proposed Acheh reservation. The [[King's authorization of 13 February 1824|King's authorisation of 13 February 1824]] was issued on the day after [[Elout's advice of 12 February 1824|Elout's advice of 12 February]].

On Captain Elout's return to London, the British plenipotentiaries "brought the Acheh treaty to the table" on [[23 February]] [[1824]] (see [[Raising of the Acheh treaty in London negotiations]]). The "venerable assembly was, on the reading of it, surprised", for the convention contained article 6, excluding other Europeans and Americans from residence in [[Acheh]] and binding the sultan to make no treaty without British consent — provisions that went far beyond mere hospitality to shipwrecked mariners.

## The dispatch

In their dispatch to The Hague of [[21 February]] [[1824]] (a date that, as Van der Kemp notes, must be a slip for a later date, since the disclosure was on 23 February), the Dutch plenipotentiaries reported as follows:

> "The treaty concluded in 1819 by Sir Stamford Raffles has been read and mutually recognised as incompatible with the new state of affairs, and as not susceptible of express maintenance. But our co-plenipotentiaries then for the first time communicated to us their concern that, should the sultans of Acheh be left to themselves, the United States of America, or earlier, will seek to establish themselves with them, and get a footing for a settlement which, in case of war, would be to the great detriment of British navigation. The readiest means of averting this evil appears to be that the Netherlands undertake to stipulate with those of Acheh that they will never admit any establishment of a foreign power."

The dispatch thus records two things. First, the Dutch plenipotentiaries' recognition that the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] could not be *expressly* maintained under the new treaty. Second, the British concern about American designs on [[Acheh]] — a concern that, as Van der Kemp notes, "might indeed be called no idle talk" given the warning that [[J. C. Baud|J. C. Baud]] had addressed to [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] in his letter of 28 November 1822 about North-American designs on the Philippines. The British suggestion — that the Netherlands stipulate with the Achehnese that they would never admit any foreign establishment — would form the basis of the Dutch declaration of February–March 1824 that was annexed to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]].

## Significance

The dispatch of [[21 February]] [[1824]] is significant in Van der Kemp's analysis for two reasons. First, it records the Dutch plenipotentiaries' explicit recognition that the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] was incompatible with the new treaty — a recognition that would have warranted the *abandonment* of the 1819 convention, rather than its mere modification. Second, it shows that the Dutch plenipotentiaries were prepared to entertain the British concern about American designs, and to propose a Dutch guarantee against foreign establishments in [[Acheh]] — a guarantee that went well beyond the King's verbal authorisation of [[13 February]]. The dispatch thus marks the moment at which the Dutch plenipotentiaries began to drift from their instructions, and to accept obligations that the Dutch government had not authorised.

The follow-up [[Dutch plenipotentiaries' dispatch of 24 February 1824|dispatch of 24 February 1824]] would take the next, more dangerous step: proposing to meet the British wishes, not by inserting further articles in the treaty itself, but by a separate nota or declaration.
