**The patchouli-oil industry in Atjeh** is the indigenous processing of patchouli leaves into essential oil, begun at [[Tapa Toean|Tapa Toean]] on the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust van Atjeh]] in [[1921]] with government support and the cooperation of the *Afdeeling Nijverheid* of the *Landbouwconsulent* at [[Buitenzorg|Buitenzorg]], and surveyed in two *Berichten* of the [[Afdeeling Handelsmuseum|Afdeeling Handelsmuseum]] of the [[Koninklijke Vereeniging Koloniaal Instituut|Kon. Vereeniging Koloniaal Instituut]] — *Bericht* No. 21 of May [[1925]] and *Bericht* No. 71 of [[1932]], the latter by [[Ir. W. Spoon|Ir. W. Spoon]] under the title *[[Atjeh Patchouli-Olie (1932)|Atjeh Patchouli-Olie]]*.

## Background and Origin
Before [[1921]] the entire patchouli-leaf harvest of Atjeh had been shipped raw to [[Singapore|Singapore]] and [[Penang|Penang]] for distillation by Chinese-owned stills. The first factory at [[Tapa Toean|Tapa Toean]] was founded in [[1921]] with government support and the cooperation of the *Afdeeling Nijverheid* of the *Landbouwconsulent* at Buitenzorg, with the aim of showing that an oil of at least equal quality to the Singapore product could be distilled in Atjeh itself. The plant — *[[Pogostemon Cablin (patchouli plant)|Pogostemon Cablin Benth.]]* — is called *nilam* on Sumatra and *dilem* on Java. On Sumatra the chief centres of cultivation are on the Westkust at [[Tjalang|Tjalang]], [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]] and Tapa Toean, and on the Oostkust at [[Langsa|Langsa]]. Soil and climate in Atjeh appear naturally more favourable to the plant than on Java, where the oil, though of respectable constants, has *'lang niet zoo intensief'* an odour.

## The Two Berichten
The first *Bericht* (No. 21, May [[1925]]) recorded the earliest attempts at indigenous processing. The second *Bericht* (No. 71, [[1932]]) surveyed the industry's progress over ten years through export statistics and a programme of laboratory analysis on six sample consignments received by the Handelsmuseum in 1931 and early 1932. Spoon's tables show the Atjeh leaf export rising from 1.097 tons in [[1924]] to a peak of 1.375 tons in [[1927]] and falling to 638 tons in [[1931]]. Of the 1931 figure, 9 tons went direct to the Netherlands, 12 tons to Germany, 512 tons to Penang and 105 tons to Singapore — showing that the greater part of the leaf harvest was still being shipped out raw.

## Atjeh Nilam-Oil Export
The Atjeh nilam-oil export, tracked in Spoon's *Tabel III*, rose from 50 kg in [[1924]] to 3.468 kg in [[1931]], while the Java *dilem*-oil export in the same year was 1.865 kg. The destination of the 1931 Atjeh oil exports was: 2.465 kg to Singapore, 855 kg to Penang, 360 kg to Japan, 110 kg to the Netherlands (the first direct shipment to the Netherlands), 841 kg to France, 50 kg to Germany and 38 kg to the United States. By [[1931]] there were patchouli-distilleries near [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]], at [[Meulaboh|Meulaboh]], and two or three at [[Tapa Toean|Tapa Toean]].

## Laboratory Findings
Spoon's six sample oils — numbered 1874, 1882, 1958-1, 1958-2, 1986 and 2076 — were analysed for *soortelijk gewicht* (specific gravity), *brekingsindex* (refractive index), *draaiing* (optical rotation in a 100 mm tube), *zuurgetal* (acid value), *estergetal* (ester value) and solubility in 90 vol.% alcohol. The first two samples were judged impure — *'deze olie was dus onzuiver, er had een bijmenging plaats gevonden'* — but the four later samples gave good constants. To test market reception Spoon sent them to perfume manufacturers in the Netherlands, Germany, England and France. The French house replied that the oil *'correspond aux qualités bonnes normales connues jusqu'à ce jour de provenance de Singapore'*; the German house found the constants identical with those of the Singapore distillates but the odour *'ausdruckslos'*; the English house judged the oil *'definitely much inferior to oil distilled by us from Singapore leaves'*, with a camphoraceous and peppery note absent from its own product, and suggested that the difference lay in the age and dryness of the leaves distilled; the Dutch house found the odour *'niet slecht'* but *'iets kamferachtigs'*.

## Significance
The patchouli-oil industry in Atjeh is the type-case of the post-pacification *volkscultuur* promoted by the [[Koninklijke Vereeniging Koloniaal Instituut|Koloniaal Instituut]] and the colonial government. It demonstrates both the possibilities of indigenous processing of an existing raw-material export and the obstacles — quality-control, market-reception, the competition of the Chinese distilleries of Penang and Singapore — that such an industry faced in the inter-war period.
