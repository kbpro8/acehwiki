The **Cape of Guinea cession of [[1871]]** — in Dutch the *afstand van de Kust van Guinée* — was the transfer by the Kingdom of the Netherlands of its possessions on the [[Cape of Guinea|Cape of Guinea]] (the Dutch Gold Coast, in present-day Ghana) to Great Britain, by a separate convention concluded in the same year as the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra Treaty of 1871]]. Van der Kemp introduces the cession in order to examine — and largely to qualify — the widespread Dutch belief that the surrender of the Cape was the *price* paid by the Netherlands for the British abandonment of the Acheh reservation.

## The Question of Linkage

The Sumatra Treaty of [[1871]] had, by its principal article, released the Netherlands from the British reservation in favour of Achehnese independence that had been attached, almost casually, to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Treaty of 17 March 1824]]. It was widely asserted in the Netherlands — and is asserted, Van der Kemp notes, by public opinion to this day — that the two treaties of [[1871]] "stood and fell together," and that the abandonment of the Acheh reservation could not have been obtained without the sacrifice of the Cape. [[E. de Waal|De Waal]], the former minister of colonies, stigmatised this view in his *Onze Indische Financiën* as "a historical inaccuracy." According to De Waal he himself, as minister, had offered the Cape to England "without particular occasion," simply in order to put an end to the inter-tribal warfare among the negroes — "a pretty idea" — and only later had England demanded that the two treaties "should stand and fall together."

Van der Kemp accepts that public opinion in the Netherlands underrated the importance of the Acheh concession; that an incoming ministry, under whom the treaties were adopted by the legislature, was able to declare that the contention that it was easier to recruit forces on one's own territory than under a foreign flag had "no shadow of ground" — a declaration Van der Kemp finds inexplicable. But he does not press the question of linkage. The mere fact, he suggests, that the two treaties were concluded in the same year, and that the abandonment of the Acheh reservation could not have been obtained had not the Cape been offered, was enough to fix the impression that the two were linked; and the public could hardly be blamed for feeling the weight of the sacrifice even where it did not grasp the precise diplomatic sequence.

## The Sumatra Treaty as the Substantive Concession

The substantive Dutch gain of [[1871]] was accordingly the Sumatra Treaty, by which Britain renounced "all remonstrances against the extension of Netherlands authority in any part of the island of Sumatra," and therewith renounced the Acheh reservation of [[1824]]. Van der Kemp records that the abandonment was accorded, in the words of the treaty, "with the relinquishment of the proviso in this respect occurring in the notes exchanged by the Netherlands and British plenipotentiaries at the conclusion of the treaty of [[17 March 1824|17 March 1824]]." The Cape of Guinea cession, whether linked or not, was the smaller of the two transactions of that year; but it was the more visible, and it has therefore remained in Dutch memory as the symbol of the price paid for the [[Acheh War|Acheh expedition]] of [[1873]].

## Significance

Van der Kemp's discussion of the Cape of Guinea cession is, in effect, a footnote to his larger argument about the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra Treaty of 1871]]. It serves to mark the public impression of the treaty at the time of its conclusion, and to introduce the celebrated Second Chamber statement of [[Van Bosse's Second Chamber statement (17 January 1872)|Van Bosse]] of [[17 January 1872|17 January 1872]], in which the minister of colonies acknowledged that "it is Article 3 of the treaty of 1824 that has given rise to the conflict." The reader is left to draw his own conclusion as to whether the abandonment of the Cape was the price of the Acheh reservation or merely its occasion; but Van der Kemp clearly inclines to the former view, since he records De Waal's disclaimer without endorsing it.
