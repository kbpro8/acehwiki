**The Razing of Lam-Asan (30 May – 2 June 1896)** — the large-scale destruction of the *kampong* of [[Lam-Asan]] and its fortifications — was a sustained four-day engineering operation undertaken by 2 sections of engineers, 800 forced labourers, and 400 Chinese coolies, under the protection of multiple battalions — the [[12de Bataljon Infanterie|12de]], [[15de Bataljon Infanterie|15de]], [[6e Bataljon Infanterie|6e]], and [[9e Bataljon Infanterie|9e]] — in the immediate aftermath of the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive of 23–25 May 1896]]. The covering troops were commanded successively by [[Luitenant-Kolonel G. F. Soeters|Lt.-Col. Soeters]] on [[30 May]] [[1896]] and by [[Luitenant-Kolonel A. G. Hansen|Lt.-Col. A. G. Hansen]] on [[31 May]] [[1896]]; on [[2 June]] [[1896]] the [[9e Bataljon Infanterie|9e Bataljon]] under [[Luitenant-Kolonel J. F. T. Veeren|Lt.-Col. Veeren]] marched from [[Ketapan Doea]] along the foot of the gebergte. The operation was completed on [[2 June]] [[1896]] at 2 p.m., with no enemy fire on the last day, and was accompanied by the beginning of the excavation, levelling of the grave-mounds at 225 m west of [[Lam-Djamoe]].

## Background

The [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896]] had broken [[Teuku Umar|Teungkoe Oemar]]'s *stelling* around [[Lam-Pisang]] and had driven his warriors behind the [[Kroeng-Raba]]. The *kampong* of [[Lam-Asan]] — the scene of the heavy combat of [[23 May]] [[1896]], when the [[7e Bataljon Infanterie|7e Bataljon]] under [[Majoor J. R. Jacobs|Maj. Jacobs]] had stormed the south-eastern *rand* — was now in Dutch hands, but its *versterkingen*, *loopgraven*, and *benteng*s remained intact. The [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Regeeringscommissaris]], [[Lt.-Gen. Vetter]], resolved to raze the *kampong* and its works to the ground — both to deny the Achehnese the use of the position in any future operation and to demonstrate, in the most emphatic way possible, the cost of continued resistance. The operation would also mark the beginning of the excavation of the grave-mounds — the Islamic shrines that dotted the Achehnese landscape and that served as both religious sites and as fortified points of resistance — at 225 m west of [[Lam-Djamoe]].

The engineering resources committed to the operation were substantial: 2 sections of engineers, 800 forced labourers — typically convicts or impressed men from the consolidated line, and 400 Chinese coolies — hired labourers from the Chinese community of [[Koeta Radja]]. The covering troops — multiple battalions drawn from the [[12de Bataljon Infanterie|12de]], [[15de Bataljon Infanterie|15de]], [[6e Bataljon Infanterie|6e]], and [[9e Bataljon Infanterie|9e]] — were rotated daily to maintain freshness and to provide a continuous defensive perimeter.

## The Covering Troops and the Daily Rotation

The covering troops were commanded successively by different senior officers. On [[30 May]] [[1896]] the covering force was under [[Luitenant-Kolonel G. F. Soeters|Lt.-Col. G. F. Soeters]] — the same officer who had commanded the [[12e Bataljon Infanterie|12e Bataljon]] at the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive]] and who would, three weeks later, command the reserve colonne at the [[Tuchtiging der V Moekims Montassik (16–17 June 1896)|tuchtiging of the V Moekims Montassik]]. On [[31 May]] [[1896]] the covering force was under [[Luitenant-Kolonel A. G. Hansen|Lt.-Col. A. G. Hansen]] — who had commanded the [[3e Bataljon Infanterie|3e Bataljon]] at the [[Machtsvertoon in IX Moekims (29 May 1896)|machtsvertoon in the IX Moekims]] of [[29 May]] [[1896]] and would command the [[3e Bataljon Infanterie|3e Bataljon]] at the [[Ladoeng Landing and Glé Broeë Attack (22–23 June 1896)|Ladoeng landing]] of [[22 June]]–[[23 June]] [[1896]]. On [[2 June]] [[1896]] the [[9e Bataljon Infanterie|9e Bataljon]] under [[Luitenant-Kolonel J. F. T. Veeren|Lt.-Col. J. F. T. Veeren]] marched from [[Ketapan Doea]] along the foot of the gebergte — a wide flanking movement intended to discourage any Achehnese attempt to interfere with the razing.

## The Engineering Operation

The engineering operation itself was systematic and thorough. The 2 sections of *genietroepen* supervised the 800 *dwangarbeiders* and the 400 Chinese *koelies* in the demolition of the *versterkingen*, the filling-in of the *loopgraven*, the dismantling of the *bamboedoeri* abatis, and the burning of the houses. The *grafheuvels* at 225 m west of [[Lam-Djamoe]] were excavated — a delicate operation, given the religious sensitivity of the Islamic shrines, but one that the Dutch considered necessary to deny the Achehnese the use of the mounds as fortified points. The work was slow, hot, and arduous: the climate of Atjeh in early June, the weight of the earth-works, and the constant threat of sniping from the surrounding *kampong*s combined to make the razing a trial of endurance for both the engineers and the labourers.

The operation was completed on [[2 June]] [[1896]] at 2 p.m. — the fourth day of the razing. Remarkably, on the last day no enemy fire was directed against the working parties. The absence of fire was, in itself, a significant indicator: it suggested that the Achehnese had withdrawn their warriors from the immediate vicinity of [[Lam-Asan]], either to conserve their forces for the defence of the interior *stellingen* or in recognition that the position was no longer tenable. The Dutch had, in the course of four days, accomplished the complete destruction of [[Lam-Asan]] and its works.

## Significance

The Razing of Lam-Asan from [[30 May]] to [[2 June]] [[1896]] was the closing operation of the May phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and the most thorough single act of chastisement of the spring campaign. The commitment of 2 sections of engineers, 800 *dwangarbeiders*, and 400 Chinese *koelies* — protected by no fewer than four battalions of infantry — was a measure of the importance that the [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Regeeringscommissaris]] attached to the complete destruction of the position. The operation also marked the beginning of the *afgraven* of the *grafheuvels* — a policy that would be repeated elsewhere in the residency and that would, in the longer term, prove a significant source of Achehnese resentment. The razing was, in tactical terms, a defensive measure — the denial of a fortified position to the enemy; in strategic terms, it was a declaration that the Dutch intended to hold the ground they had taken at the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive]] and that the Achehnese could expect no respite. The absence of enemy fire on the last day was, in its way, the most significant outcome of the operation: the Achehnese had, for the moment, conceded the field.
