The **N.E. Monsoon** (the [[musem tiniu]] of the Achehnese) was the northerly monsoon season which set in around September, marking the close of the favourable period for voyages from the [[Banda Acheh|capital]] of [[Acheh]] to the [[West Coast|West Coast]] and the opening of the dangerous season for shipping to the [[North Coast|North]] and [[East Coast|East Coasts]]. Like its counterpart the [[S.W. Monsoon]], it was punctuated by a brief interval of calmer weather, the prediction of which was a prized science among Achehnese sailors and traders.

## The season and its intervals

In the Achehnese calendar the N.E. Monsoon commenced in keunong lhang (about September) and continued through the early months of the year. Although the season was dangerous for voyages from the capital to the N. or E. coast, the first keunong of the year—keunong dua ploh lhèë (about January)—contained a remarkable period of from five to seven days during which the voyage could be undertaken without risk: "a sort of interval in the N.E. Monsoon." Achehnese sailors and traders set a high value on the knowledge of the exact times when these intervals took place, asserting that there were certain signs by which they could be ascertained each year, but that there were only a few who possessed the requisite knowledge.

## The kanduri la'ot at Ulee Lheue

The N.E. Monsoon had its chief ceremonial significance at the kanduri la'ot, the annual sea-feast held by each *lho'* (division) of the seine-fishers of [[Ulee Lheue]] to invoke God's blessing on the labours of its *pawangs*. The time chosen for this feast was that when the fishery enjoyed a compulsory holiday owing to the rough weather brought on by the changes of the N.E. and S.W. monsoons. Thus the foreshore at Ulee Lheue was divided for the pukat fishermen into two *lho's*, one of which gave its religious feast in keunong 17 (about April, at the beginning of the [[S.W. Monsoon]]), and the other in keunong 5 (about September, at the beginning of the N.E. Monsoon). The pawangs of the lho' bore the expenses of the feast, but could claim a contribution of about four dollars from each of their contract buyers.

## Lesser importance compared with the S.W. Monsoon

The interval in the N.E. Monsoon was of less importance to traders than that of the S.W. Monsoon, because there was a better supply of all necessaries on the [[North Coast|North]] and [[East Coast|East Coasts]]. The interval of the S.W. Monsoon, by contrast, gave access to the *rantòs*, the wild and inhospitable districts on the West Coast which separate the larger settlements from one another and are the field of the pepper-planters' labours; nothing could be obtained in those districts during the S.W. Monsoon itself, and whoever succeeded in conveying thither a cargo of pots and pans (*kanèt blangbòng*), clothing, salt, sugar and molasses was certain to return home with a handsome profit.
