A **kanduri** (Achehnese; cognate with the Malay *kenduri*) is a religious feast, the most characteristic social institution of Achehnese Islam and the form in which the great majority of collective religious observances find their expression. Small kanduris are of very common occurrence in every gampong, and some require the presence of the teungku. The word covers both the periodic feasts of the ecclesiastical year and occasional feasts given at the transitions of life — at births, circumcisions, marriages, deaths, the completion of a house, the safe return from a journey, and the like.

The principal dish at a kanduri is glutinous rice (*bu kunyèt*) coloured yellow with turmeric, to which are always added *tump'òë* (a sort of pancakes, six or seven of which are served with each dish). At smaller feasts the yellow rice forms the pièce de résistance, though a goat is sometimes slaughtered for the guests. Otherwise buffaloes, oxen, goats and sheep are seldom killed except at the great annual festivals. Tea and coffee are also served on such occasions, though the use of these beverages is generally restricted to invalids. The food is prepared by the women of the household or, at larger community feasts, from general contributions under the supervision of the teungku, and is brought in *idangs* (trays) to the guests assembled in the *seuramoe Mòë*, the front verandah of an Achehnese house to which the uninitiated are admitted, or in the meunasah when the feast is given by the gampong.

Each of the lunar months has its characteristic kanduri, which gives the month its popular Achehnese name. The succession includes the [[Kanduri Mo'lot|Kanduri Mo'lot]] (Rabi' al-awwal), the Kanduri Apam (Rajab), the Kanduri Bu (Sha'ban), the [[Puasa (Ramadhan fasting month)|Puasa]] (Ramadhan) and the [[Leubè raya (Hari Raya Puasa / Eid al-Fitr)|Leubè raya]] that closes it, the Kanduri Apam Seulawah or Kanduri Boh Kayèë in Jumada al-akhir, and so on through the year. These monthly kanduris are given in the meunasah, where the teungku recites the appropriate formulas — *meuliku* from the dikr at the Mo'lot, the *Barzanji* in honour of the Prophet's birth, and the like — before the assembled householders distribute the food. A specially great kanduri is held at the tomb of [[Teungku Anjong]] in [[Gampong Jawa]] on the 12th of Maulid.

Beyond the calendar, kanduris are given whenever a religious motive invites one: at the conclusion of a Quran recitation (*peutamat daroh*), on the appointment of a panglima kawom or uleebalang, at the [[Peusijuek (cooling ceremony)|peusijuek]] or "cooling" of a new house or a returned traveller, and as vows (*nazar*) for the recovery of the sick. The official invitation (*meuròh*) to a kanduri is always given in the form of an offering of *ranub batèë* (betel in a copper or silver bowl lined with ornamental cloth), and, where the invitation is addressed to persons of high consideration such as tuankus, the symbolical gift is presented in a more costly sirih-bowl (*keureudong*) in place of the batèë. Snouck Hurgronje notes that Teungku Tiro was rebuked by his fellow ulamas for lavishing on kanduris and on the repair of chapels money that, in their view, should have been used to erect forts along the *linie* and to fit out a fleet to harass the Dutch by sea — an indication of how central the institution had become to Achehnese religious and political life alike.

## Additional account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Kanduri (general)

A **kanduri** (Achehnese; cognate of the Malay *kenduri* and the Arabic *qandūriyya*) is the general Achehnese term for a religious feast — the characteristic form in which the great majority of collective religious observances find their social expression in Acheh. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] observes, the kanduri is distinguished from a purely secular meal by a formal religious element: it is marked by the recitation of selections from the [[Quran]] or of litanies, followed by a final prayer, after which the assembled company partake of the food. A feast offered to specially honoured guests which lacks this religious consecration is called a *peujamè* (penjamee), or "guest-meal," and is "not in any sense of religious character." The distinction between *kanduri* and *peujamè* runs through the whole etiquette of Achehnese hospitality.

The domestic kanduri may be given for many reasons — at the conclusion of a Quran recitation (*peutamat daroh*), at the appointment of a panglima kawom or uleebalang, at the "cooling" ([[peusijuk]]) of a new house or of a returned traveller, or as a vow (*kaoy* or *nazar*) for the recovery of the sick. The viands of an ordinary kanduri consist of an *idang* (a tray of food) with rice as the principal dish, but the kanduri held in honour of the dead — called *kanduri bu* — gives to the eighth month of the Achehnese year ([[Sha'ban|Shaʽban]]) its popular name "Rice-kanduri."

Each of the lunar months has its own characteristic kanduri, which gives the month its popular Achehnese name. The succession includes the [[Kanduri Apam]] (Rajab), the [[Kanduri Bu]] (Shaʽban), the [[Puasa]] (Ramadhan) and the *Leubè raya* that closes it, and the [[Kurubeu'en]] (Dul-hidjah). Other annual kanduris are tied to the agricultural calendar — the *kanduri blang* or "field-feast" that opens the [[Musem ludih blang (Season of the freedom of the land)|musem ludih blang]], and the *kanduri laot* or "sea-kanduri" of the seine-fishers. Each *gampong* also holds its own kanduri for the dead, occasional as well as calendrical, and the village teungku is the indispensable consecrator.

As a social institution, the kanduri expresses at once the commensality of the Muslim community and the Achehnese idea that no important transition — birth, [[peutron|first contact with the earth]], [[peusunat|circumcision]], marriage, voyage, sickness, death — is complete without a religious feast. The kanduri thus occupies in Achehnese life a place midway between the prescribed liturgy of Islam and the purely customary usages of the country. Even where the proximate occasion of a feast is a secular one — a wedding, a safe return, the completion of a house — the presence of the teungku and the recitation of the Quran transform it into a kanduri, while the absence of these would leave it a mere *peujamè*.
