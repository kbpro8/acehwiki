The **edicts of Meukuta Alam** are the royal decrees (*[[Sarakata|sarakata]]*) ascribed by general consensus to [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Sultan Meukuta Alam]] — also called Eseukanda (Iskandar) Muda, who reigned from 1607 to 1636 — and regarded by the Achehnese as the foundational constitutional documents of their country. They are treated at length by C. Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese* as the chief documentary source — alongside oral tradition — for the political institutions of the Sultanate of [[Acheh]] at its apogee.

## The Sultan and His Power

Even the demands made on the [[uleebalang|uleebalangs]] by the port-kings in the most flourishing period of their rule — the second half of the sixteenth and first half of the seventeenth century — were extremely moderate, and bear witness partly to the want of power on the part of these princes to control the government of the interior, and partly to the small interest they seem to have taken in any such interference. Even the edicts ascribed by general consensus to Meukuta Alam (1607–36) confine themselves within very narrow limits, although that prince had at his command a considerable fleet and a small standing army. Besides, these documents are not evidence of a state of things that ever actually existed, but simply the expression of the wishes of the king and his councillors as to what they would see carried out. It was enough for the successive Sultans to have immunity from annoyance at the hands of the numerous potentates of the interior, and this object they easily gained by the means that lay beneath their hand.

## The Extant Edicts

During his residence in Acheh, Hurgronje obtained copies of a number of sarakatas not included among those published by [[Van Langen|Van Langen]] in his *Atjehsch Staatsbestuur* (1888). They were as a rule lengthy documents, and most of them bore dates. Those ascribed to Meukuta Alam include: one dated 1607, revived by the princess [[Princess Sapiatodin|Sapiatodin]] in 1645, intended to regulate the court ceremonial and solemnities at festivals (very rich in details); and two dated respectively 1635 and 1640 (*sic*). These documents, together with certain undated edicts of Sapiatodin, [[Amat Shah Juhan|Amat Shah Juhan]] (the first prince of the [[Bugis-Origin Dynasty|latest dynasty]]), and [[Badrudin Asem|Badrudin Asem]] (Hashim), deal with commercial and port regulations as affecting different nationalities and different kinds of merchandise, and the collection and distribution of taxes. In these documents are even found detailed customs-tariffs.

## Contents

It is not difficult, Hurgronje observes, to distinguish in these edicts the old and already established conditions from the new ones which they purport to introduce. The principal features of the old status were the great independence of the numerous chiefs and the all-prevailing influence of traditional custom. The new elements may be classified as follows: first, attempts at an extension of the authority of the Sultan by allotting to him, the "king of the port," a certain control over the succession of the other chieftains of the land, over the disputes of these chiefs with one another, and over the interests of strangers; second, certain rules intended to bring about a stricter observance of Mohammedan law; third, regulations dealing with trade, the shares of certain officials in the profits of the port, the court ceremonial, and the celebration of great religious festivals.

## Practical Development and Popular Reverence

Of the three new elements introduced by these edicts, only the regulations dealing with trade and ceremonial had any significant practical effect, and these only during the reign of their promulgator. Of the other innovations, hardly a single one has passed from document into actuality; the state of things the edicts reveal as already in being has, on the other hand, continued its existence.

The ordinary Achehnese, however, holds the edicts of Meukuta Alam in great reverence. All that recalls to him the greatness of his country is closely connected with the names of those very princes who are generally regarded as the authors of the sarakatas. He firmly believes that all that he reveres as the sacred institutions of his country — albeit not mentioned in a single one of the edicts — is *adat Meukuta Alam*, or at any rate *adat pôteu meureuhôm*, "adat of defunct royalties," and he is convinced that information respecting them is of a certainty contained in some one or other of the sarakatas. The Achehnese chiefs have a secondary aim in referring with a certain emphasis to these edicts as the laws of Acheh: all that is contained therein respecting court ceremonial, festivals, religion, and so forth, they exalt; what conflicts with their own pretensions they quietly pass over.
