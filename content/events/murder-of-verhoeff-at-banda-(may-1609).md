**The murder of Admiraal Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff at Banda on 22 May 1609** — the Bandanese ambush near Rada on the island of [[Banda-Neira|Banda-Neira]] in which [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff]] and forty-six of his men were killed, after Verhoeff had landed with a *breeden raad* of 150 soldiers for a "vriendschappelijke bespreking" with the Bandanese chiefs — was the proximate cause of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]]'s coercive monopoly policy at [[Banda-eilanden|Banda]] and the foundation of [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s later argument that monopoly could only be obtained by force. The murder is the central violent episode of Van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]] and the principal instance of Bandanese resistance to the Dutch monopoly claim.

## Background: the secret instruction of 11 April 1608

The [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet of nine ships and four jachts — including the young [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Jan Pieterszoon Coen]] as onderkoopman — had left Texel on [[22 December]] [[1607]] with the secret instruction of [[11 April]] [[1608]] to seize Banda and attack [[Malaka|Malaka]]. The instruction, dispatched after the fleet's departure and reaching Verhoeff at [[Bantam|Bantam]] on [[26 December]] [[1608]] by fast jacht, committed the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] to the new monopolistic policy: "de eylanden van Banda op 't allerhoochste gerecommandeert". After the failure of the [[Verhoeff's Malaka expedition (1608)|Malaka expedition of 1608]], Verhoeff sailed to the Banda islands to execute the instruction.

## The arrival and the negotiations (April 1609)

Verhoeff arrived off Banda-Neira on [[8 April]] [[1609]] with nine ships and four jachts, joined by the English ship *Hector* under [[William Keeling|William Keeling]]. He presented to the Bandanese chiefs a letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]] — a letter that, as Van der Woude notes, was "pasklaar gemaakt voor het beoogde doel" — demanding the spice monopoly and permission to build a fort. The Bandanese chiefs, after protracted negotiations, gave formal consent to the spice monopoly and the construction of a fort, but quietly evacuated women and children to the hills and "wierpen de mannen versterkingen op". When the Dutch landed 700 men on [[25 April]] [[1609]], they found the houses abandoned: "t welke haer seer wel te passe quam om te logieren", but the abandonment was an early sign that the Bandanese consent was not genuine.

## The ambush (22 May 1609)

On [[21 May]] [[1609]] the Bandanese requested Verhoeff to land with his *breeden raad* for a "vriendschappelijke bespreking" the next day. Verhoeff accepted, landing on [[22 May]] [[1609]] with 150 soldiers. Near the town of Rada, he and his *breeden raad* walked into an ambush: "op kinderlijk eenvoudige wijze in een hinderlaag". Verhoeff and forty-six of his men were killed; many of them were beheaded. The following day, the bodies of five hostages were found, one Dutchman was killed, and on [[Groot-Banda|Great Banda]] Dutch assistants and merchants were murdered. On [[Poeloe Ai|Poeloe Ai]] the Dutch were taken under the protection of [[William Keeling|Keeling]], the English captain.

The expression "op syn Admiraels Verhoeven leeren bitcharen" — "to consult on Admiral Verhoeff's bench" — became a Dutch proverb for walking into a trap. Van der Woude notes that the expression was used for years afterward in the Indies.

## The aftermath

Vice-Admiraal **Simon Hoen** assumed command after the murder. The Dutch retaliated: they destroyed the surroundings of the fort — which had been named **Fort Nassau** — up to an hour's walk; they attacked Labetaka on [[15 July]] [[1609]] (an attack in which "man keines Menschen verschonet, sondern alles mit Weib und Kindt, Jung und Alt zugleich erschossen vnd erstochen hat"); and they blockaded the Bandanese into submission. The Bandanese offered peace on [[7 August]] [[1609]], and the so-called *eeuwig verbond* (eternal covenant) of [[10 August]] [[10 August]] [[1609]] was concluded. By the terms of the covenant, the Bandanese chiefs were placed under the jurisdiction of Fort Nassau, all spices were to be sold only to the Dutch, and the island of Banda-Neira was ceded to the VOC in perpetuity.

The covenant, however, was not the end of the conflict. The Bandanese continued to resist the Dutch monopoly, often selling their spices to the English at [[Poeloe Ai|Ai]] and [[Run|Run]] or to Javanese and Makassarese middlemen. The Dutch position at Banda remained precarious until [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s conquest of [[1621]], in which the Bandanese were depopulated and the *perkenier* system of nutmeg plantations was established.

## The young Coen and the formative experience

The [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] fleet included the young [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Jan Pieterszoon Coen]] as onderkoopman. Van der Woude notes that [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] "het begin van het treurspel van Banda meegemaakt" — was present at the beginning of the tragedy of Banda. The experience of the murder of Verhoeff — and the bewindhebbers' instruction that had produced it — shaped [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s entire later policy. The [[Discours of 1 January 1614|Discours of 1 January 1614]] — [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s foundational text — would explicitly cite the Banda experience and the bewindhebbers' instructions of [[1608]]-[[1609]] as the foundation of the monopoly-by-force policy: "Van Banda connen ons meester maken, dezelvige eylanden met macht aantasten; sulcx moeten nootelijcken doen ende de plaetse populeren, ofte het landt verlaten, want 't en sal anders met Banda nyet zijn".

## Significance

The murder of Verhoeff at Banda is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the proximate cause of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]]'s coercive monopoly policy at Banda and the foundation of [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s later argument that monopoly could only be obtained by force. The murder — and the bewindhebbers' instruction of [[11 April]] [[1608]] that had produced it — was the formative experience of the young [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]], who would later cite the Banda experience as the foundation of the [[Discours of 1 January 1614|Discours of 1 January 1614]] and the monopoly-by-force policy that would define VOC conduct for the next two centuries. The murder also marks the institutional transition from factorij to fort — the transition from the commercial establishment of the pre-VOC period to the military-administrative compound of the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] era — and the *eeuwig verbond* of [[10 August]] [[1609]], by which Banda-Neira was ceded to the VOC in perpetuity, was the principal legal foundation of the Dutch position at Banda until [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]]'s conquest of [[1621]]. The murder's role in the Dutch-Atjeh relationship was indirect: by [[1609]] the strategic centre of Dutch-Indies policy had shifted from Atjeh to Banda, and the Banda experience — not the Atjeh experience — would define the [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] programme.
