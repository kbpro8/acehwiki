The **Treaty of Quedah of [[1786]]** — referred to in Dutch sources as the *afstand van Pinang 1786* — was the agreement by which the [[Raja of Quedah|Raja of Quedah]] ceded the island of [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]] to Great Britain for a yearly payment of 10,000 dollars. The treaty was arranged by [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]], who had concluded that [[Penang|Pinang]] was preferable to [[Acheh]] as a site for a British settlement, and it marked the beginning of the British presence on the [[Malay Peninsula|Malay Peninsula]] — a presence that would, within a generation, draw [[Penang]] into the management of Achehnese affairs and ultimately into the events surrounding the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]].

## Background

European relations with [[Acheh]] had, throughout the eighteenth century, been "either very loose or very strained." Even so powerful a figure as [[Warren Hastings|Warren Hastings]], Governor-General of Bengal, had to submit in [[1786]] to the [[Sultan of Acheh's letter to Warren Hastings|insulting letter of 7 November 1786]] from the [[Sultan Alaeddin Muhammad Shah of Acheh|Sultan of Acheh]], who returned the worthless muskets Hastings had sent him and declared that, if he thought of sending more such goods, he had better leave it alone. In the same year [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]] had hit upon the idea of a British settlement on [[Penang|Pulo Pinang]], which he judged more suitable than [[Acheh]].

[[Captain Francis Light|Light]]'s report to the Governor-General of Bengal of 15 February 1786 had described [[Acheh]] as "a good road, but no place of security against an enemy there — the country is fertile beyond description and very populous. The inhabitants [are] rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous. To form a settlement there of safety and advantage, a force sufficient to subdue all the chiefs would be necessary." [[James Price|James Price]], reporting to the Supreme Board in London from Calcutta on 23 February 1786, agreed: "I prefer [[Penang|Penang]] to the port of Acheen, as being more healthy and entire free from that oppression, war and confusion which however distressed and at length driven out every European nation who has attempted to settle there."

## The Cession

The outcome of these deliberations was a treaty with the [[Raja of Quedah|Raja of Quedah]] by which, for a yearly rent of 10,000 dollars, the island was ceded to Great Britain. The Sultan's letter, preserved in Van der Kemp's source, recorded that "Captain Light Deva Rajah came here and informed us that our friend requested Pulo Penang, we have instantly given to our friend and akeel Captain Deva Rajah, to plant the Honble Company's English flag upon Pulo Penang, a place for trade and to repair your ships of war — and for refreshments, wood and water." The cession was signed on the 20th day of the Moon Shaval in the year 1199 Regira.

## Significance

Although the treaty was with [[Quedah]] and not with [[Acheh]], Van der Kemp emphasises its bearing on the subsequent Achehnese story. The early years of [[Penang|Pinang]] were a "history of suffering," and at one time the abandonment of the island was considered; yet the thought of a settlement at [[Acheh]] — and of gaining predominant influence in that kingdom — was never given up. The supervision of Achehnese affairs was specifically entrusted to the government of [[Penang|Pinang]], and a particular circumstance in 1812 brought that supervision more directly into the hands of the Pinang administration. It was from this connection that there flowed the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]], the [[Captain Canning's mission to Acheh (1814)|Canning mission of 1814]], the [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|Coombs mission of 1817–18]], and ultimately the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs commission]] and the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]].
