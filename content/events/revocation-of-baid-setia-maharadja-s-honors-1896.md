**The revocation of Baid Setia Maharadja's honours** occurred in [[1896]], when the Netherlands Indies government confiscated the gold chain bearing the Dutch coat of arms that had been granted to [[Teukoe Moeda Baid Setia Maharadja]], [[Uleebalang|uleebalang]] of the [[VII Mukims|VII Moekims]], just eight years earlier in [[1888]]. The original Dutch text states that the chain was *"hem in 1896 wegens ontrouw ontnomen"* — "taken from him in 1896 on account of disloyalty."

## The revocation

The confiscation of a formally awarded colonial honour was a serious political act, representing not merely a personal disgrace but a public rupture in the relationship between the colonial state and one of its indigenous allies. The Dutch word *ontrouw* (disloyalty, faithlessness) is unambiguous: Baid Setia Maharadja was judged to have betrayed the trust placed in him when he accepted the chain.

## Interpretation

The revocation illustrates a recurring pattern in the [[Aceh War]]: the difficulty the Dutch faced in securing lasting loyalty from the [[Uleebalang|uleebalang class]]. Uleebalangs who accepted Dutch honours and subsidies frequently found themselves caught between the demands of their colonial patrons and the expectations of their own communities, where collaboration with the Dutch could be seen as betrayal of the Islamic cause. Many uleebalangs who initially cooperated with the Dutch later rejoined the resistance, or were suspected of maintaining secret contacts with the guerilla fighters.

The year [[1896]] was still well before the Dutch adopted their final, more effective strategy — the so-called "short war" under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and the systematic use of Marechaussee units — and the colonial position in the interior of Atjeh remained precarious. Baid Setia Maharadja's apparent defection, whether real or merely suspected, reflects the unstable and contested nature of Dutch authority in the Acehnese hinterland at this date.

## The chain in the museum

After confiscation, the chain entered the collection of the [[Koninklijk Bataviaasch Genootschap van Kunsten en Wetenschappen|Batavian Society]], where it was displayed in Cabinet 5 (Atjeh) as a specimen of Acehnese-period goldwork — albeit one with a distinctly colonial backstory. Its catalogue entry in the 1928 guide preserves both the date of the original grant and the date and reason for its revocation, making it a valuable historical document in its own right.
