The **Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission** of [[1819]] was the joint British commission dispatched from [[Calcutta]] in early 1819 to negotiate a political and commercial treaty with the sultan of [[Acheh]], to settle the long-running [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]], and to forestall suspected Dutch intrigues on the north coast of [[Sumatra]]. It was composed of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]], lieutenant-governor of [[Bencoolen]], and [[Captain Coombs|Captain Coombs]] of the Bengal establishment, with Raffles as senior commissioner enjoying a casting vote. The commission's work culminated in the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] of 22 April 1819, but the undertaking was marked throughout by acute personal dissension between the two commissioners, by disputes with Governor [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] of [[Penang]], and by [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s unauthorised diversion to the [[Karimon Islands]] — which led, indirectly, to the founding of [[Singapore]].

## Origin and Instructions

The commission originated in the early months of 1818, when reports reached [[Calcutta]] of Dutch activity in the archipelago and of the anarchy prevailing in Acheh. [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]'s [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|first Acheh mission]] of late 1817 – February 1818 had concluded that the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] was the recognised sultan; [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]], writing from [[Bencoolen]], insisted that the rightful ruler was the elder [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]] and that the Dutch were negotiating with him. The Bengal government under [[Lord Moira|Lord Moira]] — alarmed by the rejection of [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] at [[Pontianak]] by [[Van Boekholz|Van Boekholz]] and by the destruction of [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]]'s treaty at [[Lingga]] by [[Schout-bij-nacht Wolterbeek|Wolterbeek]] — determined to send a commission. In its despatch to London of 14 January 1819 the Calcutta government explained that it hoped "in time to establish our political influence in Acheh to the exclusion of that of the Dutch or other foreign nations."

The commissioners were instructed: (1) to prevent future misunderstanding; (2) to secure free navigation of the [[Straits of Malacca|Straits of Malacca]] and uninterrupted commercial intercourse with the northern coast of Sumatra; and (3) "to ascertain whether the party of the old king [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jowhar 001 Alum]] or that of the new king [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf 001 Mum]] predominated in the state; and to negotiate with the ruling party whichever it might be." Should doubt remain, the commission was to return to Bengal for further orders; to minimise that risk, [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] was given a casting vote.

## The Karimon Detour

The commission was barely constituted before [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] threw it into confusion. On 19 January 1819 [[Major William Farquhar|Farquhar]] wrote from [[Malacca]] to propose a joint inspection of the [[Karimon Islands]] as a site for a British settlement. [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] accepted without informing either [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] or [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]], abruptly sailed from Penang, and — having taken with him the artillery detachment that [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] believed was destined for Acheh — only belatedly dispatched the [[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir|Minto to Pedir]] under [[Lieutenant Criddle|Lieutenant Criddle]] to inform [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] of the commission's approach. This [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|Karimon detour]] resulted in the founding of Singapore but provoked bitter complaints from [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|Bannerman]] and [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]], and was the subject of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s lengthy defence in his letter to the government secretary at Calcutta of 30 September 1819.

## Seven Weeks at Banda Acheh

The commission anchored off [[Banda Acheh]] on [[14 March 1819|14 March [[1819]]]]. There was no sultan at the capital: authority was, as the Bengal government reported to London, "exercised in name by the council of the Sagis, but in reality by [[Panglima Polem|Panglima Polem]] with his adherents." The [[Three Sagis|three Sagis]] — the chiefs of the [[XXII Mukims|XXII]], [[XXV Mukims|XXV]] and [[XXVI Mukims|XXVI Mukims]] — were the traditional guardians of the sultanate. The commission sent a circular summoning the chiefs to a conference; [[Panglima Polem|Polim]] replied from the [[XXII Mukims|XXII Mukims]] that the meeting would take place in eight days and that, until then, the commissioners were not to land.

Although the prohibition extended only to the commission as a body, the receptions the commissioners met with when they did land were, in Van der Kemp's words, "extremely uncivil" — corresponding closely to the insulting reception later reported by the crew of the *[[Wilhelmina mutiny and murder of Bouwens|Wilhelmina]]* (22–24 June 1819). The populace began to throw up fortifications at the mouth of the [[Acheh River]], of which the commission complained to [[Panglima Polem|Polim]]. When Polim at last appeared and took up residence in the palace, the Bengal government concluded that he alone was the actual ruler, that [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] was not acknowledged at all, and that [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé]] was merely a tool in the hands of the parties.

## The Paper War

By this time the divisions within the commission itself had erupted. [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s earlier investigations had confirmed him in the view that [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] should be recognised; [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] held with equal force that the sultan recognised by the Sagis was [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]]. Worse, [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] had been designated to remain at Acheh as British resident and was now disappointed of his post. The two men, sitting side by side on the same ship, engaged in what [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] himself — in a letter to [[Sir Robert Harry Inglis|Sir Robert Harry Inglis]] of 12 June 1819 — called a [[Paper war|paper war]] that "actually occupies above a thousand pages of the Company's largest sized paper." Eventually [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] came round to [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s opinion, and the commission sailed for [[Pedir]].

## Conclusion at Pedir

At Pedir the commission was received by [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]], and the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 22 April 1819]] was signed. Arms and a draft for 100,000 rupees were handed to the sultan; the commission then proceeded to [[Telok Semawé]] to demand that [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] renounce his claims, which he did in a submission as worthless as the treaty itself. The Bengal government, in its report to London of 7 October 1820, expressed its dissatisfaction: it was well enough that Syf had been dropped, but to furnish arms and money in return for nothing but evil promises seemed indefensible. [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s own self-congratulation Van der Kemp characterises as pure exaggeration: nothing had been gained, and the commission might as well have stayed at home. Yet the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty]] it produced would, five years later in London, supply the pretext for the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Acheh declaration annexed to the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824]] — and so, in Van der Kemp's argument, condemn the Netherlands to half a century of impotence on Sumatra.
