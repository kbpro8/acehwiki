**Asan-Usen** is the Achehnese name for the first month of the Mohammedan year, Muharram. The name is taken from the commemoration of [[Hasan and Husain|Hasan and Husain]] — the sons of [[Ali]] and grandsons of the [[Prophet Muhammad|Prophet]] — which in Acheh, as in parts of the Deccan and the West Coast of Sumatra, is associated with the tenth day of this month, the *Ashura* (Achehnese *Achura*). The Achehnese name *Asan-Usen* is the popular form of the Arabic *Hasan wa'l-Husain*; in Java the same commemoration has given the day its name *Sura*, applied also to the whole month.

In the books of Mohammedan law it is set down as *sunat* — a meritorious though not obligatory work — to fast on the 10th day of Muharram. In Acheh, [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] notes, "none but very devout persons observe this custom, so that this day, which is named Ashura (in Acheh Achura and in Java Sura) passes almost unnoticed as far as concerns its celebration." In Shiʽite countries the case is the reverse: there the first ten days of this month are devoted to all manner of ceremonies, processions, discourses, and theatrical representations purporting to commemorate the conflict between Husain and the Umayyads at [[Kerbela]], culminating on the Ashura, the day of his martyrdom. These festivities, with their mourning, frenzied self-laceration, and the *tābūt* processions in which a symbolic coffin of the martyrs is carried about, belong properly to Persia and the Shiʽite portions of British India.

What is "worthy of remark," Hurgronje observes, is that "even Mohammedan peoples who follow the orthodox ritual, but whose life and thought have been subjected to Shiʽite influences, celebrate feasts of the above description." The Achehnese — Shafiʽites like the other Mohammedans of the Eastern Archipelago — received their Islam through the Deccan, and popular customs of Shiʽite origin entered the Archipelago along with the fundamental truths of the Shafiʽite law. Thus, "even previously to this [the English introduction of the *tābūt* processions by sepoy immigrants], a Hasan-Husain feast enjoyed much popularity both in Sumatra and elsewhere"; and the Achehnese name of the month preserves the trace of that older celebration. The noisy *tābūt* processions that may now be witnessed year by year at [[Kuta Raja]] are, however, "for the most part got up by the Padang people who have settled there. Some Klings and Hindus take part in them, but the Achehnese merely act as spectators."

A further custom, "which is really no more than an insignificant adjunct of the Hasan-Husain festivals, but which exists elsewhere as an independent usage, is the cooking of special viands on the Ashura day." In Acheh this dish is a porridge called *kanji Achura*, consisting of rice, cocoanut milk, sugar and pieces of cocoanut, mixed with various fruits cut into small pieces — papayas, peas, pomegranates, plantains, sugarcane and various edible roots. The *kanji Achura* is not cooked in every separate house: "one or two large pots full suffice for a whole gampong. Those who undertake the cooking receive voluntary subscriptions from their fellow-villagers." The mess is brought to the *meunasah* or sometimes to the junction of the gampong-path with the main road, and "all who wish fall to and gormandize, generally to such an extent as to cause indigestion."
