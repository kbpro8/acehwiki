**The Battle of the Missigit** on **[[10-april|10 April]] [[1873]]** was the first major Dutch assault on the fortified great mosque (*missigit* or *mesigit raja*) that stood between the beachhead at [[Pante Perak|Pantei-Tjermin]] and the [[Kraton (institution)|Kraton]] of the Sultan of [[aceh|Atjeh]] during the [[April 1873 Atjeh Expedition|first Aceh expedition]] under Major-General [[J. H. R. Köhler|Köhler]]. The Dutch took the mosque by fire and storm in the afternoon but abandoned it the same evening, exhausted by heat, mud and continuous Acehnese pressure. The battle was the turning-point of the first expedition: although the mosque was successfully stormed a second time on [[14-april|14 April]] [[1873]], Köhler was killed the same day and the expedition was ultimately withdrawn.

## Background

After the successful landing at [[Battle of Pantei Tjermin and Kota Mogat (8 April 1873)|Pantei-Tjermin and Kota Mogat]] on **[[8-april|8 April]] [[1873]]** and a day of reconnaissances on [[9-april|9 April]], Köhler ordered a general advance on the Kraton for **[[10-april|10 April]] [[1873]]**. Intelligence and the easy abandonment of the coastal forts had convinced the Dutch staff that the Acehnese would not offer serious resistance before the Kraton itself. The troops carried only one day's rations; "if the troops did not return the same day," rations were to be sent after them. As Brooshooft later described the mood, it was thought that the march would be little more than a military parade.

Considerable delay was already encountered at the bridge over the lagoon on the inner side of the beach — a bridge of four *jukken* (canoes) with a plank 0.40 m wide, "therefore unsuitable for the crossing of troops." The engineers lacked the time and materials to make it passable for the artillery, so that the guns had to be taken apart and manhandled across; moreover it was slippery with all the slime that the soldiers, who had had to wade through the mud of the lagoon, had brought upon it. Although the march had begun at seven o'clock, it was half past eleven before the whole column had passed the bridge.

## The First Attack, 10 April

At about one o'clock in the afternoon the vanguard came in sight of the missigit, a square, walled religious enclosure that commanded the approaches to the Kraton, and the attack was ordered. "Here too an unexpected stubborn resistance was met," Brooshooft writes. The attempts to outflank the position failed, while the Dutch troops were themselves attacked from all sides — from the wood's edge and from the neighbouring *kampongs*. The guns proved to have little effect on the thick walls, and the high parapet made a direct storm virtually impossible. Acehnese marksmen and *voorvechters* (champions), working themselves up with the war-dance (*landak*), kept up a galling fire from loopholes and from behind trees.

At last, about half past one in the afternoon, light-balls (incendiary projectiles) succeeded in setting the *atap* (palm-thatch) roof of the mosque alight. As the enemy's fire then slackened, the storm column — led by officers of the [[genietroepen|Genie]] — succeeded in climbing an areng-palm that leaned against the wall and dropping into the enclosure, which was thereupon abandoned by its defenders. The Dutch flag was raised on the wall.

## The Retreat from the Mosque

The same afternoon, however, the missigit was again abandoned by the Dutch and immediately re-occupied by the Acehnese. Brooshooft, following Kielstra, gives three reasons:

1. The troops were exhausted by the long march through mud, the oppressive heat, the fight itself, and the unbearable warmth that — at least initially — prevailed inside the conquered enclosure as a result of the burning roof.
2. Acehnese parties began to press again from the surrounding *kampongs* and from the direction of the Kraton, threatening to cut off the small storm party.
3. Köhler judged it imprudent to divide his force and ordered everyone withdrawn to a more defensible position on the northern burial mound on the *sawah* (rice-field) before the missigit.

"Thus this day too ended," Brooshooft notes, "without a decisive advantage having been obtained." The Dutch had suffered roughly sixty killed and wounded; among the dead was the artillery officer [[1ste Luitenant Bordes|Bordes]], who was to be buried on the beach two days later.

## Interlude, 11–13 April

The following days were spent in resting the troops, repulsing Acehnese attacks, making reconnaissances, and preparing a second attack. Scaling ladders — which had been mistakenly stowed aboard the hospital ship for the 8 April landing — were brought up and distributed among the storm columns. The navy supplied additional guns and rocket parties. Köhler moved his headquarters forward, and dispositions were made for a more deliberate assault.

## The Second Attack, 14 April 1873

On **[[14-april|14 April]] [[1873]]** at five in the morning the troops returned to the attack. After the mortars, now more favourably placed, had fired a number of rounds, the storm column approached the wall. The scaling ladders were placed and held by the *mineurs* (sappers) of the Genie, because the Acehnese within tried to hurl them back with their lances. The ladders were climbed, but the great number of defenders who awaited the Dutchmen with lance and [[Klewang|klewang]] and threw large sharp stones over the wall prevented them from jumping inside. The soldiers who had climbed to the top of the wall fired, while those below loaded the muzzle-loaders for them; but the moments they needed to exchange the fired small-arms proved fatal. Soon the foremost were wounded; they fell from the ladders or tried to come down, causing confusion. But repeatedly the soldiers climbed the ladders anew and continued the fight. When at last a company armed with breech-loading [[beaumont-geweer|Beaumont rifles]] had climbed the wall and opened rapid fire, the Acehnese had to give way so that the soldiers could jump inside, and the missigit was soon in Dutch hands.

## The Death of Köhler

That same morning the supreme commander arrived at the missigit, "where the chief of staff informed him of the situation. He then went with the various authorities to the east side of the missigit, on the plateau, from which one could best survey the ground and observe the enemy's position. A few moments later General Köhler was mortally wounded and gave up the ghost almost immediately." The bullet was probably fired by a sharpshooter from a position that the Dutch had not had time to clear. About five o'clock on the same day, which passed in continual fighting with the enemy, the second commander, Colonel [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|van Daalen]], arrived at the missigit to take over supreme command.

## Aftermath and Significance

The double battle of the missigit was the high-water mark of the first expedition. The Dutch had carried the fortified mosque, but at the cost of their commander and of such losses — and such disillusionment as to the seriousness of Acehnese resistance — that the failure of the attempted stormings of the Kraton's outer works on [[16-april|16 April]] [[1873]] and the council of war of [[18-april|18 April]] [[1873]] that decided against renewed attack sealed the fate of the expedition. The transport fleet left the roadstead of Atjeh on [[29-april|29 April]] [[1873]].

The lessons of [[10-april|10 April]] and [[14-april|14 April]] were not lost on the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second expedition]] of [[november|November]]/[[december|December]] [[1873]] under [[Jan van Swieten|van Swieten]]: all troops were equipped with breech-loading [[beaumont-geweer|Beaumont rifles]], the artillery was considerably reinforced, the scaling ladders and engineer tools were landed with the first wave, and the total strength was raised to more than 8,000 men.
