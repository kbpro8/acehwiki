**Meudaroh** was the Achehnese practice of assembling to recite the Quran in the *meunasah* of the gampong, often extending over the months of [[Sha'ban]] and [[Ramadhan]], and culminating in a festive celebration of the completion of the Sacred Book. The custom was closely related to the *meutamat* (or *peutamat daroh*)—the celebration of the conclusion of the thirty parts of the Quran by a special meal and accompanying prayers, *dhikr*s and *ratib*s. Where a party assembled together to recite the Quran, it was customary to celebrate the completion in somewhat festive wise; for this occasion there were special prayers, dikrs and ratibs, and a special meal. Feasts of this sort were held in every *meunasah*, and the time chosen was less dependent on whether the thirty parts of the Quran had been exactly completed than on whether the time would suit the people of the gampong and their guests.

The people of the entire [[mukim]] were not invited to this *peutamat daroh* (as they were to the [[kanduri Mo'lot]]), but only those of the gampongs in the immediate neighbourhood. Some days beforehand, the authorities of the gampong began collecting contributions for the feast. As with the [[Kanduri|kanduri]] in general, the meal was preceded by Quran recitation, this time the completion of the Sacred Book—*meutamat*—concluded by the *khatam*-prayer which properly served to mark the termination of a complete recitation. For this purpose *mukadam*s (little books, each of which contained one of the thirty divisions or *juz'* of the Quran) were distributed among the more learned of the guests as they entered the house.

Meudaroh sessions were a regular feature of the long evenings of the fasting month, when the devout gathered in the meunasah after the evening prayer to recite portions of the Quran, often in shifts that allowed the entire text to be completed before the end of Ramadhan. The closing session—the *peutamat daroh* proper—was the high point: a small but ceremonious kanduri, accompanied by the special *ratib*s and *dhikr*s, and concluded by the *khatam*-prayer. Such gatherings were particularly associated with the season of greatest devotion; they brought the community together around the act of reading, and they provided a focus for the piety of those who could not themselves read but were eager to share in the merit of the recitation.

The practice also had a domestic dimension. Throughout the first ten days after a death, it was customary in Acheh to have a portion of the Quran chanted (*beuet di jeurat*) every morning and afternoon, either at the grave or in the house where the death took place. Rich people hired a number of persons for this purpose, but as a general rule it was performed by one only—again the [[teungku]], who had a sort of adat-claim to be so employed. The fee for these ten days was about two dollars, besides two meals daily, for after each recitation the reciters came and sought refreshment in the house. Through these manifold uses, the practice of *meudaroh*—the collective recitation of the Quran—wove together the annual cycle of the religious year and the personal cycle of mourning, providing a structure of devotion common to both.
