The **Padri War on Sumatra's East Coast**, c. [[1824]]–[[1828]] and beyond — referred to in the Dutch sources printed by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] as the depredations of the *Padries* or *Rinchis* — was the eastward spread of the militant Islamic reform movement from the Minangkabau highlands to the states of Sumatra's East Coast, including [[Deli]], [[Batu Bara]], [[Tanah Putih]], [[Balah]] and [[Panei]]. Van der Kemp's study mentions the movement chiefly in connection with the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|Sultan of Deli's appeal]] to Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of [[27 August]] [[1824]], and as the backdrop to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Deli affair]] and the growing Dutch intervention on Sumatra's East Coast that would, after Van der Kemp's period, culminate in the Dutch conquest of the coast and the long [[Aceh War]].

## The Padri movement

The Padris (Dutch: *Padries*; Malay: *Rinchis*) were Islamic reformers, strongly influenced by Wahhabi ideas then spreading through the archipelago after the Mecca pilgrimage, who in the early years of the nineteenth century had launched a religious war in the Minangkabau highlands of west-central Sumatra. By the early 1820s their activity had begun to spill eastward towards the Straits of Malacca, threatening the small Malay principalities of the East Coast that had hitherto lived under the loose suzerainty of [[Acheh]] and in commercial contact with the British settlement of [[Penang]].

## The Sultan of Deli's report (August 1824)

The principal notice of the East-Coast Padri movement in Van der Kemp's study is contained in the [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|letter of the Sultan of Deli]] to Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]] of [[27 August]] [[1824]] (printed as Appendix 6). The Sultan reported that "many of the districts of Pulo Puchee" (i.e. [[Sumatra]]) were in a very disturbed state, "for the Rinchis ([[\|Padries]]) have determined to attack all the different states and render them tributary, to become obedient to all their peculiar laws and orders." Among the states already reduced to subjection and rendered tributary were [[Tanah Putih]], [[Balah]] and [[Panei]] — and the Padries had now expressed their determination to attack [[Batu Bara]] and [[Deli]] itself.

The Sultan of Deli's letter shows that the Padri advance had, by mid-[[1824]], reached the very threshold of the principal East-Coast states. The Sultan declared that "none of us Rajahs in this quarter will consent to obey them", and that he would proceed to [[Batu Bara]] himself if hostilities commenced. He begged the British Governor for instructions as to how assistance might be obtained — a request that, given the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Anglo-Dutch treaty of 17 March 1824]] recently signed at London, the British were in no position to grant, the whole of Sumatra having been declared outside the British sphere.

## Connection with the Deli affair

The Padri threat forms the immediate background to the [[Said Akil's Deli adventure (1824-1828)|Deli affair]] of [[1824]]–[[1828]]. [[Said Akil]]'s marriage to the [[Sultan of Deli]]'s daughter c. 1824, and his installation as *Raja Mooda* (deputy ruler) of Deli after the Sultan's death, took place while the state was already menaced by the Padris. Akil's depredations, of which the [[Shahbandar of Deli]] complained to Governor Fullerton in early [[1828]], compounded the disorder produced by the Padri advance, and the inability of either the British government of [[Penang]] or the Dutch colonial government at [[Batavia]] to police the East Coast left the Malay traders of [[Batu Bara]] and the surrounding states to fend for themselves.

## Significance

Van der Kemp's mention of the Padri movement is brief but pointed. The [[Sultan of Deli's letter to Penang|letter of 27 August 1824]] demonstrates that already in [[1824]] the East-Coast states were begging for European intervention against the Padries, and that the British — having just signed the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|treaty of London]] — were unable or unwilling to provide it. The way was thus opened for the Dutch to take up the task. Within a few years the Dutch colonial government would launch military expeditions against the Padris, both in the Minangkabau highlands (the Padri War proper, c. 1821–1837) and on the East Coast, where Dutch posts were established at [[Batu Bara]], [[Deli]] and elsewhere. The Padri movement, Van der Kemp suggests, was one of the several threads — together with the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Achehnese succession dispute]], the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Raffles-Acheh convention]] of 1819, and the subsequent [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|London treaty]] — that drew the colonial powers deeper into Sumatran affairs and ultimately set the stage for the long Aceh conflict of the late nineteenth century.
