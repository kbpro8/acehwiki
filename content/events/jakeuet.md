The **jakeuet** (Achehnese, from the Arabic *zakāt*) is the religious tithe on property, especially on the rice harvest, by which every Achehnese householder discharges the obligation of almsgiving that constitutes one of the five pillars of Islam. According to the Shafi'ite law, which is pretty literally interpreted on this point, the tithe amounts to one-tenth of the harvested grain; after the cleansing and measuring (*sukat*) of the padi, those who faithfully observe their religious duties set apart this tenth as jakeuet. The tax is intended for the same eight classes of recipients named in the Quran — the poor, the needy, the collectors and distributors, debtors, poor travellers, slaves seeking to purchase their freedom, converts to Islam, and "those in the path of God."

In Acheh the administration of the jakeuet departs considerably from the letter of the law. The *amils* (collectors and distributors) are represented by the teungkus of the meunasahs, but the adat confers on them no right to collect the tax by force; as a rule the teungku waits for the share that is brought to his house, or has his portion fetched home from the rice-field if notice has been given him of the completion of the harvest. Measures of compulsion are resorted to only in districts where some ulama or other representative of religion has for the time being gained the upper hand, or where the chiefs retain a share of the jakeuet for themselves. "Poor" and "needy," in the Achehnese understanding of the law, are usually synonymous with *ulama* or *teungku*, and the other classes fare variously: debtors seldom enjoy any share, poor travellers (*meusapi*) occasionally get something from the teungku in whose meunasah they find temporary lodging, and converts (*mualaph*) go about begging through the whole country after the harvest — a practice formerly constituting the chief means of subsistence of deserters from the Dutch forces in Acheh.

The eighth class — the "way of God" (*sab'il Allah*) share — has had a peculiar political importance in Acheh. According to the letter of the Shafi'ite law, the jakeuet should be distributed in equal shares among the several classes; but in the last twenty years of which Snouck Hurgronje writes (1882–92), this portion of the jakeuet became the mainstay of the constantly increasing power of the ulama party. The diversion of the sab'il Allah share to the [[Holy War (Jihad) against the Dutch|holy war against the Dutch]] supplied the material means by which [[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] and his cooperators financed the *prang sabi*: each energetic ulama travelled to and fro in his sphere of influence, gathering in the *ka sadi* and other contributions to meet the expenses of the war. The jakeuet thus ceased to be merely a religious tax and became, in effect, the revenue of a rival government.

In the same period the secularisation of taxation in Mohammedan countries generally — the *maks* or impious imposts branded by the religious law — has made the jakeuet appear as a voluntary contribution even where it is still collected. In Acheh, where the Sultan's government had long ceased to exercise effective fiscal power, this appearance of voluntariness served the ulama party well; it left the teungku to gather what he could, and converted the surplus into the sinews of war.
