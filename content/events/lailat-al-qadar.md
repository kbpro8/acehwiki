**Lailat al-Qadar** (the "Night of [Divine] Decree") was, in the Achehnese as in the wider Mohammedan tradition, the night on which, it was said, the eternal Quran was sent down by Allah to the world below, to be finally revealed to Mohammad piece by piece through the agency of the Archangel Gabriel. It was generally believed that on the day—or rather night—which formed the anniversary of this great event, the whole creation felt its influence. On this special night, no less than on that of the middle of [[Shaʿban]] (the [[Malam beureu'at]]), all manner of rich blessings were supposed to be dealt forth by Allah to those who kept vigil therein, wakeful and, if possible, engaged in pious devotions.

At the present time, however, no one could fix the date with certainty. The sole rule was the prevalent idea that the night of the qadar was one of the last five odd-numbered nights of the fasting month—that is, the nights preceding the 21st, 23rd, 25th, 27th or 29th of [[Ramadhan]]. A weighty reason was found herein for devoting all of them to devotional exercises. These nights were in all Mohammedan countries spent by the devout in recitations from the Quran and other such-like devotions. Here and there certain superstitious practices were resorted to by the people for the purpose of drawing down upon their own heads the blessings of the qadar night; in Java, feasts known as *maliman*s were given on these nights by princes and other persons of distinction to a multitude of guests.

Among the five nights, two disputed with each other in the general estimation the right to the name of qadar night with a greater show of probability than the other three: viz. the 21st and the 27th. The 21st (*malam salikur*) enjoyed this preference throughout a great portion of Java, a preference which displayed itself principally in popular rejoicings. In Acheh, however, it was on the night before the 27th—the [[Malam dua ploh tujoh]]—that the greatest animation was displayed. Before every housedoor was set a lighted lamp with seven mouths, or "eyes" as the Achehnese called them; the young amused themselves by letting off crackers (*beudèe China*, i.e. "little Chinese guns"); and at sunset persons of substance brought complete *idang*s to the *meunasah*, so that on this occasion the celebrants of the feast might enjoy a hearty meal instead of the preliminary mess of pottage with which they usually broke their fast.

The tradition had it that during the qadar night the very trees bowed to the ground in awe in the direction of the *kiblat*, that is to say toward the Sacred Mosque at Mecca. This was firmly believed by the Achehnese, though with the restriction that the phenomenon was rarely visible to the eyes of ordinary mortals. The young folk, however, made expeditions on the night of the 27th "to seek trees doing obeisance" (*taja mita kayèe sujud*), an expression which, Snouck Hurgronje cautions, must not be taken too seriously. The festival was also connected with the wider belief that the *jinn* were chained up during the fasting month, and thus powerless to harm mankind; during the [[Puasa]], the fear of going about in the dark was reduced to a minimum, and the night of the qadar was the most blessed of all the nights of the fast.
