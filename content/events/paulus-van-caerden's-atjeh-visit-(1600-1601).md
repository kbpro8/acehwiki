**Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit of 1600-1601** — the second Dutch commercial mission to the [[Acheh|kingdom of Atjeh]], conducted by Vice-Admiral **[[Paulus van Caerden|Paulus van Caerden]]** of the [[Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie|Nieuwe Brabantsche Compagnie]] fleet of [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] with two ships, anchored off Atjeh from [[21 September]] [[1600]] to January [[1601]] — is the episode that produced the first formal Dutch-Achehnese pepper contract, the release of the surviving [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|Houtman captives]], and, by Van Caerden's seizure of pepper from Arab junks on [[12 January]] [[1601]], the first armed clash between the Dutch and the Atjeh shore batteries. The visit is the central episode of Johan van der Woude's chapter on Atjeh in his [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## Background and arrival

Van Caerden had been sent by his admiral [[Pieter Both|Pieter Both]] to the west coast of Sumatra, where he visited [[Priaman]], [[Tikoe]] and [[Pasaman]] "zonder resultaat" — pepper was scarce everywhere on the west coast in that season. Deciding to try Atjeh for cargo, "hoewel hij wist wat [[Cornelis de Houtman|De Houtman]] daar was overkomen", he arrived off the roadstead on [[21 September]] [[1600]]. He was met by the same **Franciscan monk** who had been pressing the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]] to cede his strongest fort to the Portuguese — the monk whose clumsy intrigue had helped turn the sultan against the Dutch after the Houtman affair. Van Caerden returned the monk's gifts and had the refreshments he sent first tasted before eating.

The sultan, on Van Caerden's arrival, set aside unread the letter from [[Prince Maurits|Prince Maurits]] — Van Caerden had heard the sultan complained it was written on pigskin — but released **[[Hans Decker|Hans Decker]]** and the two other Dutchmen still held from the [[Houtman brothers' visit to Atjeh (1599)|1599 attack]]. The release was a calculated gesture, designed to show the new arrivals the sultan's good disposition and to balance the Portuguese interest at court.

## The contract of 28 December 1600

Negotiations were opened. The sultan at first signed a contract, then tore it up when the Dutch had Hans Decker translate the text and discovered discrepancies with what had been agreed. On [[28 December]] [[1600]] a new agreement was reached, fixing the price, quantity, delivery time and export duties, and providing that "geen koopluijden ofte schippers van watt natie die ook zouden mogen zijn en zullen geen peper mogen koopen offte laeden, voor en all eer de Hollanders hare ladinge bekomen hebben". Van Caerden unwisely agreed to advance part of the payment for the delivery — a concession the Zeeland merchants later criticised — although the escaped Dutch prisoners had already warned him that the sultan planned an attack and that they themselves had been sent to Pedir to summon the Atjeh fleet down.

## The flight of Frederik de Houtman and the seizure of the Arab junks

On [[30 December]] [[1600]], [[Frederik de Houtman|Frederik de Houtman]] and three companions escaped from Pedir in a small prauw, having fashioned their own sail and oars. They reached Van Caerden's ship on New Year's Eve. Houtman offered to return to shore to keep relations smooth during the wait for the new pepper crop; the sultan granted him his freedom, but on his return to land he was seized on a fabricated theft charge at the instigation of the sabander of [[Pedir]], chained, and taken back to Pedir in irons with three companions. Van Caerden, fearing that the sultan would not deliver the pepper for which he had paid in advance, decided to take pepper by force from the Arab junks at anchor on the roadstead. On [[12 January]] [[1601]] he carried out the seizure; the shore batteries answered with cannon fire; Van Caerden, after warning that the fire must cease, set fire to a number of small coastal craft, including the boat of the Portuguese Franciscan monk.

Van Caerden's act has been the subject of controversy. The Zeeland merchants [[Gerard le Roy|Gerard le Roy]] and [[Laurens Bicker|Laurens Bicker]], who arrived later in [[1601]], conceded that the sultan's conduct gave ground for suspicion but argued that Van Caerden had exceeded his commission, since the contract gave him a monopoly only on the new crop and not on cargo already in port. They undertook to compensate the Arab claimants and reached an agreement with the [[Nieuwe Compagnie|Nieuwe Compagnie]] in [[1602]] for the payment of ƒ 51,048 in damages — a sum that, according to [[Francisco Pyrard|Francisc Pyrard]], was still unpaid in [[1608]] and, per [[P. A. Tiele|Tiele]], still pending in [[1617]]. Van der Woude defends Van Caerden: "Van Caerden was gealarmeerd door de berichten van de ontvluchte Hollanders en wantrouwde den vorst, indachtig [[Frederik de Houtman|De Houtman]]'s waarschuwing. Bovendien was hij er op bedacht waar te ontvangen voor het voorgeschoten geld."

## Departure and aftermath

Van Caerden released the sixty prisoners he had taken from the Arab junks — he did not wish to make enemies of their compatriots — sailed to **[[Poelau Way|Poelau Way]]** to wait a few days for an answer from the sultan, and then set course for [[Bantam]] by way of the west coast of Sumatra, taking on no cargo. Van Caerden "liet nu de Hollanders van land terugroepen en besloot, bevreesd dat geen levering zou volgen voor de voorgeschoten gelden, peper te lichten uit jonken van Arabische kooplieden, die op de reede lagen." The visit thus ended in a strategic retreat: the first Dutch-Achehnese contract had been signed, the surviving Houtman captives had been freed, but the pepper monopoly had not been obtained and the Dutch-Achehnese relationship was at its lowest point.

## Significance

Paulus van Caerden's Atjeh visit is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal instance of the difficulty the early Dutch companies faced in operating at a court where the sultan's balancing policy was at once the source of commercial opportunity and the obstacle to monopoly. The visit produced the first written Dutch-Achehnese contract, the first release of Dutch captives from Atjeh, and the first armed clash between Dutch ships and Atjeh shore batteries — three "firsts" that together frame the visit as the pivot on which the early Dutch-Atjeh relationship turned. Van Caerden's seizure of the Arab junks also established the legal-political pattern that would recur throughout the Dutch-Atjeh relationship: aggressive commercial action by a Dutch commander, controversy at home about whether his instructions permitted it, and a long-running compensation claim that dragged on for years. The visit's outcome — no monopoly, no cargo, and a damaged relationship — also showed the bewindhebbers that no single fleet could secure the Atjeh trade, a lesson that fed directly into the formation of the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|united VOC]] in [[1602]].
