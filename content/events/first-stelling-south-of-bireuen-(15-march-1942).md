**The first stelling south of Bireuën**, fought on [[15 March]] [[15 March 1942|15 March 1942]] at a position about 10 km south of [[Bireuën|Bireuën]] behind a river, was the only set-piece Dutch infantry engagement of the central-Atjeh theatre of the [[Japanese invasion of Atjèh|Japanese invasion of March 1942]] at which the Japanese suffered *aanzienlijke verliezen* — approximately 40 killed — for no Dutch loss. In the [[Nederlands-Indië contra Japan|Dutch staff history's]] account (Deel VI, Hoofdstuk V), the engagement is the staff history's principal example of a successful Dutch tactical defence undone by *munitie-uitputting* and by the premature blowing of a *wegvernieling* behind the stelling.

## The three-stelling defence of the Bireuën–Takengon road

[[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Palmer van den Broek]]'s three-stelling defence of the [[Bireuën|Bireuën]]–[[Takengon|Takengon]] road was the operational plan for the *3de hoofdgroep* — the central-Atjeh Dutch troops — in mid-March [[1942]]. The first stelling, about 10 km south of Bireuën behind a river, was held by three brigades of the IIIde divisie Maréchaussee under Lt [[W. van Pesch|Van Pesch]], with four machine guns and two pieces of light field artillery. The stelling's orders were "de vijand zoveel mogelijk verliezen toe te brengen en voor overmacht op Takengon terug te trekken."

The second stelling, 25 km further back, was held by five brigades under Captain [[F. Wetzels|Wetzels]] (IIIde divisie); the third, halfway between Bireuën and Takengon, by four brigades of the Vde divisie under Captain [[L. A. Boucherie|Boucherie]].

## The 15 March 1942 engagement

In the morning of [[15 March]] [[15 March 1942|15 March 1942]] Van Pesch received word of the Japanese nadering from Bireuën — the Japanese having occupied Bireuën on [[13 March|13]]–[[14 March]] [[14 March 1942|14 March 1942]] with about 1,200 troops. Van Pesch obtained permission to hold the demolition of the bridge himself until 13.00.

In the course of the morning — no Japanese had yet been observed — a panic broke out in the detachment "om onverklaarbare redenen, waardoor een aantal militairen op de vlucht sloeg." Van Pesch was at the telephone. As he came outside he saw the European sergeant [[Haveman|Haveman]] standing on the *rijweg* with his karabijn in the aanslag, calling to the fleeing men that he would shoot any who did not return. "Dit krachtige optreden is de oorzaak geweest, dat de paniek snel en volledig werd bedwongen." Van Pesch held *appèl*, addressed the troops, and every man returned to his post.

At 10.30 an Atjehnese was observed spying out the stelling. As the enemy could now be expected at any moment, Van Pesch ordered the bridge — whose demolition had been prepared in haste — to be blown. "Deze operatie gelukte boven verwachting." The two pieces of light field artillery, originally assigned to engage the bridge with grape-shot in case the prepared charges failed, were now available against the enemy. Van Pesch's request to use the 90 available grenades against the enemy was — "om onbegrijpelijke redenen" — refused by the Territoriaal Commandant at Takengon.

At approximately 17.00 the stelling was alarmed by the approach of two cars, which emerged from a *kampongrand* at 3 km distance and drove over the straight road toward the bridge. The Dutch opened fire when the cars were at 300 metres' range and inflicted "aanzienlijke verliezen (ongeveer 40 man)." The enemy received reinforcements and the engagement flared up again. The Dutch *geest*, "nu zij succes hadden geboekt," was good: the machine-gun crew "vertoonden zich zelfs boven hun dekkingen, om met water uit petroleumblikken de gloeiende lopen van hun wapens af te koelen." After 15 minutes, however, the *munitie* of the machine guns was practically exhausted.

At 18.00 Van Pesch went to the telephone post to brief his chiefs; the telephone crew had fled during the fight. At 19.00 he decided to break off the engagement owing to darkness, the enemy being able to outflank the stelling unseen. The cars had been moved owing to enemy fire; after a kilometre's march they were recovered and the detachment drove to a kampong 10 km south of the stelling to bivak.

At 21.00 Van Pesch heard a heavy explosion: Dutch troops had prematurely blown a prepared *wegvernieling* some kilometres further on, breaking both the road and the telephone link to Takengon. The troops, "niettegenstaande de genomen veiligheidsmaatregelen, bevreesd waren voor een nachtelijke omsingeling," concluded that the Takengon command had abandoned them. The staff history notes that "het niet bekend is, wie de opdracht tot het uitvoeren van de vernieling heeft gegeven."

## The subsequent guerrilla (16 March – 1 April 1942)

On the morning of [[16 March]] [[16 March 1942|16 March 1942]] Van Pesch had the cars — now useless, the road being blown — destroyed, and marched the detachment to a secret *opslagplaats van vivres* in the forest west of the road. There he ordered the detachment to split into groups of two or three men, each with its own area, to begin the guerrilla along the *rijweg* "zoals tevoren in vredestijd reeds was beoefend." The brigadecommandanten were each given a gebied; a *samenkomst* was agreed for a fixed day at a fixed place.

Between [[16 March]] [[16 March 1942|16 March]] and [[1 April]] [[1 April 1942|1 April 1942]] Van Pesch and sergeant [[Haveman|Haveman]] — the latter having joined the lieutenant on [[22 March]] [[22 March 1942|22 March 1942]] — successfully laid several ambushes against advancing Japanese, "waarbij oprukkende Japanners werden beschoten." The Japanese, often accompanied by Atjehnese porters, were unable to deploy. By [[1 April]] [[1 April 1942|1 April 1942]] the indigenous militairen of the Van Pesch group had deserted; Van Pesch and Haveman surrendered some days later at a *koffieonderneming* near Takengon, after learning of [[Major W. F. Palmer van den Broek|Palmer van den Broek]]'s capitulation order.

## Significance

The first stelling south of Bireuën is, in the staff history's Atjeh chapter, the single clear Dutch tactical success of the central-Atjeh campaign: an engagement in which the Japanese suffered *aanzienlijke verliezen* for no Dutch loss, and in which the Dutch troops — once Haveman had steadied them — fought with credit. The staff history's contrast between the Van Pesch stelling and the panic that overtook the noordkust troops under [[C. J. Bekkers|Bekkers]] is the compilers' principal evidence that the central-Atjeh troops, when competently led, were still capable of effective resistance. The *munitie-uitputting* and the premature *wegvernieling* are the staff history's principal illustrations of the logistical and command failures that undid the stelling despite its tactical success.
