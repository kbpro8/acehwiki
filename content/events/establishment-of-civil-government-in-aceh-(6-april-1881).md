**The Establishment of Civil Government in Aceh** on [[6 April]] [[6 April]] [[1881]] was the separation of the civil and military commands in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] by the Governor-General [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]] and the appointment of [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|A. Pruys van der Hoeven]] as civil governor of the gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden, with Colonel [[J. F. Haus|J. F. Haus]] taking the military command. The establishment of civil government was preceded by the administrative reorganisation drafted by the commission of [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]] and Pruys van der Hoeven in [[1880]]-[[1881]], and by the mission of [[T. H. der Kinderen|T. H. der Kinderen]] to Atjeh in [[November 1880|November 1880]]-[[February 1881|February 1881]] for the reorganisation of justice in the buitenbezittingen. It was one of the last administrative acts of Van Lansberge, who had himself requested discharge for health reasons.

## Background

After the close of large-scale operations in Great Acheh in [[August 1879|August 1879]] the Dutch government initiated the definitive administrative reorganisation. [[K. van der Heyden|Van der Heyden]] was placed in commission with [[A. Pruys van der Hoeven|A. Pruys van der Hoeven]], last resident of [[Palembang|Palembang]], as government commissioners. They were charged, in joint consultation, "to arrange and propose everything necessary to arrive at an adequate organisation of the gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden, in connection with the political situation and the relation of the various Onderhoorigheden to our administration." The assistant-resident of Groot-Atjeh was attached to them as secretary for that assignment.

Pruys van der Hoeven arrived in Atjeh on [[21 February]] [[21 February]] [[1880]] and visited in succession the territory occupied by the Dutch, both in Great Acheh and in the vazal staten. On [[10 July]] [[10 July]] the commissioners declared their task ended. After in an extraordinary meeting of the [[Council of Netherlands India|Raad of Nederlandsch-Indië]] with both commissioners a further discussion had taken place, and the Governor-General had taken several decisions in preparation, the governor was yet charged with the submission of new proposals regarding the organisation of the inland administration in Great Acheh, based on an investigation into the existing situation and the institutions under the sultans. He was to consult with the member of the Raad of Nederlandsch-Indië, [[T. H. der Kinderen|Mr. T. H. der Kinderen]], who would also come to Atjeh as government commissioner for the reorganisation of justice in the possessions outside Java and Madura, to draft proposals concerning the regulation of justice there. Der Kinderen arrived in Atjeh in [[November 1880]] and remained there until [[February 1881]].

## The Administrative Organisation of March 1881

In accordance with the proposals submitted, the definitive administrative organisation of Atjeh came into force in [[March 1881|March 1881]]. According to this organisation, the administration of the gewest Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden would rest with a governor, whose residence would be at [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]]. Under him would stand three assistant-residents and ten controleurs. The assistant-residencies were:

1. that of [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] with [[Kota Radja|Kota Radja]] as chief place, in which direct Dutch administration was introduced;
2. that of the [[Noord- en Oostkust|Noord- en Oostkust]] with [[Telok Semawe|Telok Semawé]] as chief place, with controleurs at [[Edi|Edi]], [[Samalanga|Samalanga]] and [[Segli|Segli]];
3. that of the [[Westkust van Atjeh|Westkust van Atjeh]] with [[Malaboeh|Malaboeh]] as chief place.

To the landscapes belonging to the two last afdeelingen, self-government was left. In Groot-Atjeh the inland administration would be conducted by district-heads or [[Uleebalang|hoeloebalangs]], to be appointed by the Governor-General; their replacements and helpers, as well as the moekim-heads, imams, were appointed by the governor; the replacements and helpers of the imams, as well as the kampong-heads, ketjihiks, by the assistant-resident; the heads of the wards, mandarsé's, by the controleur.

## The Separation of Commands

By the Governor-General's decision, by virtue of authority already granted by the King in [[1879]], it was decided to separate the civil and military commands and to entrust the civil administration in Atjeh to a civil governor. The Lieutenant-general Van der Heyden was honourably discharged from his functions, and Pruys van der Hoeven was appointed civil governor; the military command was entrusted to Colonel [[J. F. Haus|J. F. Haus]] of the infantry. Both assumed their functions on [[6 April]] [[6 April]] [[1881]].

## Significance

The discharge to Van der Heyden — "which set many pens in motion, and also gave rise to discussions in the [[Staten-Generaal|States-General]]" — was one of the last administrative acts of Governor-General Van Lansberge. For health reasons he had requested discharge in the spring of [[1881]], which was granted by Royal Decree of [[24 November]] [[24 November]] [[1880]]; on [[12 April]] [[12 April]] [[1881]] he handed over his dignity to his successor. The establishment of civil government marked the formal transition of Atjeh from a military command to a regular administrative gewest of Netherlands India — though the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] was by no means over, and was to continue for more than two decades in the form of guerrilla resistance, especially in the upper lands of [[Pedir|Pedir]] and the [[XXII Moekims|XXII Moekims]].

## Account from *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* ([[1923]])

The **introduction of civil government in Atjeh** in [[1881]] — the resolution of [[11 March]] [[1881]] replacing Lieutenant-General [[General van der Heijden|Karel van der Heijden]] by the civilian Governor [[A. Pruijs van der Hoeven]], effective [[6 April]] [[1881]] — was the Netherlands Indian Government's answer to General Van der Heijden's declaration of [[1879]] that the war had ended and the time of [[Pacification of Atjeh|pacification]] and restoration had arrived; within three years the experiment collapsed, and by [[13 September]] [[1884]] military and civil authority were again united under Colonel [[Demmeni|H. Demmeni]].

### The resolution of 1881

Using the authorization granted the year before by the Supreme Government — leaving it to the Governor-General when he should deem it desirable to entrust the civil policy in Atjeh to a civil governor — the Landvoogd determined by resolution of 11 March [[1881]] that a chief official with the title of governor should henceforth head the province. Lieutenant-General K. van der Heijden was by the same resolution honourably discharged; in his place came A. Pruijs van der Hoeven, last employed for the organisation of Atjeh and before that resident of [[Palembang]], with Colonel of Infantry [[J. F. Haus]] as military commandant. Both took office on 6 April 1881. Governor-General [[Johan Willem van Lansberge|Van Lansberge]] told Van der Heijden that thereby "the crown was set upon the building" — but, Langhout remarks, "all too soon it would show that 'the crown on the building' did not fit", and that the walls raised by the Indian army's heroism could not yet bear the crown's weight; for it to fit, thirty-seven years more had to elapse (until [[1918]]).

### Legal and police organization

By ordinance of [[14 March]] [[1881]] the province was brought under the jurisdiction of the *Raad van Justitie* (Court of Justice) at [[Padang]]; a regulation of the administration of justice in Atjeh was fixed, and the judicature for the native population of Groot-Atjeh was further regulated: that population for the time being kept its own jurisdiction, the district chiefs and the *moesapats* (native courts, composed of native chiefs and presided over by the head of the sub-department) continuing to judge according to the institutions and customs existing in Groot-Atjeh, insofar as these did not conflict with the general principles of justice. The corps of "gewapende politiedienaren" (armed police officers) — instituted in Atjeh by way of trial, partly mounted, armed with Beaumont rifles — was to contribute to the securing of safety; whether the trial succeeded in every respect, Langhout doubts. Plunder and robbery steadily increased; attacks of robber bands on Chinese settlements made, for a long time, the number of departing Chinese — traders as well as artisans — exceed that of arrivals.

### Works and trade measures

Road-building went on: the road from Lambaroh to Aneugalong was completed in August [[1882]]; the road to [[Glé Kambing]] was continued; a road from [[Toengkop]] to [[Montassik]] was decided upon; a bridge over the [[Kroeëng Raba]] created a regular connection with [[Lehong]]; the great transport road to Glé Kambing and the road Aneügalong–Toengkōp were wholly finished, and iron bridges were laid over the [[Atjeh River]] at Glé Kambing and Lambaroh, smaller bridges being progressively replaced by iron against breakage by the evilly disposed. The new [[Baiturrahman Grand Mosque|Missigit]] was festively opened on [[27 December]] [[1882]], with several clergy attached to it at government cost. In the course of [[1882]] the compulsory visitation at [[Oeleë Lheuë|Oelèë Lheue]] of vessels arriving in the Government was abolished, opening the whole coast of Atjeh to trade; at [[Idi]] trade revived, a Chinese temple rose with government help and the chief's gift of 500 dollars; at [[Modjopahit]] the pepper gardens were saved from abandonment by the acting assistant-resident's chastisement of the robbers and a government grant of 500 dollars (March [[1882]]) from the hacil; on the West Coast the pepper culture extended and the assistant-resident of [[Meulaboh]] explored the coals of [[Seunagan]].

### The collapse, 1883–1884

In December [[1882]] the government passed to [[P. F. Laging Tobias]], resident of Palembang (taking over [[16 March]] [[1883]]) — who had to declare the state in Atjeh "zeer ongunstig" and soon felt it "bijna geheel hopeloos" ("almost wholly hopeless"). Governor-General [['s Jacob]]'s visit, and the visit of the newly appointed Governor-General [[O. van Rees]] of [[11 April]] [[1884]], were passed over by the official documents in a speaking silence. The year 1883 became a *rapjaar*: the Atjehnese struggle took on an ever more religious character, some trusted chiefs fell away, a violent cholera epidemic made victims by thousands, and a violent earthquake shook [[Koeta Radja]] to a wide distance; by resolution of [[29 May]] [[1883]] the open-coast measure was withdrawn, trade being confined to [[Oeleë Lheuë|Oelèë Lheue]], [[Sigli]], [[Samalanga|Samelanga]], [[Lho Seumawè]], [[Idi]] and [[Meulaboh]] (with [[Tapa Toean]] to follow when an official should be posted there) — a control publicly defended, on explicit government order, as exercised without preference of any nationality. Dr. [[E. B. Kielstra]] explained the failure by the contrast between Pruijs van der Hoeven's gifted conduct of persuasion along the coasts and his failure to see that Groot-Atjeh, won by the sword, still had to be kept by the sword. The [[Nisero affair (1883–1885)|Nisero affair]] completed the discomfiture. After more than three years of civil government the Government realized that a reserved and very forceful attitude toward the Atjehnese left no place for a separate civil administration: Governor Laging Tobias was honourably relieved, and Infantry Colonel H. Demmeni accepted the administration as Civil and Military Governor on 13 September [[1884]]. "Aan het voor ons leger roemlooze en voor den handel uiterst ongunstige tijdperk van het eerste civiele bestuur op Atjeh was een einde gekomen!" — "An end had come to the period of the first civil government in Atjeh, inglorious for our army and most unfavourable for trade!"
