**Opiumregie in Atjeh** — also **Opiumregie in Atjèh** — refers to the establishment of a government monopoly over the import, preparation, and retail sale of opium in the gewest [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]], which was fully implemented by the Dutch colonial government on [[1 April]] [[1913]] (Stbl. [[1913]] N°. 219, modified by Stbl. [[1920]] N°. 235), replacing the earlier *pacht* (farm) system. This event marked the final abolition of the infamous opium *pacht* (farming) system in the [[Dutch East Indies]], extending direct government control over the narcotic to the last major territory where the system persisted. The transition to the *regie* was part of a broader colonial policy aimed at controlling consumption and generating revenue, coinciding with the final consolidation of Dutch rule following the [[Aceh War|pacification of Aceh]].

## Context and the Pacht System

For centuries, the [[Dutch East India Company|VOC]] and later the Dutch colonial government had been involved in the opium trade, viewing it primarily as a lucrative source of revenue. The right to sell opium at retail was farmed out to private entrepreneurs — predominantly Chinese — in a system known as the *pacht*. This system incentivised the farmers to maximise consumption through aggressive and often corrupt practices, leading to widespread addiction and smuggling.

By the late nineteenth century, growing moral and economic concerns, particularly in the Netherlands, led to calls for reform. In [[1893]], a government-run system, the *regie*, was introduced on [[Madoera]] as a trial. This gave the government direct control over the manufacture and retail sale of opium, aiming to reduce abuse while maintaining state income.

## Earlier opium systems in Atjèh

Before the *Opiumregie*, opium in Atjèh was managed through the *pacht* (farm) system:

- **Stbl. [[1876]] N°. 151** — introduced *opiumpacht* and *speelpacht* in Groot-Atjèh, applying the Soematra's Oostkust pachtreglement (Stbl. [[1875]] N°. 236 Bijl. C)
- **Stbl. [[1884]] N°. 168** — reorganised the *opiumpacht* for Groot-Atjèh

In the Onderhoorigheden, opium was managed through agreements with the hoofden. The *verklaringen in achttien artikelen* (after [[1877]]) ceded opium rights to the Government with *schadeloosstelling*. The [[Hasil-reglement|Hasil-reglement]] of [[1902]] governed the collection for and on behalf of the hoofden.

## The Westkust licenses

On the Westkust, before the *Opiumregie*, governors issued opium-import licenses to hoofden:

- **Generaal Van Teijn** (as Westkust governor, September [[1887]] – March [[1891]]): 619 kists at ƒ300/kist
- **Kolonel Pompe van Meerdervoort** (Van Teijn's successor, April [[1891]] – January [[1892]]): 67 kists

## Introduction of the Regie in the Outer Possessions

The *regie* was gradually rolled out across the [[Buitengewesten|Dutch East Indies outer territories]]. The Outer Possessions saw its introduction over a period of years:

- [[1897]]: [[Lombok]]
- [[1902]]: [[Lampongsche Districten]]
- [[1905]]: [[Sumatra's Westkust]], [[Tapanoeli]], [[Benkoelen]], and the places Ternate and Laboeha in Ternate en Onderhoorigheden
- [[1906]]: [[Palembang]], [[Djambi]], and Banka
- [[1907]]: Celebes en Onderhoorigheden and Menado
- [[1908]]: Zuider- en Oosterafdeeling van Borneo and Bali
- [[1909]]: Billiton, Westerafdeeling van Borneo, and Timor en Onderhoorigheden
- [[1910]]: Amboina en Onderhoorigheden
- [[1911]]: Riouw en Onderhoorigheden
- [[1912]]: [[Oostkust van Sumatra]]
- **[[1913]]: [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh and Dependencies]]**

The introduction in [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh]] in [[1913]] was significant as it was the last major area where the *pacht* system persisted. Its final replacement with the *regie* marked the end of a system long criticised by moralists and reformers in the Netherlands.

## The Regie System in Atjeh

The opium *regie* in [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh]] and across the archipelago was characterised by a geographically nuanced system of control:

1. **Free Consumption:** In some areas, particularly in major cities and older settlements, the use of opium was officially "free" for certain classes of consumers.
2. **Licensing System (*Licentie-stelsel*):** In other regions, users were required to obtain a license from the local head of government:
   - **Restricted (*beperkt*):** Use was often prohibited for the indigenous population.
   - **Unrestricted (*onbeperkt*):** Anyone could obtain a license.
3. **"Decreasing" Licenses (*Afloopend licentie-stelsel*):** In some areas, the number of licenses was deliberately reduced over time, aiming to phase out consumption entirely.
4. **Prohibited Zones (*Verboden kringen*):** Regions where opium use was entirely forbidden, most notably in areas with populations without a tradition of opium use.

In [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh]] specifically, consumption was "free" with one exception: a decreasing licensing system (*afloopend licentie-stelsel*) prevailed in the temporary sub-department of [[Gajo-Loeos]], with exceptions similar to those applied in [[Sumatra's Westkust]], reflecting the colonial administration's recognition of differing social and geographic realities across the territory.

## The verboden kringen

The *verboden kringen* (forbidden circles) where opium sale was prohibited were:

- [[Sërbödjadi|Sërbödjadi]] (from [[1911]], Stbl. N°. 280)
- [[Gajö Loeüs|Gajö Loeüs]] (from [[1913]])
- [[Alaslanden|Alaslanden]] (from [[1913]])
- [[Takéngön|Takéngön]] (added [[1920]])

In the *verboden kringen*, a license system applied, but few opium users were found there — the Gayo and Alas populations did not use opium.

## Sales points and price

By [[1920]] there were 49 sales points (down from 57 in [[1913]]). The price was raised from ƒ12.50/thail to ƒ20 in May [[1921]] (Bt. N°. 71).

## Immediate Effects and Revenue

A notable economic effect of the switch to the *regie* was an immediate, sharp increase in reported consumption figures — not because of a real increase in use, but because the government was now capturing revenue from opium that had previously been smuggled and sold illegally. The colonial government could thus turn a vice into a more controlled and transparent source of state revenue.

The financial results were significant. For [[1913]], the total revenue from the Opium *regie* across the Indies reached **10.729 million guilders**. The introduction of the *regie* in [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh]] in [[1913]] demonstrated how the colonial government monetised control over a newly consolidated region.

## Significance

The introduction of the *opiumregie* in [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Atjeh]] in [[1913]] symbolised the final consolidation of Dutch administrative control over the sultanate after decades of brutal war. As the "last bastion" of the old, exploitative financial system, the abolition of the *pacht* and its replacement by the *regie* marked a shift from military conquest to administrative and economic governance. The opium issue was a subject of intense contemporary debate in the Netherlands, with reformers arguing for total abolition and others defending the government's pragmatic approach as a lesser evil compared to the unregulated spread of alcohol. Kreemer's account, in turn, supplies the technical anatomy of the Atjèh *regie* — the *verboden kringen*, the sales points, the price increases, the earlier pacht regulations, and the Westkust license system under Van Teijn and Pompe van Meerdervoort — that allows the financial machinery of the consolidation to be studied in detail.
