The **musem tiniu** was the Achehnese name for the North-East Monsoon season, which began in keunong lhang (the tenth keunong of the Achehnese year, falling in September). The arrival of the musem tiniu marked the onset of dangerous weather for shipping between the capital [[Banda Acheh|Kuta Raja]] and the northern and eastern coasts of [[Acheh|Aceh]]. Mariners generally suspended such voyages until the succeeding [[S.W. Monsoon|musem barat]] brought favourable conditions, although the calendar contained a brief respite within the season itself.

## The interval in the monsoon

Within the musem tiniu, keunong dua ploh lhèë (the first keunong of the Achehnese year, falling in January) contained a remarkable five-to-seven-day interval during which the voyage from the capital to the northern or eastern coast could be undertaken without risk. This brief period of calmer weather was regarded as a sort of interlude in the [[N.E. Monsoon]], and was eagerly exploited by traders and travellers who had been detained for months by the stormy weather of the early monsoon.

## Other associations of the season

The opening of the musem tiniu in keunong lhang was also the moment at which the second of the four divisions (lho') of the seine-fishers of [[Ulee Lheue]] celebrated its kanduri la'ot or sea-feast, a propitiatory rite held at the commencement of each monsoon by the fishing community. The musem tiniu was therefore a season of contrasting associations: dangerous for deep-sea voyages, but marked by communal rites that bound the fishing population of the coast together in shared anticipation of the season's challenges.

## Place in the Achehnese calendar

Like the other major divisions of the Achehnese year, the musem tiniu was reckoned by reference to the keunongs—the twelve (sometimes thirteen) annual markers which combined Muslim month-names with a fixed numbering system independent of the Hijrah calendar. The Achehnese recognised that Allah's established custom was to bring definite states of weather in each keunong, although pious teachers admonished their fellow-countrymen against treating the keunongs themselves as the cause of drought and rain.
