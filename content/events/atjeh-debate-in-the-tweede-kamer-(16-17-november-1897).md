**The Atjeh debate in the Tweede Kamer** was the two-day parliamentary confrontation over the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] held at [['s-Gravenhage]] on Tuesday [[16 November]] and Wednesday [[17 November]] [[1897]], during the general deliberations on the [[Indische begrooting voor 1898|budget of Nederlandsch-Indië for 1898]]. It was opened by the newly elected social-democratic deputy for Enschedé, [[H. van Kol]], with a comprehensive historical indictment of Dutch policy in [[Acheh|Atjeh]] since [[1824]], and closed on the second day with the rejection of [[Van Kol's motion of 16 November 1897|his motion]] for an impartial commission of enquiry by seventy-two votes to three. The debate is preserved in full in the pamphlet *[[Atjeh in de Tweede Kamer (1897)]]*, published by the [[Sociaaldemokratische Arbeiderspartij]].

The debate was the first occasion on which the newly formed socialist group put the legitimacy of the twenty-four-year war before the chamber as a whole, and the last major parliamentary discussion of the war before the appointment of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] as governor. It ranged over the origins of the war, the conduct of Dutch troops, the mortality of the garrison, the economic interests bound up in the conflict, the reach of Dutch sovereignty on the Acehnese coast, and the question of whether the war could ever be ended at all.

## The first sitting: Van Kol's indictment

[[H. van Kol|Van Kol]] opened by declaring that no reform in the Indies could come to anything unless "die kanker aan de welvaart van Indië" — see [[Atjeh-kanker]] — were cut out with a firm hand. He recalled that the sitting minister had himself conceded on [[13 November]] [[1896]] that he would rather economise on every point than on Atjeh, and that "voor den Atjeh-oorlog moeten tijdelijk alle belangen zwichten" (for the Atjeh war all interests must temporarily give way). Such words, Van Kol said, must awaken pain in everyone who wished well to the Javaan: the thousands of hectares of arable land of the Solo-vallei were to be left to wither with drought, the plains of Tegal and [[Bagelen]] to be ravaged by floods, the seaports to become unusable, and in [[Bantam]] the native to wrestle with sickness and disaster, until the Dutch "annexatie-fanatisme" should be satisfied.

He set the war in the context of a general European [[Annexatie-geest|annexation spirit]] of the previous thirty years, naming the partition of Africa at [[Berlin]], the quarrels of Russia and [[Japan]] over [[Korea]], of the Japanese and Americans over Hawaï, the pressure of [[England]], [[Germany]], [[France]], Austria and Russia on [[Egypt]] and the Balkans, the conflicts in Tunis, Achter-Indië and the Niger region, the watch of England and Russia on the [[Afghanistan|Afghan]] frontier, the overwhelming of [[Madagascar|Madagaskar]], and the rivalries in [[Sudan|Soedan]], Senegal, [[Abyssinia|Abyssinië]] and Ethiopië.

He then examined the three alleged causes of the war — piracy, the incessant wars among the Acehnese statelets, and the danger of foreign intervention — and rejected each in turn; the argument is set out in detail at [[Van Kol's refutation of the causes of the Atjeh-oorlog (1897)]]. He rehearsed the history of Dutch encroachment on Acehnese territory from the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London]] to the [[Sumatra Tractaat (1871)|treaty of Sumatra]] and the declaration of war of [[26 March]] [[1873]]; recited the passage from [[E. B. Kielstra|Major Kielstra]]'s *[[Kielstra's Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog (1883-1885)|Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog]]*, volume I, page 27, conceding that the Acehnese had ample reason to distrust the Dutch; and condemned the mission of the [[Vice-President van den Raad van Indië|Vice-President of the Council of the Indies]], a man antipathetic to the sultan as the author of the [[Siak Tractaat (1858)|Siak treaty]], who was required to demand recognition of the sovereignty of the King of the Netherlands within twenty-four hours.

Van Kol dwelt at length on the consequences: the unknown numbers of dead on both sides; the wounded dragging out a maimed existence; the multitudes carried off by dysentery, cholera, marsh fever and *berri-berri*; the widows, sisters and mothers standing in mute grief. He gave the financial estimates — some eleven hundred million spent on army and navy since [[1873]]; extra expenditure of three hundred and fifty million guilders over twenty-five years; the figure of seven to eight hundred million reached by others; and the *[[Java Bode]]* of [[13 April]] [[1897]] with its estimate of a thousand million guilders and a hundred thousand human lives. He noted that the minister [[Mr. J. H. Bergsma|Bergsma]] had been obliged to declare five months earlier that neither the [[Ministerie van Koloniën|Ministry of Colonies]] nor the Indies could state with precision how much had been spent specifically on Atjeh, and that [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]], then an assistant-resident, had objected in a review in *[[Insulinde]]* to Van Kol's earlier figure in his brochure *[[Land en Volk van Java]]* on the ground that all extra civil expenditure, leave allowances, search vessels, travel and subsistence costs and part of the pensions must be added.

He recounted the atrocities: the boast about the *snaphan setan* ("the devil's rifle"); the phrase of [[Rutgers van Rozenburg]] about dividing fallen Acehnese in two to hinder their entry into the Turkish heaven; and the killing of [[Toekoe Tjoet Machmoed Lam Tenzah]] with his wife, children and seven followers as they slept on [[16 May]] [[1897]], reported in *[[De Kroniek]]*. Against this he set his tribute to the Acehnese *voorvechters* who allowed themselves to be shot down calmly, wrapped in white shrouds with arms crossed on the breast — true *[[Jahid|jahid's]]*, witnesses and martyrs of their love for religion and fatherland. The fanaticism that drove them, he said, the Dutch had themselves awakened, turning a manly resistance against foreign intruders into a *[[Prang Sabil|heiligen strijd]]*, a people's war.

He closed with his *gewetensvraag* and the text of [[Van Kol's motion of 16 November 1897|his motion]].

## The first sitting: the replies

[[Pijnacker Hordijk]] followed, quoting the [[Memorie van Antwoord on the Indies budget for 1898|Memorie van Antwoord]] on the fiscal weight of the war and asking the minister what precisely was meant by "consequent volhouden in de thans ingeslagen richting," whether the pacification of the whole of Atjeh was thought possible without conquering a like position in the federations of [[Gighen]] and [[Pedir]] and in the southern settlements, and observing from the report on events in [[Groot-Atjeh]] of 11\u201324 September that the incursion of [[Teuku Umar|Toekoe Oemar]] into [[Lamtih]] had caused a flight of people from [[Lepong]] to the mountains and to the [[IV Moekims]].

An unnamed minister-supporting speaker protested against the charge that nothing had been done for the welfare of the population of [[Java]] and the [[Buitenbezittingen]]. [[J. B. Verhey|Verhey]], a marine officer of the [[1875]]\u20131876 campaign, conceded that from a historical point of view little could be brought against Van Kol's account, but drew a contrary conclusion; he approved the mastery of the three *[[Sagi|sagi's]]* of Groot-Atjeh, compared the position to that reached when the victorious troops of [[Karel van der Heijden|general Van der Heyden]] could join hands at [[Selimoen]] from Lampenas and [[Indrapoeri]] in [[1879]], analysed the army's capacity to sustain the occupation, and stated his objections of principle to a [[Staatscommissie voor Atjeh (proposed 1897)|Staatscommissie]] — above all its paralysing effect and the impropriety of excluding military men. He closed with the figures of the *berri-berri* mortality set out at [[Beri-beri epidemic in the Atjeh garrison]].

[[Abraham Kuyper|Kuyper]] then delivered the speech printed under the heading *Een Christen aan het woord*, arguing that the question of the war's original justice was now a matter for the historian and not for parliamentary debate, that he took back no word of what he had said in [[1874]] and [[1875]], but that the *vitium originis* was not decisive for the present position. He illustrated his case with the parable of the boy in the neighbour's apple tree, urged *stelselvastheid* in place of *stelselloosheid*, declared that [[Groot-Atjeh]] was "de sleutel van heel de positie" — the key to the whole position as a citadel is the key to a city, and pressed the minister to complete at once the number of *[[Recherchevaartuigen|recherchevaartuigen]]*. He looked forward to bringing Atjeh the blessings of firm and orderly government, of higher civilisation, and above all of the Gospel.

[[Van der Zwaag]] answered Kuyper directly, holding that the debate on the justice of the war was more than ever timely, that whoever thought the war must end by force was morally bound to go and fight in Atjeh himself, and that the only means open to a member was to refuse the money — which he undertook to do so long as no end was made of the war. He supported Van Kol's motion while declaring that it did not go far enough and rejecting its final clause on the *volkenrechtelijke stelling*.

[[Bahlmann]] welcomed the new minister as a guarantee that there would be no change of system, identified the instability of policy at each change of [[Governor-General of Netherlands India|Governor-General]] or cabinet as the chief reason the war had lasted so long, deplored the *fanfaronnades* of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|colonel Heutsz]] and of the outgoing army commander, read the [[Voorloopig Verslag on the Indies budget for 1898|Voorloopig Verslag]]'s question into the record, and named the two founding errors of the war — [[Isaac Dignus Fransen van de Putte|Fransen van de Putte]]'s dispatch and [[Van Swieten's proclamation on the sovereignty of Atjeh|Van Swieten's proclamation]]. He demanded that [[Teuku Umar|Toekoe Oemar]] should long since have been in Dutch hands living or dead, and that the submission of the [[Pretendent-sultan van Atjeh|pretendent-sultan]] should be assured.

[[Van Karnebeek]] found no logical connection between Van Kol's speech and his motion and feared the effect of the speech if its sound reached the Atjeh chiefs. [[Pyttersen]] welcomed the minister's declaration of continuity and invoked the precedent of the commission of the [[Padri War|Padri-oorlog]] and the capture of [[Bonjol|Bondjoe]] by [[Andreas Victor Michiels|Michiels]]. [[De Visser]] objected that the motion's logic led to the surrender of the colonies either to foreign powers or to the power of [[Islam]], citing [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]]'s *[[Mekka (Snouck Hurgronje)|Mekka]]*. [[Van Vlijmen]] gave his adhesion to everything that had been and was being done in Atjeh, praised the three bivouacs of [[Tjot Mautjang]], [[Indrapoeri]] and [[Lhoknga|Loknga]] as commanding the whole valley, welcomed the lesson given to [[Segli]], recalled that his own motion of the previous year had been withdrawn on [[J. T. Cremer|Cremer]]'s advice, and asked for ten *recherchevaartuigen* rather than three, instancing the running of contraband at [[Simpang Anas]].

[[Pieter Jelles Troelstra|Troelstra]] closed the opposition case with the speech summarised in the formula "de Kamer is radeloos, de partijen zijn redeloos en onze positie in Atjeh schijnt reddeloos te zijn" — the Chamber is at its wits' end, the parties are unreasoning, and our position in Atjeh appears past saving. He analysed the economic interests of [[Deli]], [[Tamiang]] and [[Perlak]], the shares of the [[Koninklijke Paketvaart-Maatschappij|Koninklijke]], and the former directorships of the minister; the argument is set out at [[Economic interests alleged behind the Atjeh-oorlog (1897)]].

## Cremer's reply

The Minister of Colonies [[J. T. Cremer|J. T. Cremer]] answered at length. He declined to follow Van Kol's historical exposition step by step, but defended the origin of the war by reference to the declaration annexed to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|1824 treaty]] and to the *[[Nota over de betrekkingen van Nederland tot het Rijk van Atjeh sedert 1824|Nota over de betrekkingen van Nederland tot het Rijk van Atjeh sedert 1824]]*; compared Acehnese piracy to that of the corsairs of Algiers and the Rif of [[Morocco]], which had cost Algiers its independence to [[France]]; cited the British government's complaints of ships plundered "by people in the neighbourhood of Atcheen"; and recalled the statement of the Minister of Foreign Affairs [[Gericke van Herwijnen]] in the *[[Comité-generaal van 1874|comité-generaal]]* that he had proofs of Acehnese approaches to [[Ottoman Empire|Turkey]], [[United States of America|America]] and [[France]].

He noted that fourteen successive Ministers of Colonies — thirteen predecessors and himself — had borne the responsibility, each for his own phase, and that he accepted responsibility only for what was now being done, as the continuator of a plan of action adopted under Royal authorisation on the proposal of his predecessor and in accordance with the proposals of the [[Governor-General of Netherlands India|Governor-General]]. He denied being a Messiah or an adventurer.

On the economic charge he answered that the maintenance of quiet in and the development of the colonies was a very great economic interest of the Dutch people and that the *pacificatie* of Atjeh had become an inherent part of it. As former chairman of the [[Koninklijke Paketvaart-Maatschappij|Koninklijke Paketvaart-Maatschappij]] he observed that the Atjeh war had existed for years before that company was founded, that the chartering of ships for expeditions did indeed temporarily strengthen its treasury but far less than under its predecessor the [[Nederlandsch-Indische Stoomvaart-Maatschappij]], and that troop transport imposed such heavy demands on the fleet that peaceful routes being won from foreign competitors were lost. As former co-director of a tobacco company he pointed out that Troelstra had himself quoted the profits made despite the war.

He then set out his defence of the [[Post-1896 system in Atjeh|present system]] — the abandonment of the [[Geconcentreerde linie|geconcentreerde stelling]], which had had a *fair trial* from [[1884]] and had produced only the actions at [[Kota Pohama]] and the *échec* of [[Kaloet]]; the reduction of posts from twenty-one under Van der Heyden plus eight added under civil government to seventeen, of which eight were to be abolished, leaving about nine; the concentration of strong mobile columns in a few well-placed *kampementen*; the improvement in health, with only two to four *berri-berri* patients a day entering hospital in the whole of Atjeh out of eight thousand men; the expansion of the [[Korps Marechaussee]]; and the tram to [[Indrapoeri]] soon to be extended to [[Selimoen]]. Asked whether he wished to conquer the whole of Atjeh, he answered no: the interior was unknown, there was no evidence that fighters from there joined the Acehnese bands, and when the coast was mastered the hinterland would come under Dutch influence of itself.

He explained the [[Sigli expedition (August 1897)|Segli expedition]] as directed not at taking [[Segli]] but at driving hostile bands out of the *[[Moekim|moekims]]* [[Pakan Baroe]] and [[Pokan Soth]]; defended the speeches of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|colonel Van Heutsz]] and [[General Vetter|general Vetter]] against the charge of *fanfaronnade*; refused to name a date for the end of the war; and compared the Acehnese question to a chronic invalid whose bedside deliberations would be overheard by the patient. He denied cruelties in cold blood, and closed with a tribute to the [[Nederlandsch-Indisch Gouvernement|Indies government]] and to the [[Governor-General of Netherlands India|Governor-General]].

## The second sitting

On [[17 November]] [[1897]] Cremer began by placing on record the [[Verklaringen van de kustvorsten van Atjeh|declarations of the sixty-two coastal princes]]. [[H. van Kol|Van Kol]] replied with the speech printed under the headings *Nog eens de zeeroof*, *Onze volkenrechtelijke stelling*, *Ons prestige in Indië*, *Waarom niet uit Java?*, *Atjeh onneembaar*, *De belanghebbenden* and *De conclusie*, in which he documented the atrocities in detail, expounded the doctrine of the *[[Kring van belang|kring van belang]]* (sphere of interest), argued that Dutch prestige would be raised and not lowered by withdrawal, cited the prophecy of [[Toekoe Baid]] that if the men were killed the women would make war and after them the children, and named the contractors, suppliers, shipping companies and the merchants of [[Penang|Pinang]] who profited by the war.

[[Abraham Kuyper|Kuyper]] answered [[Van der Zwaag]], [[Pieter Jelles Troelstra|Troelstra]] and [[H. van Kol|Van Kol]] in a short reply invoking the impoverishment that the loss of the colonies would bring, the European war that their abandonment would provoke, the condition of Tripoli and Klein Azië as an image of non-European rule, the Russian legend of the two heavenly beings over the steppes, the botanist and the rose, and the answer of the Christus concerning the penny and the image of Caesar in Roman Palestine.

[[Ketelaar]] rose to withdraw the support he had given the motion. [[Pieter Jelles Troelstra|Troelstra]] replied to Cremer's jest about Russian and Swiss professors, restated that he had named the minister's former directorships not to cast a personal stain but to characterise the class in power, and was twice called to order by the [[Voorzitter der Tweede Kamer|President]]. Cremer answered briefly, associating himself with Kuyper on the question of cruelties and quoting [[Mr. J. H. Bergsma|Bergsma]]'s statement of [[22 September]] [[1896]] on mutilation.

The general deliberation and the deliberation on the motion of order were then closed, and the motion was put and rejected by seventy-two votes to three.
