**The publication of *Beschouwingen over Atjeh en onzen oorlog met dat rijk*** — *Reflections on Aceh and our war with that kingdom* — by the anonymous author [[Een Ooggetuige|Een Ooggetuige]] ("An Eyewitness"), issued by the publishing house [[Van Meurs & Stufkens]] in [[Rotterdam]] in the [[1880s]], was a contribution to the contemporary Dutch pamphlet literature on the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]]. The pamphlet, which was issued with a folded map — the [[Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel|*Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel, de Vaarwaters en vereischte Kustlichten voor den Wereldhandel*]] — "Map of the Theatre of War, the Waterways and required Coastal Lights for World Trade" — belongs to the body of contemporary Dutch writing that debated the conduct, aims, and strategic value of the war against the [[Acheh|Acehnese]] sultanate.

## Context: the Atjeh War in the 1880s

The [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] entered its middle phase in the [[1880s]]. The Dutch, having taken the kraton of the Sultan in [[1874]] and established their administrative centre at [[Kota-Radja|Kota-Radja]], spent the years [[1874]]–[[1884]] in offensive operations aimed at extending their control over [[Groot-Atjeh|Groot-Atjeh]] and the surrounding areas. An experiment with separate civil administration was introduced in [[1881]] but proved unsuccessful. The *oorlogspartij* (war party) and the *heilige oorlog* (holy war) movement gained strength.

The concentration decision of [[19 August]] [[1884]] marked a strategic shift from offensive to defensive operations. Dutch forces withdrew from scattered posts to a *geconcentreerde stelling* (concentrated position) around [[Kota-Radja|Kota-Radja]] and [[Olehleh|Olehleh]], completed on [[1 March]] [[1885]]. The concentration system, and the strategic debate that it provoked, set the conditions for the pamphlet literature of the [[1880s]].

## The pamphlet and its author

The pamphlet was issued under the pen name *Een Ooggetuige* — "An Eyewitness" — a name that signals the author's claim to first-hand knowledge of the Atjeh theatre, without revealing the writer's identity. The choice of a pseudonym was common in the Dutch pamphlet literature of the Atjeh War, in which serving and retired officers, colonial officials, merchants, and private commentators debated the war in print while preserving their anonymity. The author's real identity is not recorded on the title-page and has not been identified.

## The publisher and the place of publication

The pamphlet was published by [[Van Meurs & Stufkens|Van Meurs & Stufkens]] in [[Rotterdam|Rotterdam]]. The Rotterdam location of the publisher is consistent with the strong interest of the Rotterdam merchant community in the colonial trade — including the [[pepper cultivation in Atjeh|pepper trade]] of [[Acheh|Atjeh]] — and with the role of Rotterdam publishers in circulating pamphlet literature on colonial questions to that mercantile readership.

## The argument on Atjeh's strategic position

A central argument of the pamphlet concerns the strategic position of Atjeh in relation to world trade. The author compares the position of Atjeh — commanding the northern entrance to the [[Strand Malaka (Strait of Malacca)|Strait of Malacca]] through the [[Strait of Halakka|Strait of Halakka]] — with that of [[Singapore|Singapore]], and argues that Atjeh lies on the great sea-route between the [[Suez Canal|Suez Canal]] and the Far East in a way that makes it, properly developed, a natural station for world shipping. The author holds that ships bound for [[Australia|Australia]] from Europe could pass more easily through the Strait of Halakka, north of [[Sabang|Pulau Weh]], than through the Strait of Malacca itself, and that a properly lighted and buoyed approach to the Atjehnese coast would shorten the passage for trade between Europe, [[India|India]], and the archipelago.

The comparison with Singapore is made explicitly: the author argues that, for several branches of world trade, Atjeh is more favourably placed than Singapore, and that the neglect of the Atjehnese ports and waterways has handed an advantage to the British colony that the Dutch could yet recover.

## The pepper trade and the interior

The pamphlet devotes attention to the Atjehnese pepper trade, noting the extent of the pepper plantations (*pepperplant teelt*) along the Atjehnese coast and the dependence of the Atjehnese planters on foreign — chiefly [[Penang|Penang]] and [[Singapore|Singapore]] — buyers. The author remarks on the flow of pepper from the Atjehnese interior to the coastal ports, and on the role of the Atjehnese traders in carrying that pepper to market. The discussion of the pepper trade is tied to the argument on strategy: the author holds that a settled Atjeh, with its pepper trade properly organised, would be a source of revenue to the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] and a safeguard against the further intrusion of foreign traders into the Atjehnese market.

## The conduct of the war and the Pall Mall Gazette

The pamphlet discusses the conduct of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]] and the controversy then current in the Dutch press over the course of the campaign. The author refers to commentary in the London *[[Pall Mall Gazette|Pall Mall Gazette]]* on the Atjeh question, and to the argument — then current in the Dutch and British press — over whether the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] should continue the war or should withdraw from Atjeh. The author argues against withdrawal, holding that a withdrawal would be read by the Atjehnese as a defeat and would weaken the Dutch position throughout the archipelago, but is critical of the way the war had been conducted and of the absence of a clear plan for the settlement of the country once the war should be brought to an end.

## The coastal lights and the map

The pamphlet was issued with a folded map — the [[Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel|*Kaart van het Oorlogstooneel, de Vaarwaters en vereischte Kustlichten voor den Wereldhandel*]] — which set out the Atjehnese coast, the waterways around it, and the positions at which coastal lights (*kustlichten*) were held to be needed for world trade. The map is one of the elements that gives the pamphlet its character as a practical contribution to the strategic debate, since it allowed the reader to follow the author's argument on the shipping routes and the lighting of the coast in visual form. Among the coastal places named in the pamphlet and shown on the map are [[Panton Laboe|Panton Laboe]], [[Kampang|Kampang]], and the places on the Lampong coast — [[Kroeë|Kroeë]] and [[Oosthaven|Oosthaven]] — discussed in connection with the [[Sunda Strait|Sunda Strait]] and the sea approaches to Atjeh.

## Significance

The publication of the *Beschouwingen over Atjeh en onzen oorlog met dat rijk* is significant within the AcehWiki corpus as an event in the contemporary Dutch pamphlet literature on the [[Aceh War|Atjeh War]]. The pamphlet is one of a number of anonymous or pseudonymous writings on the war — including the accounts of [[Terwogt's account of the Atjeh-oorlog (1891)|Terwogt]] and [[Beuming's account of the origin of the Atjeh-oorlog|Beuming]] — that shaped the Dutch public debate on the war. Its anonymous authorship, its insistence on the strategic and commercial value of Atjeh, and its accompanying map of the theatre of war and the coastal lights together make it a document of the practical, strategic, and commercial thinking that underlay the Dutch continuation of the war. The pamphlet's comparison of Atjeh with [[Singapore|Singapore]], and its argument for the development of the Atjehnese coast as a station for world trade, place it within the wider nineteenth-century debate on the sea approaches to the archipelago, of which the [[Suez Canal|Suez Canal]] — opened in [[1869]] — was the decisive modern fact.
