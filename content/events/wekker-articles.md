The **"Wekker" articles** were a series of articles published in the Dutch newspaper *[[De Avondpost]]* in [[October]] [[1907]], written under the pseudonym "Wekker." They accused [[G. C. E. van Daalen|Governor Van Daalen]] of [[Acheh|Atjèh]] of gross misconduct, cruelty, and the toleration of atrocities, and sparked a major political controversy in the Netherlands. The articles led directly to the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] of [[1907]]–[[1908]] and ultimately contributed to Van Daalen's removal from office in [[1908]]. However, the subsequent official inquiry found that the articles were largely false, exaggerated, or based on unreliable hearsay — though they accelerated a political process that was independently justified.

## Content and impact

The Wekker articles, appearing in multiple instalments, presented a lurid picture of atrocities committed by Dutch troops under Van Daalen's command — including allegations of unprovoked killing of civilians, torture of prisoners, and systematic plunder and destruction of villages. They were widely read in the Netherlands and fuelled parliamentary criticism, particularly from left-wing members such as [[Van Kol]], [[De Stuers]], and [[Thomson]], who used them to press for a formal inquiry. They also strengthened the hand of those who already opposed [[J. B. van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]'s "imperialist" colonial policies, and his initial public defence of Van Daalen made the controversy a political liability for him personally.

## The official inquiry's findings

The military inquiry conducted by [[Rost van Tonningen]] as part of the [[Atjéh-enquête|Atjéh-enquête]] examined each of the Wekker allegations in detail. The findings, later summarised in the parliamentary record, stated that the articles contained "certain untruths"; that many incidents had been misrepresented; that some events described had taken place before Van Daalen became governor; and that others, when stripped of their dramatic embellishment, were simply the unavoidable consequences of guerrilla warfare. The inquiry concluded that the author "had been intent on placing Van Daalen in a bad light" and that the accusations were "mostly false or strongly exaggerated." Despite this exoneration on most specific charges, the political damage was done, and the inquiry that the articles had forced into existence exposed genuine and serious abuses by Van Daalen that the Wekker articles had not identified — the arbitrary fines, unjustified internments, and neglect of local governance that Van Heutsz himself condemned.

## Aftermath

Van Daalen was removed from his post in 1908, though the grounds were the administrative abuses documented by Van Heutsz's own investigation — not the specific atrocity allegations of the Wekker articles. The affair contributed to the broader political campaign against Van Heutsz, though it also helped restore public confidence in the overall conduct of the colonial forces once the official inquiry cleared them of the most dramatic charges.

## Significance

The Wekker episode illustrates the power of the press in colonial politics and the danger of unverified allegations, while also demonstrating that the controversy they ignited exposed genuine abuses through a process they set in motion. It showed the willingness of the Dutch government to investigate its own officials — a sign of the increasingly ethical tone of colonial governance — and for Van Heutsz, the affair was a painful lesson in the limits of personal trust and the importance of close supervision of subordinates.
