**The construction of the Gajoweg** — the building of the Gayo Road (Dutch: *Gajoweg*) — was the major engineering project undertaken by the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Dutch colonial administration]] in the first decade of the [[20th century|twentieth century]] to drive a military mountain road from the northern coast of [[Aceh|Atjeh]] to the interior [[Gajolanden|Gayo highlands]] of central [[Sumatra]], linking the coastal town of [[Bireuen|Bireuën]] with the highland settlement of [[Takengen|Takengeun]] on the [[Laut Tawar|Lake Laut Tawar]] across a distance of approximately 103 kilometres. The work required blasting rock with dynamite, throwing large bridges over deep ravines — including one of the longest pile-bridges (Dutch: *paalbruggen*) in the entire Dutch East Indies across the [[Peusanganrivier|Peusangan river]] — and protecting the labourers from the bandit gangs (Dutch: *roverbenden*) that operated in the still-unpacified interior. The road, described in the source as "a fine piece of Dutch engineering," opened the [[Gajolanden|Gayo Lands]] to plantation capital and government administration and was the principal strategic infrastructure of the post-[[1904|1904]] consolidation of Atjeh under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|General Van Heutsz]] and his successors.

## Strategic context and the post-pacification highlands

The construction of the Gajoweg was a direct consequence of the [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]] of the [[Gajolanden|Gayo]] and [[Alaslanden|Alas Lands]] by the [[Colonne van Daalen 1904|colonne van Daalen of 1904]], which extended Dutch military authority into the central highlands of [[Sumatra]] for the first time. The [[Gajolanden|Gayo Lands]] had been outside the effective reach of the [[Dutch East Indies Government|colonial government]] throughout the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], serving as a refuge for Acehnese resistance and as the principal source of the gold, coffee, and camphor that sustained the highland economy. After the [[Colonne van Daalen 1904|colonne van Daalen]] broke organised resistance in the highlands, the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden]] needed a reliable military road to project power into the interior, to link the new administrative posts at [[Takengen|Takengen]], [[Blang Kedjeren|Blang-Kedjeren]], and [[Lokop|Lokop]] with the coastal capital at [[Koeta-Radja|Koeta Radja]], and to open the highlands to the plantation enterprises authorised under the [[Agrarische Wet 1870|Agrarian Law of 1870 (Dutch: *Agrarische Wet 1870*)]]. The Gajoweg was the principal instrument of this consolidation, and its construction was accordingly a military as well as a civil undertaking.

## Engineering the road

The construction of the Gajoweg was the work of the [[Departement der Burgerlijke Openbare Werken|Department of Civil Public Works (Dutch: *Departement der Burgerlijke Openbare Werken*)]] and of the soldier-roadbuilders of the [[Indische Leger|Indische Leger]] under the direction of the engineer-officer [[Heinzenknecht|Heinzenknecht]], a Bavarian-born lieutenant whose bronze bust at the head of the road commemorates both the work and his murder by a forced labourer during it. The route was indicated on military command with the *klewang* (the colonial army's sword) — that is, it was cut through the jungle in a direct southerly direction — and laid out at a hurried pace, which made the road's serpentine alignment a permanent feature of its character. The construction required blasting rock with dynamite along the entire ascent from the coastal plain, and it required the throwing of substantial bridges over the deep ravines of the highland rivers. The bridge over the [[Peusanganrivier|Peusangan river]] at kilometre 11 — one of the longest pile-bridges (Dutch: *paalbruggen*) in the whole of the Dutch East Indies — was the principal hydraulic-engineering feat of the project, and it carried the water of [[Laut Tawar|Lake Laut Tawar]] to the sea. The road's serpentine alignment and the construction of the substantial bridges are described in the source as "a fine piece of Dutch engineering," and the work as a whole set the standard for colonial road-building in the highlands of Sumatra.

## Labour, *roverbenden*, and the cost of the road

The construction of the Gajoweg was a costly affair in both money and lives. The labourers — among them the forced labourers (Dutch: *heerendienstplichtigen*) drawn from the surrounding villages under the corvée regulations and the Javanese contract labourers recruited for the more specialised work — were guarded by soldiers throughout the project, for the interior was still plagued by the bandit gangs (Dutch: *roverbenden*) that had survived the [[Pacification of Aceh|pacification]] and that preyed on isolated working parties. The most notorious incident of the construction was the murder of [[Heinzenknecht|Heinzenknecht]] himself, "basely murdered by a forced labourer during that work" — the murder commemorated by the bronze bust that stood at the head of the road on the yard of the controller at [[Bireuen|Bireuën]]. The murder was the book's marker of the cost of the road's construction: the soldier who laid the road was murdered by one of the forced labourers who built it. The combination of military protection, forced labour, and the constant threat of bandit attack made the Gajoweg a paradigm of the colonial state's highland infrastructure, and the cost of its construction — in money, in lives, and in the resentments it generated — was the long-term price of the road's strategic effect.

## Economic consequences and demographic transformation

The opening of the Gajoweg enabled the transport of plantation products — [[latex-aceh|rubber]], [[palm-oil|oil-palm]], and [[sisal|sisal]] — from the newly opened highland estates to the coast for export through the harbour at [[Oeleë-Lheue|Olehleh]], and it facilitated the demographic changes that reshaped the highlands in the inter-war period. The influx of Javanese labourers onto the east-coast and highland plantations was the principal demographic shift, and the road made possible the rapid expansion of the [[Deli-planters Vereeniging|Deli planters]]' rubber and oil-palm enterprises into the central Sumatran interior. The road also opened the [[Gajolanden|Gayo Lands]] to the [[arabica-coffee|Arabica coffee]] cultivation that would become the principal cash crop of the highlands, and it linked the highland economy to the colonial market in a way that had not been possible under the [[Koetaradja Sultanate|Acehnese sultanate]]. The road's economic effect was thus the integration of the Gayo highlands into the colonial economy of northern Sumatra — an integration whose strategic and commercial logic is recorded in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael|Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]) and in the broader literature of the Atjeh consolidation.

## Legacy

The Gajoweg remained, throughout the colonial period and into the post-[[1949|1949]] Indonesian republic, the principal land route between the northern coast of [[Aceh|Atjeh]] and the central Sumatran highlands. Its construction was the model for the later road-building of the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies Government]] in the [[Buitenbezittingen|Outer Possessions]], and its strategic logic — the projection of military and administrative power into the highlands through a single engineered road — was the model that the colonial state applied wherever its authority was newly extended. The road's continued use, and the continued presence of the bronze bust of [[Heinzenknecht|Heinzenknecht]] at its head, made it the principal physical monument of the Atjeh consolidation, and its memory — recorded in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael|Oost-Indisch Journael]]* — made it the principal literary monument of the cost of that consolidation.
