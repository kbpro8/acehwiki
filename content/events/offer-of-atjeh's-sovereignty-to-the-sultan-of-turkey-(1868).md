**The offer of Atjeh's sovereignty to the Sultan of Turkey** was the attempt of the [[Sultan of Atjeh|Sultan of Atjeh]] in [[1868]] to place his lands under the sovereignty of his religious head, "den Beheerscher aller Geloovigen" — the Commander of the Faithful — as a means of preserving Acehnese independence against Dutch encroachment.

## The event as recounted in [[1897]]

[[H. van Kol]] narrated it to the [[Tweede Kamer]] on [[16 November]] [[1897]] as the natural consequence of a generation of Dutch expansion. Having recounted the occupation of [[Baros]] in [[1839]] and [[Singkel]] in [[1840]], the [[Tractaat van 1857]], the [[Siak Tractaat (1858)|Siak treaty]], the extension of Dutch sovereignty to the northern boundary of [[Tamiang|Tamian]], the subjugation of [[Asahan|Assahan]] by the expedition of [[1865]] and thereby of the whole East Coast, and the expeditions to [[Nias]] in [[1863]] and to [[Singkel]] in [[1865]], he continued:

"het is dus niet te bevreemden, dat de Sultan van Atjeh steun zocht tegen onze overweldiging, en zelfs in [[1868]] de Soevereiniteit zijner landen aanbood aan zijn godsdienstig Opperhoofd, den Beheerscher aller Geloovigen, aan den [[Sultan of Turkey|Sultan van Turkije]]. Eene alleszins begrijpelijke poging, om zijne onafhankelijkheid te bewaren." — it is therefore not surprising that the Sultan of Atjeh sought support against our overwhelming, and even in 1868 offered the Sovereignty of his lands to his religious Head, the Commander of all the Faithful, to the Sultan of Turkey.

## The Dutch counter-measures

Van Kol set the offer against the Dutch diplomacy of the following years: by abolishing the differential duties in [[Siak]] English trade was satisfied; by throwing a piece of the coast of Guinea to the English government that government was satisfied; and in November [[1871]] the [[Sumatra Tractaat (1871)|treaty with England]] was concluded which left the Dutch a free hand on [[Sumatra]], that is against Atjeh. Use was made of it "met al te veel gretigheid," and already on [[26 March]] [[1873]] war was declared.

## The government's reading

The Minister of Colonies [[J. T. Cremer|Cremer]] treated the same approaches as evidence for the justice of the war rather than against it, holding that in judging that justice one might not overlook "de pogingen van Atjeh om zich onder de suzereiniteit te stellen van [[Ottoman Empire|Turkije]], van [[United States of America|Amerika]] en van [[France|Frankrijk]]," — the attempts of Atjeh to place itself under the suzerainty of Turkey, of America and of France, concerning which the Minister of Foreign Affairs [[Gericke van Herwijnen]] had said in the *[[Comité-generaal van 1874|comité-generaal]]*: "Ik heb de bewijzen hier voor mij liggen."

[[H. van Kol|Van Kol]] answered that the alleged Acehnese *verraad* had consisted in "enkele heel of half verdichte samenspraken van twijfelachtige personen en avonturiers met handels-consuls van Italië en N.-Amerika, die niet de minste macht uitoefenden en reeds dadelijk door hunne Regeeringen werden gedésavoueerd," — a few wholly or half invented conversations of doubtful persons and adventurers with trade consuls of Italy and North America, who exercised not the least power and were immediately disavowed by their Governments. and that the sultan, as an independent prince, had the fullest right to seek support — indeed that it was for him "een eisch van zelfbehoud."

## Account from *Vijftig jaren economische staatkunde in Atjeh* ([[1923]])

The **Atjehnese offer of sovereignty to the Sultan of Turkey** in [[1868]] was the step by which the chiefs of [[Acheh|Atjeh]], in the anarchy of their realm and in apprehension of Dutch expansion, tendered the sovereignty of their country to the [[Sultan of Turkey|Ottoman Sultan]] — "die echter voor de eer stichtelijk bedankte" ("who, however, piously declined the honour") — while, in the same years, the chiefs of several Atjehnese coast states themselves asked to be placed under Dutch authority.

### The offers of 1868

"Nadat in 1868 de hoofden van Atjeh de souvereiniteit van hun land hadden aangeboden aan den sultan van Turkije (die echter voor de eer stichtelijk bedankte!), verzochten de bestuurders van onderscheidene kuststaten, de anarchie in het Rijk moede, om onder ons gezag te mogen worden geplaatst" — writes author [[J. Langhout|Joh. Langhout]]: after the chiefs of Atjeh had offered the sovereignty of their country to the Sultan of Turkey, the rulers of several coast states, weary of the anarchy in the realm, requested to be placed under the Dutch authority. For the time being the Government deemed it right not to accede, partly because by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 17 March 1824]] was established that the Government would take care that the relations with Atjeh would be so regulated that this native state, without losing anything of its independence, would not disturb the safety of trade and shipping. Even after the [[Treaty of 1871|Sumatra treaty of 20 November 1871]] had set the Netherlands free, the Government did not directly grant the like request of some Atjehnese chiefs: a similar request of the [[Sultan of Deli]] was declined, and the offers of the [[Radja of Troemon|Radja of Troemoen]] to mediate for the acceptance of Dutch supremacy were equally not accepted.

### Significance

The twin offers of 1868 — to Constantinople and, by the coast chiefs, to the Dutch — showed both poles of Atjeh's political distress on the eve of intervention; Langhout cites them as the last phase of the decay of "zelfbestuur" (self-government) that prepared the [[Krayenhoff|Krayenhoff investigation (1871)]] and the mission of [[F. N. Nieuwenhuizen|Nieuwenhuizen]] ([[Nieuwenhuizen's mission to Atjeh and the declaration of war (March 1873)|March 1873]]).
