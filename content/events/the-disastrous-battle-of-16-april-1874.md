**The Disastrous Battle of 16 April 1874** (Dutch: *het nadeelige gevecht van 16 April*) was a sharp and costly defeat suffered by Dutch forces under the command of [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Colonel Pel]] during an offensive operation against a fortified Acehnese kampong in the environs of [[Kotta Radja]]. It was the most serious setback experienced by the Dutch during the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|Second Expedition's]] operational phase and was followed almost immediately by the departure of the [[Jan van Swieten|main force]] under [[Jan van Swieten|Lieutenant-General Van Swieten]] from Atjeh on [[20 April]] [[1874]].

## The Situation After the Kraton

Since the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)|capture of the Kraton]] on [[24 January]], the Dutch had been consolidating their position at [[Kotta Radja]] and in the surrounding area. A succession of fortified posts had been established — at [[Oeleh-leh]], [[Blangoe]], [[Oedjong Pelang Gatang]], [[Kotta Radja Bedil]], kampong Lamara, [[Soerian]], [[Mosapie]], and the so-called "grafheuvel" (burial mound) east of the Kraton. These posts, though well-armed and well-garrisoned, were necessarily dispersed across a landscape covered with kampongs, orchards, and watercourses, and the Acehnese defenders — though driven from the Kraton — remained formidable in the surrounding terrain.

By mid-April, Colonel [[J. L. J. H. Pel|Pel]], who would assume overall command upon Van Swieten's departure, was attempting to extend Dutch control further into the interior. The Acehnese, under the direction of leaders such as [[Panglima Polim]], continued to mount attacks on the Dutch posts and to maintain their own fortified positions.

## The Engagement

On [[16 April]] [[1874]], a Dutch column was directed against a strongly fortified kampong. The Acehnese defenders, occupying well-prepared positions with substantial earthworks and firing positions, offered fierce resistance. The Dutch assault encountered heavy fire and was unable to make good its objectives. The engagement resulted in significant Dutch casualties, including the loss of several officers and men.

The exact circumstances of the fighting — the specific kampong attacked, the tactical decisions that led to the Dutch setback, and the precise casualties — are not fully detailed in Van Swieten's account, but the event was sufficiently serious to be described in the Dutch sources as *het nadeelige gevecht* — "the disadvantageous engagement" — a euphemism for a clear defeat.

## Van Swieten's Decision Not to Resume the Attack

The most consequential aspect of the battle was not the engagement itself but [[Jan van Swieten|Van Swieten]]'s decision *not* to resume the attack. Despite pressure from those who demanded that the defeat be avenged and the kampong taken, Van Swieten refused to order a renewed assault. His reasoning, as set out in his correspondence and his letter to [[W. J. Knoop|Lieutenant-General Knoop]] of [[17 October]] [[1874]], was deliberate and principled:

When a subordinate commander is in danger, it is the duty of the supreme commander to support and rescue him — yes, always. But to resume an already concluded engagement to reverse a defeat that has no influence on the general situation: never. To do so would be to risk lives for a purpose that does not advance the overall plan. The fortified kampong lay among many others, and to attack it would risk entangling the Dutch in a series of kampong battles whose end could not be foreseen and which would certainly cost many lives.

"Moreover," Van Swieten wrote, "the resumption of the attack would have had the appearance of sanctioning a wrong, and that also could not be permitted."

## The Broader Consequences

The battle confirmed Van Swieten in the course he had already determined: the departure of the main force and the consolidation of the remaining garrison in a defensible position centred on the Kraton. The lesson he drew was not that the Dutch should attack more aggressively but that they should consolidate more carefully.

In his letter to [[W. J. Knoop|Knoop]], Van Swieten defended his decision at length against critics who demanded a more offensive posture. He invoked the principle that a supreme commander is not obliged to pursue every tactical reverse into a renewed engagement, and that the moral courage to resist such pressure — to "defy the errant opinion of others" when convinced that compliance would not serve the general interest — was itself a military virtue.

The battle also underscored the limits of Dutch power in Atjeh. Despite the capture of the Kraton, the proclamation of annexation, and the submission of several subordinate states, the Acehnese remained capable of inflicting serious losses on Dutch columns in the field. The war was far from over.
