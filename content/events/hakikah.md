The **Hakikah** (from the Arabic *'aqīqah*) is, in the Mohammedan law, the sacrifice of two sheep or goats for a male child and one for a female child, prescribed by preference on the seventh day after birth. C. Snouck Hurgronje treats it in *The Achehnese* as one of the rites of early childhood, observed in [[Acheh]] under the Arabic name *ḥakīkah* and connected both with the ceremony of the *peuchichab* (the "giving to taste") and with the wider Mohammedan law of the newborn child.

## The Sacrifice

The Mohammedan law recommends an offering of two sheep or goats for a male child and one for a female, by preference on the seventh day after birth — but if this be impossible, then at some later date, even when the child is quite grown up. This sacrifice is called *'aqīqah* in Arabic, and is not only known but actually practised in Acheh under the name of *hakikah*. The seventh day after birth is also the day recommended by the law of Islam for the *peuchichab* — the formal "giving to taste" — which is generally accompanied by the first shaving of the child's head; the *hakikah* sacrifice may thus form part of a single ceremony, though it is more often postponed to a later occasion.

## The Sacrifice at Home and in Mecca

The choice of some later occasion for the *hakikah*, rather than the seventh day after birth, is also common in Acheh. Where the sacrifice is offered at home, the meat is distributed among the poor and the kindred according to the rules of the Mohammedan law; where the parents are devotees of the Meccan school, however, the sacrifice may be offered in the holy city itself. In Acheh, no less than in other parts of the East Indian Archipelago, the people of Mecca have done their best to foster the doctrine that it is an extremely meritorious act to offer the *hakikah* sacrifice for the child in the holy city. The Mecca folk thus reap the profits on the sale of the goats, and at the same time enjoy their share of the meat. Many Achehnese are, however, aware that the *hakikah* is more properly offered at home, and the home observance accordingly continues to be the rule.

## Relation to the Peuchichab and the Naming

The *hakikah* is one of a complex of ceremonies that surround the birth and early days of an Achehnese child. On the seventh day after birth, the ceremony of the **peuchichab** ("giving to taste") takes place, generally accompanied by the first shaving of the child's head. The midwife, after shaving the head, lays the child on a small mattress covered with costly cloths, and brings it to the *teungku*. The teungku takes a small piece of date, mixes it with cocoanut milk, and rubs it on the child's lips after invoking the name of Allah. Both the teungku and after him the relatives of the infant also "give him to taste" a sort of pap made of pounded raw fruits. From this compulsory introduction to human customs the whole ceremony takes its name of *peuchichab*.

The giving of the name, for which the seventh day is also recommended by the law of Islam, generally takes place in Acheh at the end of the period of convalescence, and is not attended by much ceremony. The teungku consults a Malay manual which gives the names most suitable for a child born on a given day of the week and month throughout the year. Before the name is fixed, the child is usually called *Si Chut* or *Nya' Chut* ("the little one"), or *Si Chu'eng* or *Si Kheb* (both of which have a somewhat unsavoury significance), or, if a boy, *Si Gam* ("mannikin"), or, if a girl, *Si Inong* ("little woman"). Such names, universal and indistinctive as they are, remain in many cases the only ones by which those that bear them are known all their life long.
