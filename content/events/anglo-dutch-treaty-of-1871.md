The **Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871** was the new treaty concluded between the Netherlands and Great Britain, under which Holland was left with more freedom of action in the control of [[Acheh]]. It was the diplomatic prelude to the Dutch expedition against Acheh of [[1873]] and the long [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]] that followed. The treaty came as the necessary correction to the contradictory stipulations of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]], under which Holland had guaranteed the security of trade and shipping in Acheh while also promising to respect the country's independence — an undertaking impossible to carry out.

## Background: half a century of complaints

In the half-century following the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 1824]], the complaints of wrongs of every sort suffered at the hands of the Achehnese by European and American traders became "more and more plentiful." The Dutch, as the one power settled in [[Sumatra]] and having brought the other territories in the island under its suzerainty or control, had been compelled during the second decennial period of the nineteenth century to take measures to ensure the safety of foreign trade in Acheh — and had discovered, in the fifty years that followed, that the treaty of 1824's contradictory undertaking was impossible to fulfil. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] notes, the British experience of [[1786]] — that it would be absolutely necessary "to subdue all the chiefs" and "to overawe the inhabitants" — was now, after a long struggle, to be acquired by the Dutch as well.

## The new freedom of action

The complaints of traders at length led up, in [[1871]], to the new treaty with England, under which Holland was left with more freedom of action in the control of Acheh. The freedom was, however, immediately misused. People flattered themselves "quite seriously with the hope that a treaty with the Sultan of Acheh — forced out of him, if need be, by menaces and by the despatch of a small force — would succeed in doing all that was wanted; they did not trouble their minds in the least degree with the internal condition of the country." Even after the war had actually begun in [[1873]], the Dutch continued to entertain the vain hope that a treaty of this sort might be the basis of a solution of their Achehnese difficulties.

## Consequences

The illusion that a treaty extracted from the Sultan could substitute for the actual subjugation of the country was dispelled only slowly. After the abolition of the royal authority and the death of the last Sultan, the Netherlands India Government came forward as his successor and claimed the allegiance of the chiefs of the dependent kingdoms. Many chiefs of Achehnese coast-dependencies formally gave in their submission in order to protect their commerce from injury, "but the number who withheld their allegiance was greater; and many chiefs in the interior did not consider themselves bound by the promises of the raja of the coast." Furthermore, the state of war added to the many chiefs who already disputed authority in Acheh "a countless number of others: adventurers, religious leaders, military chiefs of every type." For years the Dutch authorities remained under the illusion that by taking up certain strong positions — one having the former Sultan's residence as its centre and others being important harbours in the dependencies — the rest of the country could be forced to submit. This was the illusion that produced, after the governorship of [[General van der Heijden|General van der Heyden]] (1877–1881), the [[Civil government period|civil government period]] and the [[Concentration Scheme|concentration scheme]], and that was finally dispelled only under [[Van Heutsz's governorship|Van Heutsz's governorship]] from [[1898]].
