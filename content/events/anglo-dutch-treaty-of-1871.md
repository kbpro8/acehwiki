The **Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1871** was the new treaty concluded between the Netherlands and Great Britain, under which Holland was left with more freedom of action in the control of [[Acheh]]. It was the diplomatic prelude to the Dutch expedition against Acheh of [[1873]] and the long [[Achehnese War|Achehnese War]] that followed. The treaty came as the necessary correction to the contradictory stipulations of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824]], under which Holland had guaranteed the security of trade and shipping in Acheh while also promising to respect the country's independence — an undertaking impossible to carry out.

## Background: half a century of complaints

In the half-century following the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 1824]], the complaints of wrongs of every sort suffered at the hands of the Achehnese by European and American traders became "more and more plentiful." The Dutch, as the one power settled in [[Sumatra]] and having brought the other territories in the island under its suzerainty or control, had been compelled during the second decennial period of the nineteenth century to take measures to ensure the safety of foreign trade in Acheh — and had discovered, in the fifty years that followed, that the treaty of 1824's contradictory undertaking was impossible to fulfil. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] notes, the British experience of [[1786]] — that it would be absolutely necessary "to subdue all the chiefs" and "to overawe the inhabitants" — was now, after a long struggle, to be acquired by the Dutch as well.

## The new freedom of action

The complaints of traders at length led up, in [[1871]], to the new treaty with England, under which Holland was left with more freedom of action in the control of Acheh. The freedom was, however, immediately misused. People flattered themselves "quite seriously with the hope that a treaty with the Sultan of Acheh — forced out of him, if need be, by menaces and by the despatch of a small force — would succeed in doing all that was wanted; they did not trouble their minds in the least degree with the internal condition of the country." Even after the war had actually begun in [[1873]], the Dutch continued to entertain the vain hope that a treaty of this sort might be the basis of a solution of their Achehnese difficulties.

## Consequences

The illusion that a treaty extracted from the Sultan could substitute for the actual subjugation of the country was dispelled only slowly. After the abolition of the royal authority and the death of the last Sultan, the Netherlands India Government came forward as his successor and claimed the allegiance of the chiefs of the dependent kingdoms. Many chiefs of Achehnese coast-dependencies formally gave in their submission in order to protect their commerce from injury, "but the number who withheld their allegiance was greater; and many chiefs in the interior did not consider themselves bound by the promises of the raja of the coast." Furthermore, the state of war added to the many chiefs who already disputed authority in Acheh "a countless number of others: adventurers, religious leaders, military chiefs of every type." For years the Dutch authorities remained under the illusion that by taking up certain strong positions — one having the former Sultan's residence as its centre and others being important harbours in the dependencies — the rest of the country could be forced to submit. This was the illusion that produced, after the governorship of [[General van der Heijden|General van der Heyden]] (1877–1881), the [[Civil government period|civil government period]] and the [[Concentration Scheme|concentration scheme]], and that was finally dispelled only under [[Van Heutsz's governorship|Van Heutsz's governorship]] from [[1898]].

## Expanded account from *The Achehnese*, Vol. I — Sumatra Treaty of 1871

The **Sumatra Treaty of [[1871]]** — known in Dutch as the *Sumatra-tractaat van 1871* — was the Anglo-Dutch convention by which Great Britain at last abandoned its long-standing objections to the extension of Dutch authority over the island of [[Sumatra]]. Negotiated and signed in 1871, the treaty abandoned "all remonstrances against the extension of Netherlands authority in any part of the island of Sumatra," and therewith renounced the Acheh reservation that had been attached to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824]]. In Van der Kemp's account the Sumatra Treaty marks the formal close of the half-century of Dutch impotence on Sumatra that had flowed from the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] and the careless Acheh declaration of 1824.

## Background

From 1824 onwards successive British governments, in Van der Kemp's words, had "attached themselves to the letter of the treaty of 1824." [[Lord Palmerston|Lord Palmerston]] in particular opposed every Dutch extension of authority on [[Sumatra]] on the ground that the treaty had granted no affirmative right — and that the Acheh independence declaration, by reserving the status of that state, must imply a general restriction on Dutch expansion. The result was the prolonged [[Paper war|paper war]] of the 1820s–1860s, exemplified by [[Robert Fullerton|Fullerton]]'s [[Pinang Nota (1825)|Pinang Nota of 1825]] and by recurring disputes over differential duties and the [[Riouw]] archipelago.

By 1871 the Dutch were anxious to subdue the disturbances on Sumatra's east coast (the [[Padri War|Padri troubles]] and later interventions) and to deal at last with [[Acheh]]. To do so they needed the removal of the British veto. Negotiations accordingly opened in London, conducted on the Dutch side by ministers [[Gericke]] and [[E. de Waal|Van Bosse]] (see [[Van Bosse's Second Chamber statement (17 January 1872)]]) and on the British side by [[Lord Derby|Lord Derby]], [[Sir Hercules Robinson|Sir Hercules Robinson]] and [[Sir Robert Herbert|Sir Robert Herbert]].

## Terms and the Cape of Guinea Question

By the Sumatra Treaty Britain renounced the Acheh reservation and undertook to make no further representations against Dutch expansion on Sumatra. A second treaty of the same year, however, transferred the Dutch possessions on the [[Cape of Guinea|Cape of Guinea]] (the Dutch Gold Coast) to Britain — a cession known to Dutch history as the [[Cape of Guinea cession (1871)|Cape of Guinea cession (1871)]]. Van der Kemp records that there has been much discussion whether the two treaties "stood and fell together." [[E. de Waal|De Waal]], the former minister of colonies, dismissed as "a historical inaccuracy" the suggestion that the Cape had been the price of Britain's abandonment of the Acheh reservation: according to De Waal he himself, as minister, had offered the Cape to Britain simply to put an end to inter-tribal warfare among the negroes, and only later had Britain demanded that the two treaties be linked. Van der Kemp, however, suggests that public opinion in the Netherlands broadly accepted the linkage, even if it underrated the importance of the Acheh concession.

## Significance

The Sumatra Treaty opened the way to the [[Acheh]] expedition of 1873 and the long [[Acheh War]] that followed — events Van der Kemp alludes to but does not narrate, his purpose being to trace the diplomatic chain that made them possible. In the Second Chamber on 17 January 1872 the minister of colonies [[E. de Waal|Van Bosse]] declared, in the debate on the treaty's ratification, that "it is article 3 of the treaty of 1824 that has given rise to the conflict. It is in this article that a provision is given from which England derived, and could derive, certain rights. And over the extent of those rights, and over the meaning of this article, conflict has repeatedly been carried on since 1824." The Sumatra Treaty was at last to remove that conflict — at the price of a colonial war that would last, with interruptions, until 1903.

Van der Kemp notes that the very fact that a separate treaty was required in 1871 to undo what had been done in 1824 is itself testimony to the magnitude of the original mistake: a treaty intended to define the spheres of the two powers had, by the casual insertion of an Acheh reservation, created an ambiguity that could only be resolved by another treaty half a century later.
