**The Treaty of 1857 with the Sultan of Atjèh** — in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s history, the diplomatic agreement concluded in [[1857]] between the [[Netherlands|Netherlands-Indian government]] and the [[Sultan of Atjeh|Sultan of Atjèh]], negotiated by the governor of [[Sumatra's West Coast|Soematra's Westkust]] (identified by Kreemer as [[Generaal van Swieten|Van Swieten]], the same officer who would later command the [[Second Aceh Expedition (1873-1874)|second Atjèh expedition]] of [[1873]]–[[1874]]) — was the first formal treaty between the Dutch and the Atjèhnese sultanate since the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 1824]]. Its Article 7 designated the governor of the West Coast as the Dutch government's representative for all Atjèh-related affairs.

## The negotiation

Kreemer situates the treaty at the end of the [[De Haai flag visit (1855)|flag-visit episode]]. After the hostile reception of the war-brig *De Haai* in [[1855]] and the Sultan's double-dealing reception of a larger ship in [[1856]], the Sultan "had nu om toenadering gevraagd en aan onze zijde haastte men zich aan zijn verlangen gevolg te geven. Zoo werd aan den gouverneur der Westkust in [[1857]] opgedragen zich naar Atjèh te begeven, om met den Soeltan over een tractaat te onderhandelen en hem een brief en geschenken van den Gouverneur-Generaal te overhandigen. Hoewel de houding van den Soeltan bij de onderhandeling in vele opzichten te wenschen overliet, werd het tractaat ten slotte toch tot stand gebracht" — "had now asked for a rapprochement, and on our side one hastened to comply with his desire. Thus the governor of the West Coast was instructed in 1857 to proceed to Atjèh, to negotiate a treaty with the Sultan and to deliver to him a letter and gifts from the Governor-General. Although the Sultan's bearing during the negotiation left much to be desired in many respects, the treaty was in the end nevertheless concluded."

Kreemer notes that the treaty is printed as Bijlage III in [[E. B. Kielstra|De Klerck]]'s *De Atjèh-oorlog*, and that the voyage of [[Generaal van Swieten|generaal Van Swieten]] is described in F. Q. Hoffman's *Verhaal der Expeditie van 1857* ([[1873]]).

## The failure of the treaty

"Nooit echter is van het tractaat van 1857 eenig nut, hoe gering ook, ondervonden. Integendeel na dit tractaat nam Atjèh meer en meer een vijandige houding tegenover ons aan en bleek de 'bestendige vrede, vriendschap en goede verstandhouding', waarvan het tractaat sprak, een doode letter te zijn" — "Never, however, was any benefit, however slight, derived from the treaty of 1857. On the contrary, after this treaty Atjèh assumed more and more a hostile attitude towards us, and the 'perpetual peace, friendship and good understanding' of which the treaty spoke proved to be a dead letter."

## Significance

The treaty of 1857 is, in Kreemer's narrative, the high-water mark of Dutch treaty-diplomacy with Atjèh — and its failure is the proof that treaty-diplomacy could not substitute for territorial control. The designation of the West Coast governor as the Dutch point-of-contact (Article 7) foreshadows the administrative structure that would later govern the [[Gouvernement Atjeh en Onderhoorigheden|Gouvernement Atjèh en Onderhoorigheden]]. The treaty's life as a "dead letter" reproduces, at the diplomatic level, the same pattern of Dutch acquiescence that characterised the entire [[1824]]–[[1873]] period, and prepares the argument that only the abrogation of the 1824 framework — achieved by the [[Sumatra Treaty of 1871|Sumatra-tractaat of 1871]] — could open the way to a forceful Atjèh policy.
