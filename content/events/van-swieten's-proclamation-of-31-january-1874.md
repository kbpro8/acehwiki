**Van Swieten's Proclamation of 31 January 1874** was the formal Dutch proclamation issued by [[Jan van Swieten]] after the [[Capture of the Kraton (24 January 1874)]]. In it, the Dutch government declared [[Groot-Atjeh]] annexed by right of conquest, invited the chiefs of the three [[Sagi|sagis]] and the [[Onderhoorigheden]] to submit in writing, and announced that Dutch rule had replaced the authority of the sultan.

The proclamation is one of the central documents of the early [[Aceh War]], because it translated a military success into a formal claim of sovereignty. It also showed how quickly the Dutch moved from taking the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] to declaring political possession of the country.

## Background

The proclamation came after two linked events. On [[24 January]] [[1874]], Dutch troops entered the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] and found it abandoned. On [[26 January]] [[1874]], the sultan died of cholera at [[Pager Ajer|Pagger Ajer]]. At that point, Dutch authorities concluded that a treaty was no longer realistic and that a direct proclamation of conquest was the next step.

## Main points of the proclamation

The proclamation stated, in substance, that:

- the people of [[Groot-Atjeh]] had been defeated after brave resistance
- the [[Kraton of Aceh|Kraton]] had been taken
- the sultan was dead
- the land therefore belonged to the [[Dutch East Indies Government|Netherlands Indies government]] by right of conquest
- chiefs who submitted in writing would receive Dutch protection
- trade could reopen in places that submitted, except for the trade in weapons of war
- only the Dutch flag and the flags of friendly powers would be recognized
- religion and local institutions would be respected
- those who did not submit within a reasonable time would be treated as hostile

The Dutch higher authorities approved this course by telegram on [[1 February]] [[1874]].

## What the proclamation tried to do

The proclamation was not only legal language. It was also a political tool. It tried to separate chiefs willing to deal with the Dutch from chiefs who still resisted. It promised protection, religious freedom, and continuity of local institutions, while also threatening hostile measures against those who refused submission.

This mixture of promise and threat became typical of Dutch policy in the early war.

## Immediate limits of the proclamation

The proclamation did not end resistance. Dutch control after [[31 January]] [[1874]] was still limited and contested. Soon afterward there were further operations such as the [[Expedition to Bital (12 February 1874)]] and the [[Expedition to Ketapang Doewa (15 February 1874)]], which showed that the surrounding country had not submitted simply because the proclamation had been issued.

Later critics also emphasized this gap between paper and reality. In the public debate summarized by [[Brutus]], taking the capital and making grand declarations did not prove that the Dutch had truly subdued [[Aceh|Atjeh]].

## Later importance

The proclamation remained important long after [[1874]] because it was the foundation of the Dutch claim that sovereignty had passed from the sultan to the colonial state. Later parliamentary critics pointed back to it as one of the boldest and most consequential acts of the early war.

## Significance

**Van Swieten's Proclamation of 31 January 1874** is significant because it marked the moment when the Dutch turned battlefield success into a formal annexation claim. It did not create peace, but it did create the legal and political basis on which later Dutch rule in Aceh was built.
