**Mendez Pinto's Aroe mission (1539)** was a Portuguese diplomatic and scouting voyage from [[Malaka]] to the [[Aroe Kingdom]] on [[Sumatra]]'s east coast, undertaken by Mendez Pinto under orders from Governor [[Pero de Faria]] on [[5 October]] [[1539]], in the midst of [[Acheh]]'s expansion against its Malay neighbors. Pinto's account, though filtered through Malay *wangsalan* (punning veiled speech), provides the only eyewitness European description of Aroe on the eve of its fall.

## Context

In [[1539]] Pinto arrived in [[Malaka]] with the new governor. The [[Sultan of Acheh]], emboldened by his recent adoption of Islam, was attacking surrounding states. The "king" of the [[Batu Islands]] — the Ophir gold districts off Sumatra's west coast — sent to Malaka an offer of camphor and *kajoe garoe*, promising to recognize the King of Portugal as suzerain in return for protection and free trade.

Lacking troops, Pero de Faria dispatched Pinto with munitions and a small trading cargo in a caravel under a Malacca-born Muslim captain.

## Voyage to the west coast

Pinto first crossed to Sumatra's west coast near [[Tikoe]] and [[Priaman]], in the country of [[Minangkabau]] opposite [[Goenoeng Pasaman]] ("Ophir"). He met the local *bandahara* ("gouverneur du royaume") and, pretending to reconnoitre [[Acheh]], witnessed the ruler kneel before a "carcasse d'une tête de vache couronnée de fleurs... les cornes dorées" — glossed by de Roo as veiled speech for the dead body of Minang-kebo with golden horns (*tandoek*), i.e. a *tanda* of the gold kingdom, nailed to the dividing mountain wall.

Nine days later Pinto joined a force of 15,000 men, 40 elephants and twelve small guns for an expedition against Acheh. Several guns bore French arms, taken in [[1526]] from a French ship; de Roo notes three ships left [[Dieppe]] in [[1527]] for pepper and reached Tikoe, where they were wrecked and plundered. The campaign faltered when Achehnese reinforcements arrived; the men of *Panaju* withdrew. The Batu king, who had already lost three sons to Acheh, parted from Pinto with gifts, realizing no military aid would come.

## Voyage to Aroe

Less than a month later, the king of [[Aroe Kingdom|Aroe]] likewise begged aid against Acheh. Pinto embarked on [[5 October]] [[1539]], sailed along the coast to the Nine Islands off [[Perak]], crossed the [[Strait of Malacca]], and entered [[Aroebai|Aroe Bay]].

Close inshore the pilot named the place *Paneticano* — which de Roo decodes as veiled Malay *roe-pang moes-tika-noe-sa*, referring to the island off Aroe famed for white magical bezoar stones, known to the Dutch as [[Poelo Kampi]] or Poelo Koempej.

Pinto sailed up what was probably the [[Besitang|Besitang River]] to the *ville d'Aru*. He found the raja supervising a defensive trench. The raja, overjoyed at the letter from Malaka, embraced Pinto and unusually presented him to his wife. Aroe could muster only 6,000 men; Acheh was approaching with about 130 vessels.

## Aftermath

Pinto stayed only days. When news came the Achehnese fleet would arrive in eight days, the raja evacuated his queen and non-combatants to the forest — the queen borne on an elephant with forty or fifty elders. Three days later the fleet arrived under [[Heredin Mahomet]].

Pinto, asked when he wished to leave, chose to depart at once for a planned China trading voyage. The raja gave him a pair of gold arm-bands and thanks for the munitions. The fall of Aroe that followed is documented in [[Fall of Aroe to Acheh (1539)]].

## Significance

Pinto's mission is significant as the only eyewitness European account of Aroe before its fall — a snapshot of a Malay-style entrepôt kingdom, its political structure (the *xabandar*, *bendahara*, *hoeloebalang* and *hamba radja*), its economy (the *minjak tanah* trade of [[Pangkalan Susu]]), and its military situation (6,000 men against 130 Achehnese vessels) in the final days before its destruction.
