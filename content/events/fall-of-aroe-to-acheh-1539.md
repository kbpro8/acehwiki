**Fall of Aroe to Acheh (1539)** was the conquest of the [[Aroe Kingdom]] on [[Sumatra]]'s east coast by forces of the [[Sultan of Acheh]] in late [[1539]], following the departure of [[Mendez Pinto's Aroe mission (1539)|Mendez Pinto]], an event that gave Acheh control of the northern [[Strait of Malacca]].

## Background

In [[1539]] the [[Sultan of Acheh]], emboldened by his recent adoption of Islam, was attacking surrounding states. The [[Batu Islands|king of the Batu Islands]] had already lost three sons to Achehnese raids and sought Portuguese aid. When [[Mendez Pinto's Aroe mission (1539)|Mendez Pinto]] arrived at Aroe on [[5 October]] [[1539]], the raja of Aroe reported that he could muster only 6,000 men against an Achehnese fleet of approximately 130 vessels.

Pinto stayed only days. When news came that the Achehnese fleet would arrive in eight days, the raja evacuated his queen and non-combatants to the forest — the queen borne on an elephant with forty or fifty elders. Pinto departed for a planned China trading voyage, leaving Aroe with munitions from the Portuguese governor but without military support.

## The Achehnese fleet

Three days after Pinto's arrival, the Achehnese fleet appeared at the mouth of the [[Panetican]] river (the [[Besitang|Besitang River]]). It was commanded by [[Heredin Mahomet]] — described by Pinto as *beau-frère du mesme Roy, marié avec une sienne soeur, & Gouverneur du Royaume de Baarros* — brother-in-law of the Sultan of Acheh and governor of [[Barus]].

## Defense and betrayal

The raja of Aroe, with 6,000 countrymen, initially resisted stoutly. De Roo records that the defense collapsed through treachery: a *Cacis* (religious teacher) bought by Acheh opened a gate, delivering the fortification into enemy hands. The raja was killed in the fighting, precipitating panic.

## Flight of the queen

Upon learning of her husband's death, the [[Queen of Aroe|queen]] attempted vengeance, attacking plunderers, but was forced to withdraw. She retreated to a place called *minha cumbaa* — identified by de Roo as *minjak sembawa*, named for the famed *minjak tanah* (earth-oil) of the region — and embarked for Portuguese [[Malaka]].

The governor of Malaka received her with honour but could offer no armed assistance. She then proceeded to [[Bintan]] and sought aid from [[Johor]]. The Sultan of Johor married her, attacked the Achehnese at Aroe, and temporarily reconquered the country.

## Consequences

The reconquest did not last. De Roo states that "ten langen leste moest Aroe toch weder worden prijsgegeven; met het rijk der Djao, of, zooals de Portugeezen schreven: Jaoa, was het voor goed gedaan."

Once ranked in the [[Sejarah Melayu]] alongside [[Pasej]] and [[Malaka]], and ruling a coast from [[Tamijang]] to [[Siak]], Aroe declined into poverty and piracy, remembered in the colonial period only as a *soekoe Aroe*.

Strategically, Aroe's fall allowed Acheh to use it as the station from which to dominate the Strait, interdict Portuguese shipping to [[Sunda]], the [[Moluccas]] and [[China]], and reap the tolls formerly collected at Aroe's *pabgjan*. The fall of Aroe in [[1539]] is the moment Acheh secured the northern Strait — a precondition for its sixteenth-century wars with [[Malaka]] and [[Johor]].

## Later traces

In [[1888]] Van Rijn van Alkemade still found local memory of the kingdom in [[Aroebai|Aroe Bay]], and the toponym Si Djanda on the [[Besitang]] was linked to *Djanda Gidau*, the widow — likely the same queen — who fled inland after the [[1539]] sack.
