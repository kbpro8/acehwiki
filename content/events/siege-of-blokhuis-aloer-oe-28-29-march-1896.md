**The Siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe (28–29 March 1896)** was the two-day investment and assault of the [[Dutch]] blockhouse (*blokhuis*) at [[Aloer Oe]] — a small fortified outpost attached to the post of [[Senelop]] on the western [[buitenlinie]] — by hostile [[Achehnese]] bands in the opening hours of the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]]. The garrison — one *maréchaussée* brigade, a European sergeant of artillery named [[son]] (No. 40248), and three European gunners serving the *mitrailleur* — was commanded by the European sergeant of infantry [[Havenga]] (No. 19908), the brigade commander and *blokhuiscommandant*, whose energetic preparations and presence of mind enabled the post to hold until it was relieved by the column of [[Majoor G. P. M. van der Noordaa|Maj. van der Noordaa]] on [[30 March]] [[1896]]. As described in [[A. Kruisheer|Kruisheer]]'s *ATJEH '96*, the defence of Aloer Oe — together with the concurrent sieges of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]] — marked the strategic collapse of the *buitenlinie* in the last week of [[March]] [[1896]].

## Background

The *blokhuis* at [[Aloer Oe]] was one of a chain of small blockhouses — including [[blokhuis Montassik]], [[blokhuis Toei Selimbing]], and the *blokhuizen* attached to [[Senelop]] — that the Dutch had constructed in the spring of [[1895]] to anchor the western *buitenlinie* beyond the post of [[Ana' Galong]]. The garrison was deliberately small: one brigade of the elite Ambonese-manned corps, plus a European artillery sergeant and three European gunners to serve a early machine-gun mounted in the work. Command devolved on the senior European NCO present, the infantry sergeant [[Havenga]] (No. 19908), brigade commander and *blokhuiscommandant*.

By [[28 March]] [[1896]] the strategic situation had deteriorated sharply. [[Teuku Umar]]'s [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection]] — already signalled by his failure to return weapons and ammunition lent for a specific purpose, by his construction of *verschanzingen* opposite the Dutch post at [[Lam-Djamoe]], and by the defection of several of his followers to the resistance — was about to break into open hostilities. [[Controleur 2e kl. bij het binnenlandsch bestuur Gisolf|Controleur Gisolf]]'s warnings of Umar's "more than suspicious attitude" had been overruled by [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. Deykerhoff]], and Umar had had, in Kruisheer's words, "the audacity" (the brass) to attend a conference at the Governor's office on [[28 March]] [[1896]] despite the warnings. That same night, the Achehnese struck.

## The Defence

Havenga had not waited for the storm to break. On taking command of the *blokhuis* he had immediately improved its defensibility: he cleared all combustibles from the immediate surroundings, improved the enclosure, laid barbed wire, planted thorn-branches, filled the water and wood supplies, and raised the parapet with sandbags. Around midday on [[28 March]] [[1896]] the *blokhuis* was fired upon from approximately 200 m. The Achehnese then worked their way forward under cover of the trees until they had closed to within 75 m of the work, from which they shouted threats and offers of life and freedom for surrender. By nightfall their numbers had grown; some nestled under a bridge only 120 m from the parapet.

The *mitrailleur* failed twice during the action. On each occasion the European sergeant of artillery [[son]] (No. 40248) repaired the weapon under fire, and on the eventual evacuation of the post it was son who safely brought the *mitrailleur* and 20,800 cartridges back to [[Senelop]]. At approximately 1:15 a.m. on [[29 March]] [[1896]] the enemy in the edge of [[Lam Raja]] raised a fearful yelling and a cannon shot. A grenade made a large hole in the front roof slope of the *blokhuis*; of eleven grenades fired by the enemy, four burst inside the emplacement without, miraculously, hitting anyone. An Ambonese corporal began to play the harmonica, and the Ambonese garrison sang along — Kruisheer records that the morale of the garrison was excellent throughout. The enemy then attempted a ruse: their buglers blew the signal "for the doctor," intending to lure out a relief detachment from [[Senelop]]. Havenga, recognising the deception, repeated the same signal but followed it with the stand-down call, which Senelop correctly interpreted. At approximately 2 a.m. Senelop talso the path toward Aloer Oe and brought off the garrison, with its *mitrailleur*, its ammunition, and its wounded, in good order.

## Aftermath

The evacuation of [[Aloer Oe]] on the night of [[29 March]] [[1896]] was a tactical success — the garrison was extricated intact, with its weapon and 20,800 rounds — but a strategic confession. The western *buitenlinie* was no longer tenable. Within days, [[Senelop]] itself would be invested; the [[Siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe (28–29 March 1896)|siege of Aloer Oe]] merged imperceptibly into the wider [[Battle of Senelop (17 April 1896)|battle of Senelop]] of [[17 April]] [[1896]] and the [[Evacuation of Anak-Galoeng, Lam-Barih, Lam-Soet, Senelop (17 April 1896)|evacuation of the western buitenlinie]] on the same day. [[Havenga]] and [[son]] were singled out for praise; the latter's salvage of the *mitrailleur* and its 20,800 cartridges was a particularly welcome economy in a campaign that would consume millions of rounds before its end.

## Significance

The siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe was one of three simultaneous sieges — together with those of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]] — that announced the [[Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)|defection of Teuku Umar]] in arms. Its principal significance lies in the testimony it bears to the quality of the European and Ambonese NCOs who held the *buitenlinie* in the spring of [[1896]]: men like [[Havenga]] and [[son]], who, in the absence of any commissioned officer, prepared their post, repaired their weapon under fire, resisted a ruse, and extricated their garrison with honour. The episode also exposed the structural weakness of the *buitenlinie* — a chain of small works held by handfuls of men, each of which could be isolated and overwhelmed once the Achehnese were unified and Dutch-supplied.
