The **concept-convention of [[24 July]] [[1820]]** — referred to by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] as the Dutch *ontwerp-conventie* of July 1820 — was the draft convention proposed by the Dutch plenipotentiaries [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] and [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] in the course of the [[Fagel-Elout negotiations (August 1820)|Fagel-Elout negotiations]] with the British plenipotentiaries Lord Castlereagh and [[George Canning|Canning]]. Its first article expressly disavowed the conduct of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and his subordinates and recognised Dutch rights in the archipelago; but the convention was not accepted, and the negotiations broke up without agreement. The text nevertheless survived in the diplomatic record and was revived, with consequences fatal to the Dutch case, in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|negotiations of 1824]].

## Background

By mid-1820 the disputes between Britain and the Netherlands over the execution of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|treaty of 13 August 1814]] had reached a point at which both sides were willing to consider a revision. The Dutch plenipotentiaries [[Hendrik Fagel|Fagel]] and [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] arrived in London armed with instructions to seek, among other things, an express British recognition that the conduct of British agents in the archipelago — particularly [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s incipient activities at [[Bencoolen]] and his intrigues against Dutch authority on [[Sumatra]] — had been contrary to the spirit of the 1814 settlement. The result was the draft convention of [[24 July]] [[1820]].

## Article 1: disavowal of Raffles

The most important article of the concept-convention was article 1, which expressly disavowed the conduct of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] and his subordinates, and recognised Dutch rights in the archipelago. Van der Kemp notes that the Dutch plenipotentiaries had "at that time" — i.e. in 1820 — already "desired an express treaty-disavowal of what had been done by Raffles and his subordinates" (*eene uitdrukkelijke afkeuring bij tractaat van hetgeen door Raffles en zijne ondergeschikten was misdaan*). This article was the direct precursor of the demand, renewed in 1824, that the British explicitly repudiate Raffles's conduct in [[Sumatra]] and the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Acheh convention]] of 1819.

## Failure and revival

The concept-convention was rejected. The British plenipotentiaries refused to subscribe to an article that would have committed them to an express disavowal of their own subordinate's conduct, and the negotiations broke up without agreement on the substantive points. "Everything remained in consideration", Van der Kemp records: "those pieces had by no means the force of a treaty, intended to operate for the future."

The concept-convention's article 1 nevertheless survived in the diplomatic record. When the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|negotiations of 1824]] opened, the British plenipotentiaries [[George Canning|Canning]] and [[Charles Watkin Williams Wynn|Wynn]] were able to point to the Dutch desire for an express disavowal of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] as evidence that the Dutch themselves had been dissatisfied with the 1814 settlement; and the demand was eventually met, not by an express article in the treaty of 17 March 1824, but by a British nota in which the British government "took note of" the disavowal. The result, Van der Kemp remarks, was that the disavowal took the form of a British *declaration* in which "England's attitude" was itself the subject of the testimony — a transformation he considered a diplomatic defeat for the Netherlands.

## Significance

The concept-convention of 24 July 1820 is important in Van der Kemp's argument as the first formal Dutch attempt to obtain British recognition of Dutch rights in the archipelago and British disavowal of [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s conduct. Its rejection in 1820 left the question open; its revival in 1824, in the form of the British nota annexed to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]], produced the awkward compromise by which the Dutch undertook to respect the independence of [[Acheh]] — a gratuitous concession that, in Van der Kemp's view, paralysed Dutch policy on Sumatra for nearly half a century. The concept-convention thus belongs to the same train of diplomatic events as the [[Fagel-Elout negotiations (August 1820)|August 1820 nota exchange]] and the [[Raising of the Acheh treaty in London negotiations|raising of the Acheh treaty]] in February 1824.
