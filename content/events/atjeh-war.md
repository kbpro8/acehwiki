**The [[Atjeh War]]** (also known as the [[Aceh War]] or *Atjehoorlog*) was the prolonged military conflict between the [[Dutch East Indies]] and the [[Sultanate of Aceh]] that lasted from [[1873]] to [[1903]]. Spanning thirty years, it was one of the longest and most costly colonial wars in Dutch history, and its final phase—guided by the collaboration of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] and [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]]—established the model for Dutch active pacification in the [[Buitenbezittingen]].

## Origins and first expeditions (1873-1874)

The war began with the first Dutch expedition in [[1873]], which ended in disaster. The second expedition, led by [[Generaal Van Swieten]], achieved greater success and finally captured the [[kraton]] (the royal palace) on [[24 January]] [[1874]]. Two days later, the young sultan died, and the Dutch declared their authority over the three [[sagi]] (bondgenootschappen) of [[Groot-Atjeh]]: the [[XXV Moekims]], the [[XXII Moekims]], and the [[XXVI Moekims]].

The initial Dutch expectation—that the Atjeh people would submit voluntarily once a strong position was maintained around the [[kraton]] and the mouth of the [[Atjeh River]]—proved mistaken. The inhabitants of the [[XXII Moekims]], led by [[Panglima Polim]] (then [[Sri Moeda Perkasa]]), played a dominant role in the resistance, even before Dutch troops entered their territory.

## The period of strategic confusion (1874-1890)

Over the next sixteen years, Dutch strategy shifted repeatedly. Successive governors—[[Generaal Van Swieten]], [[Generaal Pel]], [[Generaal Wiggers van Kerchem]], [[Generaal Diemont]], [[Kolonel K. van der Heyden]] (later [[Generaal Van der Heyden]]), [[Pruys van der Hoeven]], [[Kolonel Haus]], [[Laging Tobias]], [[Kolonel Demmeni]] (later [[Generaal Demmeni]]), and [[Generaal Van Teyn]]—applied different systems: blockades, shipping regulations, the [[Plan-Pel]], reconciliation policies, civil administration, attempts to restore the sultanate, and the [[Concentrated Line]] (completed in [[1886]]). None of these approaches produced lasting results.

In the analysis of [[Dr. J. M. Somer]], this period was characterised by a "stelsel der stelselloosheid"—a system of systemlessness. Thirteen [[Ministers of Colonies]], five [[Gouverneurs-Generaal]], and nine civil and military governors of [[Atjeh]] raised conflicting opinions on the course to follow, producing rapid strategic turnover without continuity. Political interference repeatedly restrained military action, preventing the armed forces from acting with decisive effect.

## The alliance policy and its collapse (1890-1896)

In [[1890]] the establishment of the [[Corps Maréchaussée]] signalled a shift toward more active operations. The appointment of [[Gouverneur Deykerhoff]] in [[January 1892]] introduced the alliance policy: the Dutch would use [[Atjeh]] allies—particularly [[T. Oema]]—to suppress resistance, avoiding direct military action with Dutch troops. [[Oemar]] was armed and subsidised extensively, despite the explicit warnings of [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]].

The policy collapsed in [[1896]] when [[Oemar]] defected, turning his modern Dutch rifles against his former patrons. This catastrophe—described as the moment when [[Oemar]] "threw off the mask"—confirmed [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s analysis and triggered the strategic reversal: the appointment of [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] in [[1896]] to lead military operations, followed by the formal adoption of active subjugation through the [[Indisch Besluit]] of [[14 September]] [[1896]].

## The active subjugation phase (1898-1903)

In [[March 1898]] three government decisions—on [[5 March]] (the [[Pidië Expedition]]), [[15 March]] (appointment of [[Snouck Hurgronje]] as adviser), and [[25 March]] (appointment of [[Van Heutsz]] as governor)—formalised the new approach. The [[Pidië Expedition]] began on [[1 June]] [[1898]], captured [[Keumala]] on [[14 June]] [[1898]], and established the operational model for the remaining years: sustained military pressure guided by ethnographic intelligence.

Over the next five years, Dutch forces pursued [[Panglima Polim]] relentlessly through the [[XXII Moekims]], the mountains, and the highlands. In [[1903]], [[Polim]] submitted unconditionally. With this event, the thirty-year war came to its effective end. Although some work remained—roads to be built, the [[Gajoelanden]] to be administered, a few remaining hostile bands to be pursued—the fundamental resistance had collapsed.

## Legacy

The [[Aceh War]] demonstrated that military force alone—without ethnographic understanding—could not achieve pacification, while ethnographic understanding—without military execution—could not achieve territorial control. The partnership between [[Van Heutsz]] and [[Snouck Hurgronje]] established a new model for Dutch colonial governance, combining scholarship with military strategy. The consolidation of Dutch authority in [[Atjeh]] laid the groundwork for the extension of effective authority to other parts of the [[Buitenbezittingen]] after [[1902]].
