The **Dutch policy of 'concentration'** (Dutch *concentratiepolitiek*) was the military and political strategy adopted by the Netherlands-Indian government in the early 1880s in its war against [[Acheh]], by which the Dutch withdrew their forces behind a fortified line — the so-called *linie* — enclosing [[Kuta Raja|Kuta Raja]] (Banda Acheh) and abandoned active operations in the interior. The policy, described by C. Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese*, marked a turning-point in the [[Dutch War in Acheh|Achehnese War]]: it was during the period of "concentration" that the Achehnese ulamas rose to prominence as the real leaders of the resistance.

## Origin of the Policy

After the failure of the first Dutch campaigns of 1873–74 and the protracted guerrilla warfare that followed, the Dutch government adopted a defensive posture. Troops were concentrated behind the *linie*, a continuous line of forts round the captured capital. The Achehnese territory beyond the line was left to itself, and the Dutch confined themselves to maintaining a small perimeter of occupied ground. The Dutch settlements within the line were known as the *Gompeuni* territory; the Achehnese who lived or traded within it were a constant source of contention among the war-leaders.

## The Rise of the Ulamas

It was the policy of concentration, Hurgronje argues, that marked the real beginning of the period of the ulamas. Hitherto they had remained somewhat in the background; henceforward they became the real leaders of the war. Each energetic ulama travelled to and fro in the sphere where he possessed or hoped to acquire influence, proclaiming with renewed vehemence the principles of the holy war. The better to prepare for the great work, ruinous mosques were to be repaired, religious worship held in high esteem, and the transgressions of the people checked. The godless pleasures of the Achehnese must come to an end. They did not indeed require military service of every villager, but all had to hold themselves ready to lend assistance in case of need to the bands of volunteers posted in various places, to assist in the construction of the *kutas* or forts, and to take complaints before the teungku under whose jurisdiction they were.

The argument of the ulamas ran as follows: finally there can be no success where small bodies of troops act without central organization; there must be leaders to supervise the whole and keep a watch over the faith. The Raja of Acheh is almost a negligible factor. The uleebalangs, the true chiefs of the country, indolently shut themselves up each within his own landmarks, and are incapable of united action. The leaders of the bands of volunteers fall short of the conditions which must be fulfilled by commanders in the holy war. Thus, say the ulamas, it is on us that the duty rests of regulating the conduct of the jihad.

## Teungku Tiro and the Concentration Period

[[Teungku Tiro|Teungku Tiro]] was par excellence a leader from the time of the "concentration" till the day of his death. He knew as well as anyone that money is the life and soul, even of the holy war, and he pushed on with the utmost zeal the collections of the [[Sabil Contributions|lui sabi (haqq sabil)]]. During the greater portion of the period of his activity, "the Teungku" declared those Achehnese who lived within the *linie* or traded there to be little better than unbelievers, and did not raise a hand in protest when his troops robbed them of their property or even of their life. It was only when [[Teuku Uma|Teuku Uma]] convinced him that it was in many respects desirable for the chiefs and notables within the *linie* to keep in touch with the court — so that the court might draw revenues from the other side of the line and make less frequent demand on the ulamas for financial support — that he at length changed his tactics.

## Significance

The Dutch policy of concentration thus produced, by a striking irony, the very thing it was meant to avert: instead of pacifying the country, it gave the Achehnese resistance its enduring religious and political leadership. Until the abandonment of the policy and the adoption of Hurgronje's own advice — to co-operate with compliant uleebalangs and to undermine the prestige of the ulamas — the war continued with no prospect of Dutch victory.
