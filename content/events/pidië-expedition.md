**The Pidië Expedition** was the military campaign launched by the Dutch forces under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] on [[1 June]] [[1898]] in the landscape of [[Pidië]] (also spelled [[Pedir]]). It marked the operational beginning of the active subjugation policy adopted after the strategic reversal of [[1896]] and served as the decisive introduction to the final phase of the [[Aceh War]].

## Background and strategic purpose

The expedition was approved by government decree of [[5 March]] [[1898]]—one of three closely spaced decisions that also included the appointment of [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]] as adviser ([[15 March]]) and the appointment of [[Van Heutsz]] as civil and military governor ([[25 March]]). The strategic purpose was twofold: first, to secure the north coast route by which [[Panglima Polim]]—the principal resistance leader in the [[XXII Moekims]]—maintained contact with the sea; second, to advance from [[Segli]] in [[Pedir]] over the mountains into [[Polim]]'s territory, cutting off his supply and communication lines.

The instructions carried by [[Van Heutsz]] made explicit reference to [[Panglima Polim]]: after attacking and defeating him, he was to be relentlessly pursued until his submission was obtained. This directive acknowledged, for the first time in official Dutch policy, that [[Polim]]—as head of the [[XXII Moekims]]—was not merely a local chief but the principal obstacle to Dutch authority in [[Groot-Atjeh]].

## The campaign

On [[1 June]] [[1898]], the expedition commenced from [[Segli]] in [[Pedir]]. [[Van Heutsz]] led the force with the full support of his adviser [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]]. The Dutch secured [[Pedir]] and then turned toward the interior mountains, advancing into the [[VII Moekims]]—the mountain district situated between the [[XXII Moekims]] and [[Pedir]]—where [[Panglima Polim]] had positioned his bands.

On [[14 June]] [[1898]]—only two weeks after the expedition began—the Dutch forces captured [[Keumala]], the spiritual and political centre of resistance. The capture shattered the illusion that the sultanate could be restored in [[Groot-Atjeh]] and eliminated the organisational hub of the religious resistance. The fall of [[Keumala]]—described by Dutch sources as the end of illusions regarding the restoration of the sultanate—was the most significant single event of the campaign.

Following the capture of [[Keumala]], [[Van Heutsz]] pursued active military operations against the remaining resistance. The approach—giving no quarter in either political or military terms—replaced the previous system of alternating aggression and passivity with sustained, coordinated pressure. The collaboration between [[Van Heutsz]] and [[Snouck Hurgronje]]—despite their differing orientations—proved highly effective: the scholar provided ethnographic and religious intelligence that guided military targeting, while the officer provided disciplined execution.

## Consequences

The [[Pidië Expedition]] and the subsequent capture of [[Keumala]] established the operational model for the remaining years of the war: direct military action guided by ethnographic analysis, pursued relentlessly until the complete submission of the resistance leadership. The five-year pursuit of [[Panglima Polim]] that followed—culminating in his unconditional submission in [[1903]]—was a direct continuation of the strategy first demonstrated in the [[Pidië Expedition]].

The consolidation of Dutch authority over the [[Onderhoorigheden]]—achieved through the successes of this campaign—laid the foundation for the further extension of Dutch effective authority to other regions of the [[Buitenbezittingen]] after [[1902]]. The partnership between [[Van Heutsz]] and [[Snouck Hurgronje]], formalised through the three government decisions of [[March 1898]], is regarded as one of the most effective collaborations between scholarship and military strategy in colonial history.
