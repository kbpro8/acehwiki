**The unconditional submission of [[Panglima Polim]] in [[1903]]** marked the effective end of the thirty-year [[Aceh War]]. Reported by telegraph in [[August 1903]] and described in the Dutch press as an event of greater significance for the termination of the struggle than the submission of the pretender-sultan himself, the event confirmed that the core of resistance—the hereditary leadership of the [[XXII Moekims]]—had finally been broken.

## The final campaign

The pursuit of [[Panglima Polim]] began in earnest after the [[Pidië Expedition]] of [[1898]] and the capture of [[Keumala]] on [[14 June]] [[1898]]. The instructions carried by [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] had explicitly directed that [[Polim]] be attacked, defeated, and relentlessly pursued until his submission. Over the next five years, Dutch columns—led by officers including [[Kapitein Colijn]] and [[Onderluitenant Christoffel]]—pursued [[Polim]] through the mountains, forests, and highlands of [[Atjeh]].

[[Polim]] proved extraordinarily resourceful. For months at a time he made himself unfindable to the Dutch spy army—despite being one of the best-known figures in [[Atjeh]]. He operated from the [[Pirastreek]] on the north coast, fleeing to the [[Gajoelanden]] when necessary, always seeking to revive the holy struggle and urging those who had not yet submitted to persist.

In the final phase of the pursuit, [[Polim]]'s bands suffered heavy losses. Chiefs who had long remained loyal came to submit. Finally, both his mother and one of his wives fell into the hands of [[Majoor Van der Maaten]]—an event that, as had been shown with [[Toekoe Eh]] and the pretender-sultan, often provided reason to abandon the struggle.

## The submission

In [[1903]], reports arrived that [[Panglima Polim]] had requested that operations against him be halted, because he wished to come in submission. The governor—[[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]]—refused this request: the order was to continue pursuit until unconditional surrender. Only then did [[Polim]] bow his proud neck. He asked for a warship to bring him to [[Koeta Radja]], wishing to travel as a grand seigneur to his conqueror. The governor raised no objection, for [[Van Heutsz]] and [[Dr. Snouck Hurgronje]] shared admiration for [[Polim]]'s energy and wished to honour him appropriately.

[[Polim]] submitted unconditionally. The Dutch government did not know exactly what treatment it would assign him, but it was presumed he would not have cause for complaint. [[Van Heutsz]] considered guilty only those chiefs who had first chosen the Dutch side and then abandoned it—those who had committed treason. The [[Polims]] had always been generous, honourable enemies who had done nothing to forfeit Dutch respect.

His unconditional submission was demanded precisely so that victory would not be bought through weak promises, but so that the right to grant mercy as a powerful victor would be preserved.

## The end of the war

With the submission of [[Panglima Polim]] in [[1903]], the thirty-year [[Aceh War]] came to an end. Although work remained—roads to be built in the coastal states and into the interior, administration to be established in the [[Gajoelanden]] (where only flying columns had penetrated), and a few remaining hostile bands to be pursued—the fundamental resistance had collapsed. All chiefs of significance had submitted, and the population everywhere—into the deepest interior and highest mountains—recognised Dutch authority.

The war itself was over. What remained was only the tail end of the conflict—necessary work, but no longer the fundamental struggle for control of [[Groot-Atjeh]].
