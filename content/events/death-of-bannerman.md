The **death of Colonel [[Colonel John Alexander Bannerman|John Alexander Bannerman]]**, Governor of [[Penang|Prince of Wales' Island]], on [[8 August 1819|8 August 1819]], removed one of the principal figures in the early phase of the Acheh controversy and altered the balance of British policy in the Straits at a critical moment. Bannerman had been throughout [[1819]] in sharp conflict with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Sir Stamford Raffles]] over the conduct of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs Acheh Commission]], and his death occurred before the Calcutta government could resolve the dispute between them.

Bannerman, an experienced Madras officer and former Member of Parliament and East India Company director, had succeeded [[Governor Petrie|Governor Petrie]] at Penang and "viel als zoodanig midden in de hem onbekende Atjesche quaestiën" — fell, in that capacity, into the middle of Achehnese questions with which he was unfamiliar. His differences with Raffles had been numerous and fundamental. He had insisted that the Acheh commission proceed at once to [[Acheh]] with the military force Calcutta had provided; Raffles, after his [[Raffles' detour to Karimon Islands|detour to the Karimon Islands]] and the [[Founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]], preferred to go first to [[Pedir]] and to [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Jauhar Alum]]. Bannerman and [[Captain Coombs|Captain Coombs]] both held this to be contrary to the Calcutta instructions; the Bengal government, in its report of [[7 October 1820|7 October 1820]], subsequently agreed. The dispute extended to the disposal of the Bengal artillery detachment, to the [[Dispatch of the Minto to Pedir|dispatch of the Minto to Pedir]], and to the very substance of the commission's instructions.

The conflict was still unresolved when Bannerman died. Raffles, in his reply to Calcutta of [[31 December 1819|31 December 1819]], denied having told Bannerman of any oral instructions at variance with the written ones; but, as [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] drily observes, "toen was echter Bannerman reeds overleden". The governor's death thus left Raffles' account of the controversy uncontradicted by his principal antagonist.

Bannerman's succession by [[W. E. Phillips|W. E. Phillips]] — and ultimately by [[Robert Fullerton|Robert Fullerton]] — was of considerable importance for the subsequent course of British Acheh policy. Phillips and Fullerton, together with the councillors [[W. A. Clubley|Clubley]] and [[R. Ibbetson|Ibbetson]], pursued a more coherent and assertive line than Bannerman had been able to do. It was Phillips who received the [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|letter of the three Sagis]] of [[4 December 1822|4 December 1822]], Phillips who presided over the abortive attempt to call [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] to account during the [[Lucas mission to Telok Semawé (February 1821)|Lucas mission]] of February [[1821]], and Fullerton who, in his [[Fullerton's minute on Acheh (21 March 1825)|minute of 21 March 1825]], would declare the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|treaty of 1819]] a "dead letter". In this sense Bannerman's death, falling where it did, was a small but consequential turning-point in the history of Britain's Acheh entanglement.
