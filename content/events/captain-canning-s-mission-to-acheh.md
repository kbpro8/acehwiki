**Captain Canning's mission to Acheh of [[1814]]** was the visit undertaken by [[Captain Canning|Captain Canning]] on behalf of the government of [[Calcutta]] to the court of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] of [[Acheh]], for the purpose of arranging safe trade. The mission is chiefly remembered for the highly insulting reception it received from the sultan and his court — a reception that discouraged the government of [[Penang]] from active intervention in Achehnese affairs for the next several years and that, by souring Anglo-Achehnese relations, indirectly prepared the ground for the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs commission]] of 1819 and the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]].

## Background

Since [[1802]] the sultanate of [[Acheh]] had been torn by the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] between [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] and the opposition party that would, in mid-1815, install the pretender [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] by means of [[Said Hussein's expedition to Acheh (1815)|Said Hussein's expedition]]. The government of [[Penang|Pinang]] had been entrusted with the supervision of Achehnese affairs since the [[Treaty of Quedah (Penang cession, 1786)|cession of Penang]] in 1786, but had no particular reason to intervene in the dynastic dispute.

In 1814 the Calcutta government dispatched [[Captain Canning|Captain Canning]] to Acheh "to arrange for the security of trade." The mission took place in the closing years of the British interregnum in the Dutch East Indies, while the Netherlands was still under French occupation and the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1814)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 13 August 1814]] had not yet been concluded. British authority in the region was exercised from [[Bencoolen]] and [[Penang]] without serious rivalry.

## The Insulting Reception

The mission met with what Van der Kemp characterises as "a highly insulting reception" from [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Sultan Djohor]] and his court. Van der Kemp's reference to the *Asiatic Journal* (vol. IX, p. 404) preserves the principal record. The treatment was of a piece with the insults offered to earlier British emissaries — most famously the [[Sultan of Acheh's letter to Warren Hastings|sultan's letter to Warren Hastings of 7 November 1786]] — and confirmed [[Captain Francis Light|Captain Francis Light]]'s earlier judgement (15 February 1786) that the Achehnese were "rigid and superstitious Mohammedans, sullen, fickle and treacherous."

## Consequences

The insult offered to [[Captain Canning|Canning]] discouraged the British from any active intervention in Achehnese affairs until the [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|Coombs mission of 1817–18]]. The government of [[Penang|Pinang]] had, Van der Kemp notes, "no special reason to intervene," and chose not to do so when, in mid-1815, [[Said Hussein|Said Hussein]]'s expedition installed [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] as rival sultan. By the time the question of recognition could no longer be avoided, the dispute had become entangled with the larger Anglo-Dutch rivalry in the archipelago and with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]]'s alarm at supposed Dutch overtures to [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]].

The insulting reception of [[Captain Canning|Canning]] is also referenced in the later British record: in the *Asiatic Journal* (vol. IX, p. 40) Van der Kemp notes a passage observing that the 1819 commission required from [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] "an apology for past insults and outrages committed against the British nation" before any negotiation could be opened — an apology which the sultan eventually provided and which the commissioners judged sufficient.
