The **prang sabi** (from the Arabic *sabīl Allāh*, "the way of God") was the Achehnese name for the holy war, the militant defence of Islam that during the closing decades of the nineteenth century became the principal instrument by which the [[ulama|ulamas]] of [[Acheh]] built up what [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]] called "a priestly imperium in imperio." The fund from which the prang sabi was financed was the *jakeuet* (the Qur'anic *zakāt*), the religious tax on property, the seventh share of which—according to Shafi'ite law—was set aside for those engaged in warfare in the path of God.

## The legal basis of the war fund

According to the letter of the Shafi'ite law, the jakeuet, after deduction of a suitable recompense for the collectors and distributors, was to be distributed in equal shares among the eight classes of recipients named in the Qur'an (the poor, the needy, the collectors, the converts whose hearts were to be reconciled, slaves seeking manumission, debtors, those fighting in the way of God, and stranded travellers), with the proviso that a class not represented in the country should be regarded as non-existent. The employment of part of the jakeuet for the holy war was called the "way of God" (*sab'il Allah*); where it could not be so employed, it was, according to some authorities, to be devoted to works of universal benefit to Mohammedans.

## The Achehnese deviation

In Acheh, this manner of employing the jakeuet played a prominent part during the last twenty years preceding the publication of *The Achehnese* (i.e. roughly 1882–1902). For the past ten years in particular, the share assigned to the holy war had been the mainstay of the constantly increasing power of the ulama party. The Achehnese ulamas succeeded in the course of the last few years in collecting as the share for the holy war (*prang sabi*) not merely the seventh part, or as much more as was set free by the absence of the other classes, but the major part of the whole tax—and in founding with this war fund a priestly *imperium in imperio*. This striking deviation from the letter of the law was made possible by the original position of the ulamas as official administrators of the tax: from being its managers they had come to be practically its monopolizers.

## The two faces of the war fund

In strictness, the prang sabi fund was meant to support those who actually engaged in warfare against the infidel invader. In practice, however, the collection and disbursement of the fund became a means of subsistence for a wide range of claimants. Such begging tours in the name of the holy war used to be the chief means of subsistence of the deserters from the Dutch forces in Acheh, and contributed both to the survival of resistance to Dutch rule and to the consolidation of the ulamas' authority over the rural population.

## Significance for the Aceh War

The prang sabi was thus both a religious ideal and a fiscal mechanism. As an ideal, it inspired the popular resistance to the Dutch which formed the principal phase of the [[Aceh War|Acheh War]]; as a fiscal mechanism, it permitted the ulamas to extract from the agricultural population the resources required to maintain that resistance, and at the same time to build up an autonomous clerical power that rivalled and sometimes overshadowed the secular authority of the [[uleëbalang|uleëbalangs]] and the [[Sultan of Acheh]] himself.
