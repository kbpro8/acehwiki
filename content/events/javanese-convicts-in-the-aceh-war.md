**Javanese convicts in the Aceh War** refers to the practice of the Dutch colonial administration sending Javanese prisoners sentenced to forced labour (*dwangarbeid*) to serve as soldiers in the [[Aceh War]] (*Atjeh-oorlog*). This practice was exposed by socialist parliamentarian [[H. van Kol]] during the [[1897]] budget debates and later in [[1901]], who condemned it as a violation of basic humanitarian standards and a mark of colonial barbarism.

## The practice

According to Van Kol's account, Javanese who had been sentenced to forced labour for some criminal offense — and were therefore prisoners who could be made to work but not be sent to die — were being used in wars of conquest. Hundreds died on expeditions from disease or exhaustion, fell victim to enemy fire, or were wounded or mutilated by *klewang* (traditional sword) blows. Van Kol characterized this as a policy driven by "schrielheid" (penuriousness) — the desire to save money rather than hire free labourers.

## Parliamentary protest

Van Kol protested this practice in the strongest terms, calling it a manner of warfare "elke beschaafde natie onwaardig" (unworthy of any civilized nation). He denounced the practice as inhumane and highlighted the hypocrisy of a nation that claimed to be civilized while using prisoners as cannon fodder. The protest, however, "mocht echter bij dezen Minister, noch bij deze Kamer, gehoor vinden" (found no hearing with this Minister, nor with this Chamber).

## Broader context of colonial labour

This practice was part of a broader pattern of labour exploitation in the colony. Van Kol also exposed:
- The "knodarijen" (knaveries/oppressions) to which indigenous peoples were subjected by Chinese opium farmers and their associates.
- Corruption of indigenous chiefs.
- False accusations by powerful Chinese.
- The artificial cultivation of opium addiction.
- The systematic deception and theft in recruiting coolies for the tobacco plantations of Deli.

## Significance

The use of Javanese convicts in the Aceh War is a stark example of the human costs of Dutch colonial expansion. Van Kol's exposure of the practice was part of a larger socialist critique of the colonial system as inherently exploitative and inhumane. The practice continued despite parliamentary protest, demonstrating the colonial state's willingness to sacrifice its own subjects for military objectives. This history has been cited by later scholars as evidence of the brutality of the Aceh War and the broader pattern of forced labour in the Indies.
