The **Makota Alam**, also called the **Adat Kanun**, is the body of written ordinances ascribed to Sultan [[Sultan Meukuta Alam|Iskandar Muda]] (reigned [[1607]]–[[1636]]) and regarded by the Achehnese as the chief monument of royal legislation in their history. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] explains, the sultan "was himself called Meukuta Alam after his death, and his ordinances are thus known as Adat Meukuta Alam." The ordinances themselves, however, are more commonly referred to by the alternative names *Makota Alam* and *Adat Kanun* — the latter a compound of the Arabic *ʽādah* (custom) and *qānūn* (law), which denotes a body of written regulations of royal or governmental origin.

The Makota Alam is preserved, along with other Achehnese *sarakatas* (letters patent), in manuscript documents of varying age and authority. As the Dutch official [[K. F. H. van Langen|Van Langen]] observed in the account quoted by Hurgronje: "We find in existence *undang-undang* or collections of such public ordinances attributed to various Achehnese rulers, all dealing with trade, navigation, import and export duties, administration and ceremonial. Sultan Iskandar Muda (1607–1636) in particular appears to have applied himself to calling into existence a system of rules on these subjects. His code, if we may so term it, is called Makota Alam or Adat Kanun, but copies of this are rare." The code thus belongs to the same class as the *Hukum Kanun* of Malacca and similar compilations in other Malayan countries — codes expressive not of decay, but of a temporary desire for order and reform.

The new elements which the Makota Alam introduces may be classified, in Hurgronje's summary, under three heads. First, there are attempts at an extension of the authority of the sultan by allotting to him — the "king of the port" — a certain control over the succession of the other chieftains of the land, over the disputes of these chiefs with one another or those between the subjects of different chiefs, and over the interests of strangers. Second, there are certain rules intended to bring about a stricter observance of Mohammedan law. Third, there are regulations dealing with the trade then confined to the capital, the shares of certain officials established in the capital in the profits drawn from this trade by the king of the port, the court ceremonial, and the celebration of great religious festivals. Together with the [[Sarakata|sarakatas]] of later sultans, these ordinances constitute the only attempts at centralisation of authority or reform — social, political, or religious — that can be discerned in the whole course of Achehnese history.

The practical fate of the Makota Alam was, however, very limited. The rules dealing with trade and ceremonial exercised, during the reign of their promulgator at least, some degree of authority over life and trade in the seaport town. Of the other innovations, however, hardly a single one has passed from document into actuality, but simply the state of things they reveal as already in being has continued its existence. Perpetual dynastic struggles leading to the death or downfall of the rulers, the unstable character of the rulers themselves, the endeavours of chiefs and officials even in the capital to promote their own authority and profit, the want of a proper machinery of government based on other principles than "might is right" — all this ensures that no period can be pointed out in which even the regulations affecting the capital passed current as the living law of the land.

The Makota Alam has nonetheless exercised a deep influence on the Achehnese political imagination, since "the general tendency is to refer all that has become customary law to the Sultans in general (*adat pôteu meureuhom*) and to Meukuta Alam in particular." How little reliance can be placed on Achehnese data in respect to the origin of such written laws may be seen from the mention of the tomb of [[Teungku Anjong]] (died [[1782]]) in the so-called laws of Meukuta Alam — a saint who, as Hurgronje drily notes, "was not even born in the reign of Meukuta Alam († 1636)." The ascription of all customary law to a single name has thus become a kind of constitutional myth: the Makota Alam is less a code than a fiction of legitimacy, by which the Achehnese explain to themselves the existence of the customs under which they live.
