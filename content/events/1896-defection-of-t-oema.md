**The [[1896]] defection of [[T. Oema]]** (also known as [[Teungku Oemar]], [[Toekoe Oemar]], and after his alliance with the Dutch as [[Toekoe Djohan]] or [[Panglima Prang Besar]]) was the immediate political-military turning point of the [[Aceh War]]. The event—described in Dutch sources as [[Oemar]] "throwing off the mask" (*wierp het masker af*)—demonstrated conclusively that the alliance policy of arming [[Atjeh]] proxies was not only ineffective but actively dangerous.

## The arming and subsidising

Under [[Deykerhoff]]'s administration, [[T. Oema]] had been granted official clemency and permitted to maintain a legion of 250 men at Dutch expense. He later received an additional 1,000 rifles, substantial ammunition, and significant sums of money. This arming was undertaken explicitly against the warnings of [[Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje]], whose [[1892]] report had cautioned that relying on a single powerful ally of uncertain loyalty would prove disastrous.

## The defection

In [[1896]], [[Oema]] revealed his true allegiance. Using the modern Dutch rifles with which he had been equipped, he turned his forces against the Dutch government. Dutch soldiers suddenly found themselves fighting an opponent armed with weapons supplied by the Dutch themselves.

The metaphor of the "masker" (mask) dominated Dutch descriptions of the event: [[Oema]]'s co-operative posture had concealed an underlying resistance loyalty. Once he had obtained the arms and resources he needed, he discarded the mask and joined the resistance—precisely as [[Snouck Hurgronje]] had predicted.

## The strategic reversal

The defection produced an immediate reversal of policy. [[Governor-General Van der Wijck]] ([[1893]]–[[1899]]) recognised that [[Snouck Hurgronje]]'s advice had been correct and must now be followed. In [[1896]] [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] was appointed to lead military operations in [[Groot-Atjeh]], and the active subjugation policy—rather than the alliance policy—was adopted.

The supreme authority then sent [[Generaal Vetter]] as government commissioner with three battalions of infantry. [[Toekoe Djohan]] (as [[Oema]] was called after his alliance with the Dutch) and his bands were defeated and driven out of [[Groot-Atjeh]]. The fallen-away [[XXVI Moekims]] were cleansed of enemies, and part of the [[XXII Moekims]] was treated—though not entirely subdued.

The [[Indisch Besluit]] of [[14 September]] [[1896]] No. 2—issued shortly after the defection—formally adopted the strategic framework recommended by [[Snouck Hurgronje]]: active aggression against the hostile party, combined with efforts to restore confidence in government intentions through promotion of agriculture, industry, and trade. This decision laid the groundwork for the appointment of [[Van Heutsz]] in [[March 1898]] and the subsequent [[Pidië Expedition]].

## Significance

The [[1896]] defection is regarded as the decisive hinge of the [[Aceh War]]. It confirmed that no treaty with a powerful chief and no arming of proxies could achieve pacification. Only direct military action, guided by accurate ethnographic and religious intelligence, could succeed. The event thus marks the transition from the failed policies of the [[1873]]–[[1896]] period to the successful campaign of [[1898]]–[[1903]].
