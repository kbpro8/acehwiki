**Raffles' raid on Palembang in [[1819]]** — in Van der Kemp's phrase the *tocht naar Palembang* — was an armed expedition ordered by [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Sir Stamford Raffles]] against the sultanate of [[Palembang|Palembang]] in southern [[Sumatra]], a region claimed by the Netherlands as lying within its sphere of influence under the conventions then subsisting between the two colonial powers. Van der Kemp treats it as a paradigmatic case of an unauthorized British incursion onto Dutch-claimed territory, and draws a direct comparison between it and the later [[Jameson's raid comparison|Jameson raid]] against the Transvaal.

The episode belongs to the period immediately preceding the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824]], when the frontiers of British and Dutch influence in the archipelago had not yet been formally settled. From [[Bencoolen|Bencoolen]], where he served as lieutenant-governor, Raffles had long chafed at the restrictions imposed on British activity on Sumatra and had been the moving spirit behind the [[Acheh treaty of 1819|Acheh treaty of 1819]] and the [[founding of Singapore|founding of Singapore]]. His decision to send an expedition to Palembang — like his intervention in Acheh — was a unilateral move, undertaken without prior sanction from London or from the Governor-General in [[Bengal|Bengal]].

Van der Kemp had already devoted a separate study to the affair, published in *De Gids* of February [[1898]] under the title *"De geschiedenis van een Engelschen raid op Hollandsch grondgebied"* ("The history of an English raid on Dutch territory"). In the closing pages of the 1900 article he returns to it briefly in order to point the moral that the diplomatic habits of British agents in the East — audacious action first, official disavowal afterwards, with the fruits of the action quietly retained — were by no means confined to the dealings of the East India Company with the Netherlands. The same pattern, he suggests, may be traced in the South African politics of his own day.

The comparison was pointed. In both cases — Raffles' [[1819]] expedition to Palembang and [[Leander Starr Jameson]]'s raid of [[1895]]–[[1896]] against the Transvaal — a British agent acted on Dutch (or Boer) territory in defiance of existing agreements; in both the home government, while formally reproving the agent, took no steps to undo the consequences of his action; and in both the aggrieved party was left to seek redress through negotiations in which British public opinion and parliamentary opposition were invoked as reasons why no full restitution could be made. Van der Kemp's discussion of the Palembang raid thus closes the circle of his argument that the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Treaty of 1824]], and especially the Acheh reservation attached to it, exhibit on a small scale the same diplomatic methods that were later to produce the Transvaal crisis.
