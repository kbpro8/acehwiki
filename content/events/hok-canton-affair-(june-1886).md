**The Hok Canton affair of June 1886** was the capture of the steamship *Hok Canton* by the Atjehese chieftain [[Teuku Umar|Toekoe Oemar]] on the [[Westkust|West Coast]] of [[Atjeh|Atjeh]] in [[June 1886|June]] [[1886]], in the period of Toekoe Oemar's mastery of the West Coast under the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]]. The capture, the subsequent ransom of the prisoners for 25,000 dollars, and the division of the ransom between Toekoe Oemar and [[Kemala|Kemala]] (the hinterland of [[Pedir|Pedir]] to which the irreconcilable principal chiefs had withdrawn) together constitute one of the principal Atjehese acts of piracy against foreign shipping of the middle phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]], and the precedent for the strategy that [[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]] would urge on the Atjehese resistance — to commit acts of piracy *à la* Hok Canton to bring the Dutch into difficulties with England.

## The Capture (June 1886)

By [[1886]] [[Teuku Umar|Toekoe Oemar]] was master on the [[Westkust|West Coast]] of Atjeh, levying taxes, pressing the population, and murdering, robbing, and burning where resistance was offered. In [[June 1886|June]] [[1886]] he captured the steamship *Hok Canton*. The vessel — presumably a merchant steamer in the coasting trade — was taken by Toekoe Oemar's forces, and her crew and any passengers were taken captive.

## The Ransom

For 25,000 dollars the Dutch bought the prisoners free with difficulty. The ransom of 25,000 dollars — approximately ƒ12,500 — was a substantial sum, though less than the ƒ100,000 ransom paid for the crew of the *[[Nisero|Nisero]]* in [[September 1884|September]] [[1884]]. The difficulty of the negotiation, Kruijt notes, indicates the weakness of the Dutch position on the West Coast in this period; the Dutch had no force capable of compelling Toekoe Oemar to release the prisoners, and were obliged to pay the ransom he demanded.

## The Division with Kemala

[[Kemala|Kemala]] received its share of the ransom. The division of the ransom between Toekoe Oemar and Kemala — the hinterland of [[Pedir|Pedir]] to which the irreconcilable principal chiefs of the Atjehese resistance had withdrawn after the [[Conquest of the 22 Moekims (March-June 1879)|conquest of the 22 Moekims]] and [[Conquest of the 26 Moekims (1-4 July 1879)|26 Moekims]] of [[1879]] — indicates the continuing connection between the coastal chieftains and the inland resistance leadership, and the financial interdependence that sustained the Atjehese war effort.

## The Strategic Context

The Hok Canton affair was one of a series of acts of Atjehese aggression against foreign shipping in the period of the [[Concentratie-stelsel (1884-1896)|concentration system]] of [[Demmeni|Demmeni]] (1884–1886). Many other acts of piracy occurred elsewhere against English subjects and their property, which provoked claims from the English ([[Straits Settlements|Straits]]) government. Attacks on the islands, on the [[Willemstoren|Willemstoren]] at [[Poeloe Bras|Poeloe Bras]], occurred. Even the [[Cedar Passage|Cedar Passage]], the usual sea route to the West Coast, was officially declared unsafe. Ships passing there were repeatedly fired on by the enemy from [[Koningspunt|Koningspunt]].

[[Tengkoe di Tirou|Tengkoe di Tirou]], the soul of the Atjehese resistance, would later urge the strategy of committing acts of piracy *à la* Hok Canton to bring the Dutch into difficulties with England — a strategy that, by internationalising the conflict, might have brought British pressure to bear on the Dutch comparable to that which had followed the [[Nisero affair|Nisero affair]] of [[November 1883|November]] [[1883]].

## Significance

The Hok Canton affair is one of the principal Atjehese acts of piracy against foreign shipping of the middle phase of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. The capture, the ransom, and the division with Kemala together demonstrate the mastery of the West Coast by Toekoe Oemar, the financial interdependence of the coastal chieftains and the inland resistance, and the Dutch impotence on the West Coast in this period. The affair is, with the [[Nisero affair|Nisero affair]] of [[November 1883|November]] [[1883]], one of the two principal incidents in which Atjehese acts against foreign shipping drew foreign (English) interference into the Dutch war effort, and the precedent for the strategy of internationalising the conflict that Tengkoe di Tirou would urge on the Atjehese resistance.
