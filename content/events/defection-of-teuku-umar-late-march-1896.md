**The Defection of Teuku Umar (late March 1896)** — known in the source as the *afval van Toekoe Oemar* — was the abandonment of the [[Dutch]] colonial cause by [[Teuku Umar]] — *Toekoe Djohan Pahalawan*, [[Panglima Prang Besar]] of the Government, territorial chief of [[Lepong]] — on approximately [[26 March]]–[[29 March]] [[1896]], the central event of the first semester of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] and the immediate trigger of the [[Dutch]] military collapse that culminated in the wholesale evacuation of the [[buitenlinie]] and the dispatch of [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]] as [[Regeeringscommissaris]]. Umar turned his Dutch-supplied arms against his patrons, wrote [[First Letter of Teuku Umar|three]] [[Second Letter of Teuku Umar|letters]] [[Third Letter of Teuku Umar|(of 30 March, 12 April, and 23 April 1896)]] to the [[Governor of Atjeh]] explaining his defection and demanding ƒ150,000 per month and a Royal decoration ("kruis"), and was formally dismissed from his two posts by the [[Government Resolution of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11]] (No. 159). As described in [[A. Kruisheer|Kruisheer]]'s *ATJEH '96*, the defection "was, as it were, set on loose screws" — set, as it were, on loose screws — everything that had been gained since [[1893]].

## Background

Umar had been the keystone of [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. Deykerhoff]]'s policy of native allied warfare since his [[Subjection of Teuku Umar (30 September 1893)|subjection]] of [[30 September]] [[1893]]. His [[Legioen van T. Djohan|legioen]] of 250 paid warriors, armed and provisioned by the Government, had stormed [[Ana' Galong]] in [[November]] [[1893]] and had been the principal Dutch instrument in the western mukims through [[1894]] and [[1895]]. By the spring of [[1896]] Umar had been further entrusted with the leadership of the [[XXVI Moekims]] and had been given, on loan (*bruikleen*), additional weapons and ammunition for a specific operation against the recalcitrant [[Lamkrak]] mukims.

The signs of impending defection were multiple. Umar failed to return the weapons and ammunition that had been lent to him for the Lamkrak operation. He constructed *verschanzingen* and field-works and fortifications opposite the Dutch post at [[Lam-Djamoe]] — works whose only plausible purpose was to threaten the post. Several of his followers went over to the resistance. [[Controleur 2e kl. bij het binnenlandsch bestuur Gisolf|Controleur Gisolf]] formally warned the [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Governor]] of Umar's "more than suspicious attitude," and advised that the *Panglima Prang besar* be arrested. Deykerhoff, however, "in any case little inclined to give credence to the various reports" (in any case little inclined to give credence to the various reports), refused. Umar had, in Kruisheer's words, "the audacity" (the brass) to attend a conference at the Governor's office on [[28 March]] [[1896]], where his affability so convinced Deykerhoff of his good faith that the Governor did not even consider the advice of his subordinates to arrest him.

## The Defection

Within hours of the conference of [[28 March]] [[1896]], the defection was open. The [[Siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe (28–29 March 1896)|siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe]] began on the night of [[28 March]]–[[29 March]] [[1896]]. The posts of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]] were simultaneously invested and cut off. Hostile bands, armed in part with Dutch-supplied Beaumont and repeater rifles, swarmed over the western *buitenlinie*. By [[30 March]] [[1896]] Umar was in open correspondence with the Dutch, and the colonial administration was beginning to grasp the scale of the disaster. On [[30 March]] [[1896]] — the very day after the fall of Aloer Oe — Umar wrote his [[First Letter of Teuku Umar|first letter]] to the [[Governor of Atjeh]], setting out his grievances and his demands.

The grievances alleged in the three letters (of [[30 March]], [[12 April]], and [[23 April]] [[1896]]) were, in essence, that the *Controleur te Oleh-leh* and the *hoofddjaksa* [[Mohamad Arief]] had insulted him. The demands were altogether more substantial: Umar required a Royal decoration — "kruis" (a cross, i.e., a Dutch royal order of chivalry) — and a monthly subsidy of ƒ150,000 to maintain his warriors. The figure was astronomical — many times the entire annual budget of the Atjeh government — and was understood by all parties as a deliberate insult, the equivalent of a declaration of war. Umar's letters are preserved, in Dutch translation, in the Bijlagen of *ATJEH '96*.

## The Three Letters and the Government Response

The three letters were answered, in effect, by the [[Government Resolution of 26 April 1896 No. 159/11]] — Besluit No. 159 of the acting [[Civiel en Militair Gouverneur]] — which dismissed Umar from both his posts: as [[Panglima Prang Besar]] of the Government and as *hoeloebalang* of [[Lepong]]. The same resolution stipulated that he would henceforth be officially designated by his original name, *Toekoe Oemar* — a symbolic stripping of the honours conferred by the [[Government Resolution of 5 September 1893]]. The dismissal was authorised by Government Telegram of [[26 April]] [[1896]], No. 409. On [[27 April]] [[1896]] [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Lt.-Gen. Vetter]], by now arrived as [[Regeeringscommissaris]], issued a [[Proclamation of Vetter (27 April 1896)|proclamation]] announcing that military operations would be undertaken against the former ally.

## The Military Consequences

The immediate military consequences of the defection were the [[Siege of blokhuis Aloer Oe (28–29 March 1896)|siege of Aloer Oe]] and the concurrent sieges of [[Biloel]] and [[Lamkoenjit]], the [[Relief of Biloel and Lamkoenjit (8 April 1896)|relief operation of 8 April 1896]] under [[Kolonel der Infanterie J. W. Stemfoort|Kol. Stemfoort]], and the [[Evacuation of Biloel and Lamkoenjit (12 April 1896)|evacuation of Biloel and Lamkoenjit]] on [[12 April]] [[1896]]. The entire western *buitenlinie* was [[Evacuation of Anak-Galoeng, Lam-Barih, Lam-Soet, Senelop (17 April 1896)|evacuated by 17 April]], and the [[Evacuation of Kroeng-Gloempang, Tjot-Rang, Toengkoeb (20 April 1896)|remainder of the buitenlinie]] by [[20 April]]. [[Luitenant-Generaal J. A. Vetter|Vetter]] arrived as [[Regeeringscommissaris]] on [[7 April]] [[1896]] aboard the steamer or *Bantam*; [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Deykerhoff]] was honorably relieved of his functions on the same day. A wholesale Dutch counter-offensive followed, beginning with the [[Tuchtiging van Lam Goet (14 April 1896)|tuchtiging of Lam Goet]] on [[14 April]] and culminating in the [[Three-day Dutch offensive of 23–25 May 1896|three-day offensive of 23–25 May 1896]] against Umar's stelling at [[Lam-Pisang]] and the [[Capture of Lam-Pisang and Oemar's House (24 May 1896)|capture and destruction of his house]] on [[24 May]] [[1896]].

## Significance

The defection of Teuku Umar was the pivotal event of the first semester of [[1896]] and one of the great crises of the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]]. It transformed what had been a counter-insurgency campaign fought largely with native auxiliaries into a conventional war against a unified Achehnese enemy armed with Dutch weapons and trained in Dutch methods. The strategic edifice constructed by [[Generaal-Majoor C. Deykerhoff|Gen.-Maj. Deykerhoff]] between [[1893]] and [[1896]] — the [[Legioen van T. Djohan]], the *buitenlinie*, the network of allied *hoeloebalang* — collapsed in less than a month. The political consequences reached [[Batavia]] and [[The Hague]]: Deykerhoff was dismissed; Vetter, the [[Luitenant-Generaal|Lieutenant-General]] and [[Commandant van het Leger|Army Commander]], was dispatched with full powers as [[Regeeringscommissaris]]; and the entire Dutch strategy in Atjeh was overhauled. The defection also marked the emergence of [[Tjoet Nja Din]] — Umar's wife, who would herself become a legendary resistance leader — as a figure of central importance in the war.
