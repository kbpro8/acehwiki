The **Treaty of Atjeh (1607)** was a military alliance and commercial agreement concluded between the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC)]] and the Sultan of [[Atjeh]], represented by Vice-Admiral [[Olivier de Vivere]], on [[Driekoningendag]] ([[6 January]]) [[1607]], at the conclusion of the expedition led by Cornelis Matelieff. Negotiated by the Dutch vice-admiral, the treaty sought to renew the earlier [[Treaty of Friendship and Trade with Aceh (1600)|1600 treaty]], secure a permanent Dutch foothold in [[Sumatra]], and formally exclude rival European nations — above all the [[Portuguese]] — from Acehnese ports.

## Context and negotiations

In [[1606]], a massive [[Portuguese]] armada under the Viceroy of India, Martin Affonso de Castro, had attacked Atjeh while en route to relieve the siege of [[Malaka]]. The Acehnese, aided by Dutch factors residing in the capital, successfully repelled the assault, capturing five Portuguese fusts (light galleys). The Dutch military support significantly elevated their prestige at the Acehnese court, demonstrating to the Sultan both the military value of a Dutch alliance and the vulnerability of his own position.

Capitalizing on this goodwill, vice-admiral Olivier de Vivere — dispatched to Atjeh as part of Cornelis Matelieff's expedition to consolidate the Dutch position in the region and secure the crucial trade routes to the [[Koromandel]] coast and [[Guzeratte]] — arrived in Atjeh on [[Driekoningendag]] (Epiphany) [[1607]] with three ships. His primary objective was to renew the alliance to safeguard those routes, which relied on Acehnese goodwill and safe harbor.

## Provisions of the treaty

The treaty, signed in [[1607]], contained several highly favorable provisions for the VOC, going further than the [[Treaty of Friendship and Trade with Aceh (1600)|1600 treaty]] in several key areas:
1. **Permanent Settlement:** The Dutch were granted the right to establish a *"versekerde ende vaste plaetse"* (secure and fixed place) — a fortified lodge or factory — in Atjeh, protecting their interests from both local and European threats.
2. **Colonization:** The VOC was permitted to bring artisans and households (*"ambaghtluijden ende huijsgezinnen"*) from the Dutch Republic to settle in the enclave, facilitating a permanent Dutch community.
3. **Exclusivity of Trade:** The Sultan decreed that no other Europeans, nor their descendants, would be allowed to trade in his domains. Any foreign vessel arriving without a formal pass from the Dutch "King" (the Prince of Orange or the VOC authorities) would be treated as an enemy and attacked.
4. **Renewal of Past Treaties:** The new treaty renewed the 1600 trade agreement and established a formal military alliance, drawing the two powers closer together.

## Significance and collapse

While the treaty represented the high point of the Dutch-Acehnese alliance and a theoretical triumph for the VOC's monopolistic ambitions in western [[Sumatra]] — giving the VOC a secure foothold in the vital pepper market and a strategic ally against the common Portuguese enemy — its practical application was short-lived. The Sultan who had signed the agreement died in late [[1607]]. His successor, pursuing a policy of aggressive regional expansion and wary of granting any single European power too much influence, refused to renew the exclusivity clauses. Consequently, the Dutch were forced to rely on standard commercial diplomacy rather than treaty-enforced monopolies in Atjeh for the next three decades, until the rise of Sultan [[Iskandar Muda]].
