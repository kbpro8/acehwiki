The **pula** or **seuneulong** system is the Achehnese method of rice cultivation by transplanting, in contrast to the direct-sowing method known as *tabu*. It is one of the two principal systems of padi-growing described by C. Snouck Hurgronje in *The Achehnese*, employed in a portion of the [[Acheh|Achehnese]] highlands, occasionally in the lowlands (notably the [[IV Mukims]]), and with certain modifications in swampy districts such as the [[VII Mukims Bueng]].

The system consists of two distinct operations. The first is the preparation of a nursery bed (*libuë*), in which the seed (*bijih*) is strewn (*tabu*) to produce seedlings known as *padé seuneulong*. The padi obtained in this way is called *padé peunula* or "planted padi," in opposition to the *padé teunabu* ("scattered padi") or *padé due* ("sitting padi") of the direct-sowing method. The nursery is sown in the same manner as a field under the tabu system, but the seedlings are subsequently lifted and transplanted to the *umòng* or rice-field proper.

The timing of the transplantation is governed by both practical and ritual considerations. The cultivators endeavour, if possible, to plant out on the **44th day** after sowing the nursery — a number whose ritual significance recurs throughout Achehnese custom, as in the "removal of the oven" (*beulëh dapu*) 44 days after childbirth and the [[Pula batee|setting up of the tombstone]] 44 days after death. Before planting begins, the superstitious usage of the *peusijië* or "cooling" is observed: leafy fronds of the betel-nut palm (*pinéung*) together with the plants called *manè-manòë* and *sisijië* are tied together, soaked in flour and water (*teupong taweue*), and used to besprinkle the centre of the rice-field. The green besom is then planted in the middle of the *umòng*, a rite called *peuphon padé*, the "inauguration of the padi." Planting proceeds outward from the centre, after the indispensable [[Beseumelah|beseumelah]] ("In the name of Allah") has been uttered, and follows the direction of the wind so that the young plants may acquire the slant regarded as a guarantee of straight, strong growth.

The pula-season falls in **keunongs 5 and 3**, or sometimes, when rain is unusually late, in keunong 1. In swampy ground the tabu-system cannot be applied at all; the seed would rot in the water, so a nursery must be made on drier ground nearby. Where time presses, the system is modified by wetting the seed and spreading it out to sprout before sowing — a compromise that allows the cultivator to catch up with the season.

A surviving custom whose original meaning has been wholly forgotten is that of the *inòng padé*: when planting out is completed, a handful of leftover seedlings is planted in a clump in the *umòng*. The word *inòng* in modern Achehnese means "woman" or "female," but folk-lore indicates it once also carried the sense of "mother." In herds of buffaloes and flocks of goats or poultry there is usually one tamer animal called the *inòng*, which acts as leader and is never sold or slaughtered, since to do so would bring ill-luck on the rest. By analogy the *inòng padé* was probably a "rice-mother," intended to ensure the prosperous growth of the crop — a meaning now faded from the popular mind, but paralleled in the *indung pare* or "rice-mother" of the Sundanese.
