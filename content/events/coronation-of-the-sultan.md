The **coronation of the Sultan** of Acheh was the solemn installation by which a newly chosen sultan was wedded to his country. As [[C. Snouck Hurgronje]] describes it, the ceremony "was and remained a contract with the three panglimas" of the *sagis* or main divisions of the kingdom, "though the choice of a sultan was governed to a great extent by entirely different influences." The sultan's own proper uleebalangs "played more or less the part of masters of ceremonies, while the kalis and ulamas gave as it were their blessing to the marriage of the raja with his country." The most characteristic features of the ceremony, as related to Hurgronje by eye-witnesses of the last sultan's coronation, took place on a square platform of stones somewhat roughly piled together, called the *branda seumahla* or *prana seumahla*, which stood within a low enclosing wall near the royal abode and the *bale ròïh* in which the sultan received his guests.

The platform "seems only to have been used for coronations and was suitably decorated on these occasions; at ordinary times it was neglected, so that anyone passing through the [[Dalam]] or [[Kuta Raja]] would hardly notice it." A debate lasting some weeks was first held by the three panglima sagi and other influential chiefs — in consultation, so far as they thought necessary, with the kalis and ulamas — to determine the most suitable candidate for the vacant throne. "When their final decision had been sealed by the payment of the 'wedding' presents to the three panglimas, a favourable day was fixed for the ceremony." During the forenoon of the day, the court dignitaries whose offices had not shared the universal downfall were at their posts — especially the [[Panglima Meuseugit Raya|Panglima Meuseugit Raya]], whose duty it was to keep order in the Dalam, and the [[Teuku Kali Malikon Ade]], who performed his functions within the space surrounding the *branda seumahla*, with his blunderbuss hanging from his shoulder and a white cocoanut spathe wound in his head-cloth.

The new sultan took his seat on the platform, while the three head kalis of the sagis and some of the principal ulamas ranged themselves behind him, and a huge crowd watched from a little distance. The first to approach were the panglima of the [[XXVI Mukims]] and [[Teuku Ne']], both of whom, accompanied by their attendants, advanced close to the wall within which the platform stood. One of the kalis, [[Teungku Lam Paya]] of the [[XXV Mukims|XXV Mukims]], thereupon recited a form of nomination introduced by the words from the Quran (IV, 62): "Obey Allah and his Messenger and those among you who be clad with authority." This utterance was repeated in a loud tone by the Teuku Kali so that all the people might hear. After the conclusion of the form of nomination, the Teuku Kali called the panglima of the XXVI Mukims thrice in succession by his official title, to which the latter and his followers replied each time with a loud "*deelat!*" — almost equivalent to "Yes, Your Majesty!" The panglima then drew aside to make room for his fellow-official of the XXV Mukims; with him, and after him with the panglima of the [[XXII Mukims]], the above ceremony was repeated. The official order of precedence of the three sagis on ceremonial occasions was thus XXVI, XXV, XXII — an order "always that here given," although "judging by their relative importance the order of precedence should be reversed."

The coronation thus differed from a European consecration in being, in form, a contract rather than an unction. The sultan was not so much anointed by the clergy as installed by his uleebalangs, with the religious officers acting as witnesses and blessers. Yet the ceremony did confer a kind of sacrality on its recipient. As Hurgronje notes, "the Dalam (usually called Kraton by the Dutch) and all that it contained, including the sultan himself, were since the earliest times the objects of a somewhat extraordinary reverence in the minds of the Achehnese, though this never prevented them from making a football of the sultanate at their pleasure." This feeling was "founded partly on the fact that the Dalam was the centre and apparently the origin of the glory of a past almost fabulous in comparison with later times, and partly on the kindred fact that the Achehnese (though wrongly as we have seen) ascribe all the adats of the country to the earlier sultans." The coronation was thus at once a political compact among the chiefs of the sagis and a quasi-sacramental act which imparted to the new sultan something of the *kramat* — the miraculous favour of God — by which he was thereafter regarded.
