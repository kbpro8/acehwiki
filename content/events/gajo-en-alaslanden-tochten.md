**The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions** were among the great successes of the glorious period of Van Heutsz in the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Aceh War]], the fifth-named of the expeditions and excursions that [[Musket]] lists in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* by Merac ([[1947]]). The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions were the expeditions to the [[Gajolanden|Gajoland]] and the Alaslands — the highlands of central [[Atjeh|Aceh]] — conducted under [[Joannes Benedictus van Heutsz|Van Heutsz]] as part of the all-out offensive.

## The great successes of the glorious period

"the time also of our great successes! The Pidië expedition, the expeditions in Idi and Peusangan, the Pasé expedition, the Samalanga expedition, the expeditions to the Gayo and Alaslands, the action in the Tangsé and Geumpang area and on the North Coast of Atjeh, the patrol actions on the always restless West Coast, and finally the battle of Batèë-Ilië, by which the organised, general resistance in Atjeh was broken for good." The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions are the fifth-named of the great successes.

## The Gajolanden and the Gajoweg

The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions — the expeditions to Gajoland and the Alaslands — are the book's — and the colonial memory's — marker of the colonial army's all-out offensive in the highlands of central Atjeh. The [[Gajoweg]] — the military road from [[Bireuën]] to [[Takengeun]] — was the infrastructure that the expeditions had established: the road built by [Heinzenknecht](/people/heinzenknecht) under Van Heutsz to open Gajoland to the colonial army.

## Significance

The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions are significant in *[[Oost-Indisch Journael]]* as among the great successes of the glorious period of Van Heutsz. The book's argument — that the great successes of the glorious period were the expeditions and excursions by which the organised, general resistance in Atjeh was broken for good — is the book's frame for the offensive phase of the [[Atjeh-oorlog|Aceh War]]. The Gayo and Alas Lands Expeditions are therefore the book's — and the colonial memory's — marker of the all-out offensive in the highlands of central Atjeh.
