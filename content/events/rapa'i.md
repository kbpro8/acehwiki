The **Rapa'i** (also spelled *rapa'i*) is an Achehnese musical and religious performance, named after the frame-drum (*rapa'i*) on which it is performed and from the mystic teacher [[Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī|Ahmad ar-Rifāʿī]] whose name is invoked at every representation. The sacred name of Rifa'i appears in the curious proceedings which in Acheh are included under the terms *dikr* and *ratib* — the equivalents of the Arabic *dhikr* and *rātib* — in the *sadati*-pantons, and also at the performances called Rapa'i. At these last, Ahmad Rifa'i, that master of mysticism from whom they derive their name, is always invoked by the performers.

The Rapa'i is one of the vows (*kaoy*) most commonly promised by parents at the sickbed of a child, in the hope of furthering his recovery. It is a common custom for the father or mother to vow, when a child has not yet been "brought down" (that is, has not yet had the first hair-clipping ceremony), that in the event of recovery the Rapa'i shall be performed at the ceremonies attending that operation. Such vows are regarded as of a religious nature, alongside other religious vows such as to take the recovered child to seven mosques, to present a goat bought at the fair before the fasting month (*ma meugang*) to the teungku of the village, or to bathe the child with water that has washed the feet of His Majesty the Sultan. Where a parent in desperation promises an act which has never before formed the subject of a vow, and the wished-for recovery supervenes, the means that has proved so successful is resorted to by many others, and one more is added to the list of vows.

The Rapa'i representation also forms part of the more solemn kanduris of the life-cycle. Those whose means allow of it give a Rapa'i at the kanduri that follows the birth of a child, when a number of women are invited to attend the ceremony (though they take no share in the kanduri), and the midwife removes the *dapu* or oven and pushes it beneath the house where the platform on which the mother has rested during her confinement is broken up. The feast that accompanies the first hair-clipping of a child is also frequently embellished by a Rapa'i representation, when the givers are persons of means; after the kanduri, some women, including the midwife, who is always invited on such occasions, fetch the child from the house for the operation.

Snouck Hurgronje classifies the vows of which the Rapa'i is the most important as either religious or superstitious, according to the rites that accompany them. The religious vows, including the Rapa'i, are distinguished from those classified as *puja* — efficacious against evil spirits — such as the vow to go and beg in seven shops, which belongs to the realm of superstitious practice rather than of religion. The Rapa'i thus stands on the boundary between the mystical devotion of the *dikr* and the popular vow, and shares with the [[Rateb Saman|rateb Saman]] and the kanduri the character of an Achehnese institution in which orthodox Islam, popular mysticism, and the domestic rites of the family life shade into one another.
