The **Acehnese conquest of Djohor** in [[1613]] was a major punitive military campaign launched by the Sultan of [[Atjeh]] against the Malay sultanate of [[Djohor]]. The invasion was triggered by Djohor's decision to sign a peace treaty with the [[Portuguese]] in [[1610]], an act that Atjeh viewed as a betrayal of the broader Islamic and anti-Portuguese coalition in the Strait of Malacca.

## Background: the Djohor-Portuguese peace

For decades, [[Djohor]] had been a steadfast ally of the Dutch and a persistent thorn in the side of [[Portuguese Malacca]], frequently blockading the port and disrupting its food supplies. However, exhausted by continuous warfare and the lack of consistent Dutch naval protection, the Sultan of Djohor was forced to sue for peace. In [[October]] [[1610]], a formal treaty was signed between Djohor and Malaka, reopening the waterways to Portuguese commerce and severely damaging the Dutch strategic position in the region.

## The invasion of 1613

Enraged by Djohor's capitulation to the "old enemy," the Sultan of Atjeh assembled a massive armada. In [[May]] [[1613]], the Acehnese fleet appeared on the river leading to Djohor's capital, Batu Sawar.

At the time, the Dutch ship *Hope* was anchored at Djohor to relieve the local VOC factor and replenish capital. The Acehnese demanded that the Dutch remain neutral and withdraw their support. The Dutch, honoring their defensive treaties with Djohor, refused and landed 31 men to assist the Malay defenders, alongside the six men stationed at the Dutch lodge.

## The fall of the capital

Despite fierce resistance from the combined Malay and Dutch forces, the overwhelming numbers of the Acehnese army proved decisive. On [[6 June]] [[1613]], the capital fell. Two Dutchmen were killed in the fighting, and twenty-two others, alongside the Sultan of Djohor (Sultan Sabrang), his family, and numerous nobles, were taken prisoner and transported back to [[Atjeh]]. The city was largely destroyed.

## Aftermath and reconciliation

The prisoners were held in Atjeh under the collateral of the VOC's confiscated goods. However, the geopolitical landscape shifted rapidly. The Sultan of Atjeh, seeking to consolidate his hegemony over the Malay Peninsula rather than maintain a costly occupation, reconciled with Djohor. To seal the new alliance, the Acehnese Sultan gave his sister in marriage to Sultan Sabrang.

In [[1614]], the Sultan of Djohor was escorted back to his ruined capital by 2,000 Acehnese soldiers to rebuild the state, and the surviving Dutch prisoners were released, allowing the VOC to cautiously resume its pepper and tin trade in the region.
