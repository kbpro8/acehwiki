**The plundering of two British merchant vessels at [[Kuala Bathee|Koeala Batèë]] and [[Kuala Meureudu|Koeala Meureudoe]] in [[1844]]** — in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s history — was one of the principal Atjèhnese maritime outrages of the pre-war period, answered not by the Dutch (who were bound by the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty]] to ensure the safety of Atjèhnese waters) but by the British themselves, who — like the [[United States of America|Americans]] in [[1833]] — "zichzelf voldoening verschaffen door de beide plaatsen met hun eigen oorlogsschepen te tuchtigen" — "procured themselves satisfaction by chastising both places with their own warships."

## The incident

Kreemer records the incident in a single clause: "In [[1844]] werden twee Britsche handelsvaartuigen geplunderd te Koeala Batèë en te Koeala Meureudoe; ook toen moesten de Engelschen — evenals de Amerikanen in [[1833]] — zichzelf voldoening verschaffen door de beide plaatsen met hun eigen oorlogsschepen te tuchtigen" — "In 1844 two British merchant vessels were plundered at Kuala Batëë and at Kuala Meureudu; then too the English — like the Americans in 1833 — had to procure themselves satisfaction by chastising both places with their own warships."

## The pattern of foreign self-help

The 1844 incident is, in Kreemer's narrative, the second of two cases (the first being the [[1831]]–[[1833]] American case) in which a foreign power, denied Dutch protection of its shipping in Atjèhnese waters, undertook its own punitive expedition against Atjèhnese coastal towns. The pattern revealed the hollowness of the Dutch claim — made in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 1824]] — to be the guarantor of safety in Atjèhnese waters. The recurrence of plundering — [[1851]] saw a [[Naples|Napolitaansch]] vessel plundered, [[1852]] an [[England|Engelsche]] schooner — and the Dutch government's admission in [[1852]] "dat zij dergelijke daden der Atjèhers niet beletten kon" — "that she could not prevent such deeds of the Atjèhnese" — completed the exposure of the 1824 framework.

## Significance

The 1844 plunder is, in Kreemer's account of the causes of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]], a key moment in the erosion of the 1824 treaty system. The willingness of the British to undertake their own punitive action — bypassing the Dutch — demonstrated that the international guarantee of Atjèhnese integrity was already fraying, and that the Dutch monopoly on legitimate military action against Atjèh was not matched by a Dutch capacity to police Atjèhnese waters. This disjunction between obligation and capacity is the structural flaw that Kreemer identifies as the "kiem" — the seed — of the Atjèh war, already present in the 1824 treaty itself.
