**Fullerton's letter to the Sultan of Siak of [[7 February 1828|7 February 1828]]** is the despatch by which Governor [[Robert Fullerton|Robert Fullerton]] of [[Penang|Penang]], writing from [[Singapore|Singapore]], first opened the formal correspondence with the [[Sultan of Siak|Sultan of Siak]] that was to expand into the "broad Siak-correspondence" of 1828. The letter is printed in Appendix 11 of [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]]'s study, together with the [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular of 21 May 1828]] that grew out of it.

## Background

By early [[1828]] news of the projected [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] of Commissioner-General [[Du Bus|Du Bus]] had reached [[Penang|Penang]] through the channel of [[John Prince|John Prince]], the former resident of Singapore, who had learned of it at [[Batavia|Batavia]] in November [[1827]]. The Dutch project contemplated sending the frigate *Bellona* to the East Coast to divert the salt trade from Singapore to [[Riouw|Riouw]] and to draw the native chiefs into political relations with the Netherlands. Fullerton resolved to anticipate the Dutch move by addressing the chief of [[Siak|Siak]], the most considerable of the East Coast states, in a letter intended to confirm him in his friendly relations with the British settlements.

## Contents

The letter, dated Singapore, [[7 February 1828|7 February 1828]], opens with the information that Malacca had been "permanently transferred to the British Government" and that Fullerton had arrived at Singapore to inquire into the general state of trade at the two settlements. Fullerton expressed satisfaction at finding that the trade between Siak and [[Prince of Wales Island|Prince of Wales Island]], [[Malacca|Malacca]] and [[Singapore|Singapore]] was much increasing to the mutual advantage of all these countries. The letter was written, he declared, from the continued desire to keep up amicable relations with Siak and all the states on the East Coast of [[Sumatra|Sumatra]], and to extend the trade and commercial communication between them and the several British settlements in the [[Straits of Malacca|Straits of Malacca]]. Fullerton offered to pay attention to any suggestions the Sultan might have for the improvement of commercial intercourse, and enclosed a few small presents — a piece of scarlet silk, a piece of European chintz, a silk umbrella and a piece of Bengal silk handkerchiefs — as a token of friendship.

## The Reply

A similar letter was written at the same time by the translator [[John Anderson|John Anderson]], by order of the Penang government, to the *pangeran* of Siak. The Sultan replied, acknowledging receipt, with a letter dated [[21 Dulkaida|21 Dulkaida]] — [[4 June 1828|4 June 1828]]. He expressed gratification at hearing that the three countries of Pulo Penang, Singapore and Malacca had been finally secured under the government of the English Company, and protested that he was "ten times as anxious" as his correspondent to preserve the friendly relations between Siak and the British settlements, adding that he had lately succeeded his younger brother, who had been afflicted with sickness and had lost his understanding. The presents he had duly received, with much satisfaction; he had nothing to offer in return but many compliments.

## Significance

The letter of [[7 February 1828|7 February 1828]] is the opening move in the diplomatic campaign of which the [[Fullerton's circular to nine Siak chiefs|circular of 21 May 1828]] was the second. Together they constituted, in Van der Kemp's reading, the British counter-stroke to the Dutch [[Bellona mission to Sumatra's East Coast (1827-1828)|Bellona mission]] and the first formal British intervention in the affairs of the Sumatran states since the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Treaty of 1824]]. The "broad Siak-correspondence" of 1828 marked, in Van der Kemp's view, the moment at which the Penang government began systematically to contest the Dutch claim to exclusive political relations with the chiefs of Sumatra.
