The **Dutch–Aceh Treaty of [[1641]]** was an agreement signed in February of that year between [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Tajul-alam]], the newly acceded "Sultane of Aceh" (daughter of [[Sultan Iskandar Muda|Iskander Moeda]]), and the [[Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) envoy [[Joost Schouten]], shortly after her accession. The treaty granted the VOC significant trading concessions on the West Coast of Sumatra and excluded other European nations from the pepper ports.

## Provisions

The treaty specifically addressed the pepper trade, which was of paramount importance to both parties. The sultana:
- Granted the VOC concessions on tolls in the pepper ports of [[Tikoe]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]], and [[Indrapoera]].
- Promised not to allow any other European traders to operate in those ports.
- Recognized the VOC's right to trade in Acehnese territory under favourable conditions.

## Context

The treaty was signed shortly after Tajul-alam's accession and was a diplomatic victory for the VOC, which had been seeking better access to the West Coast pepper supplies. The Company had recently conquered [[Malakka]] (1641), which gave it a stronger position in the region, and the new sultana may have been eager to secure Dutch friendship against the Portuguese and other rivals.

## Consequences

The treaty laid the foundation for a more stable trading relationship, though tensions continued. It was followed by further agreements in [[1649]] and [[1650]] that expanded VOC privileges — see [[Dutch–Aceh Treaty of 1649]] and [[Treaty of 1650 on Perak Tin]]. The 1641 treaty is notable for being the first major commercial accord between the VOC and the new Acehnese regime.

## Account from Stapel's *De Koloniën* (1931)

**The Treaty of Atjeh-VOC of 15 February 1641** was the agreement concluded in the first month of the reign of the [[Sultana of Atjeh (Taj ul-Alam)|new sultane of Atjeh]], daughter of [[Iskandar Muda of Atjeh|Iskandar Muda]], with the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Compagnie]]'s envoy [[Joost Schouten|Joost Schouten]]. It is recorded in Dr. F. W. Stapel's *Geschiedkundige Atlas van Nederland* as the foundational instrument by which the Compagnie obtained its first systematic commercial foothold on the [[Westkust van Sumatra|West Coast of Sumatra]]: toll concessions in the pepper ports of [[Tikoe]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]] and [[Indrapoera]], and the exclusion of all other European traders from those ports.

## Context

The sultane came to the throne just as the long contest for [[Malakka]] reached its climax. [[Malakka|Portuguese Malakka]] capitulated to the Compagnie on [[14 January]] [[1641]] after a five-month siege. The accession of a new sultane in Atjeh a few weeks later produced a brief but real opportunity for the Compagnie to regularise its position on the Westkust, where Acehnese sovereignty had hitherto blocked direct Dutch trade.

Stapel stresses the timing: "In 1641 a sultane came to power in Atjeh, daughter of Iskandar Muda; she concluded, still in the first month of her reign — February — an agreement with the Compagnie's envoy Joost Schouten" (*In 1641 kwam in Atjeh een sultane aan het bewind, dochter van Iskander Moeda; zij sloot, nog in de eerste maand van haar regeering — Februari — een overeenkomst met den gezant der Compagnie, Joost Schouten*).

## Terms

The agreement had two main elements. The sultane "conceded to the Dutchmen the tolls in the pepper ports Tikoe, Priaman, Padang and Indrapoera" (*zij de Nederlanders concessies deed op het gebied der tollen in de peperhavens Tikoe, Priaman, Padang en Indrapoera*). And she "promised to admit no other European traders there" (*en beloofde, daar geen andere Europeesche handelaars toe te laten*).

The careful restriction of the exclusion to *European* traders left the Atjehers' own rights on the Westkust intact — a pattern that would recur in every subsequent Acehnese-Dutch treaty, most notably the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (November 1649)|Truytman treaties]] of 1649 and the [[Treaty of Atjeh-Perak (15 August 1650)|Perak treaty]] of 1650.

## Significance

The Schouten treaty laid the legal foundation for the next two decades of Dutch commercial policy on the Westkust. It was the basis on which the Compagnie established its factories at [[Tikoe]], [[Priaman]], [[Padang]] and [[Indrapoera]], and on which it subsequently pressed its claims against Atjehsche governors there for levying tolls on Menangkabau pepper sold to the Dutch. When those claims produced only frustration, the Compagnie moved to the [[Treaty of Atjeh-VOC (November 1649)|Truytman treaties]] of 1649, which extended the Schouten concessions to the whole Westkust and excluded all native nations *except* Atjehers. The progression from Schouten to Truytman is the progression from co-operation to encirclement.
