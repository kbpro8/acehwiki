The **capture of a British brig by Tunku Pakih** in early [[1818]] was a maritime incident at [[Acheh]] that helped shape the conditions under which the [[Captain Coombs' first Acheh mission (1817-1818)|first Acheh mission of Captain Coombs]] was conducted. A few days before Coombs arrived at the Acheh roads, [[Tunku Pakih]] of [[Pedir]] — known in the Dutch text as *toekoe Packie* — overhauled a brig sailing under English colours.

The incident was raised by [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] with the Achehnese chiefs upon his arrival. According to Van der Kemp, the chiefs expressed indignation at the scandal and undertook to call Tunku Pakih to account. Their promise appears to have been purely formal, since no action followed; the chiefs' ready assurances were a typical feature of Achehnese diplomacy, in which appearance and substance were easily parted. The episode was nonetheless of diplomatic consequence, for it formed one of the outrages against the British flag for which [[Sultan Jauhar Alum]] was later required to make an apology before the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles–Coombs commission]] would open formal negotiations with him at Pedir in [[1819]].

The capture also exemplified the anarchic condition of the Sumatran coast in the years before the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)|Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London (1824)]]. Tunku Pakih, who appears in Van der Kemp's narrative as a zeeroover of considerable local standing, was at once a servant of Jauhar Alum's cause and an independent operator whose depredations complicated the sultan's relations with European powers. His death at the hands of the [[Shahbandar of Acheh]] in [[Death of Tunku Pakih|1819]] would clear the way for the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|Treaty of Pedir]], but the brig incident itself remained, in British memories, one of the outrages that the 1819 treaty was meant to remedy.
