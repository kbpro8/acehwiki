The **Hasan-Husain festival** is the commemoration of [[Hasan and Husain|Hasan and Husain]], the grandsons of [[Prophet Mohammad|the Prophet Mohammad]], celebrated during the first ten days of Muharram — the month that in Acheh bears the popular name **Asan-Usen** in consequence. The festival commemorates the martyrdom of Husain at [[Kerbela]] on the 10th of Muharram ([[Ashura|Ashura]]), but in its popular form has enrolled the blameless Hasan in the band of martyrs alongside his brother, so that the festival bears the names of both.

The most elaborate observance of the festival is found in [[Persia]] and the Shi'ite portions of British India. There the first ten days of Muharram are given over to dances and bonfires, *dikrs* with their mourning for the martyrs, wild frenzies in which the mourners gash their own bodies with knives, theatrical representations sometimes confounded with reality by the crowds of spectators — so that the actor who takes the part of the murderer of Husain becomes exposed to actual violence — and mad processions more reminiscent of a fair or carnival than of a funeral pageant. The absurd tales related of Husain and his companions, the martyrs of Kerbela, are also of this popular Indian character; and, Snouck Hurgronje observes, no works of this sort are to be met with in any country closer to Arabia.

In different parts of Netherlands-India, and especially on the West Coast of Sumatra ([[Padang]], [[Bencoolen]]), the Hasan-Husain festival is celebrated on a smaller scale but in much the same manner as described in Jaffur Shurreef's *Qanoon-e-Islam*. It has been thought that the festival was introduced along the sea-board by the *sipahis* (sepoys) who immigrated thither during the English domination. It is indeed possible that these natives of Hindustan may have had an influence on the manner of its observance. It is however probable, to say the least, that even previously to this a Hasan-Husain feast enjoyed much popularity both in Sumatra and elsewhere. Indeed, how else can we account for the fact that it is celebrated to this day in [[Trumon]] in the manner customary in the Deccan, that in Acheh the month is called Asan-Usen, and that the day Ashura — of which orthodox Islam takes but little notice — has in Java given its name Sura to the whole month of Muharram?

In Acheh the festival is reduced to its most modest form: the cooking of the kanji Ashura porridge, distributed by the gampong from one or two large pots, is virtually the only surviving observance. The name Asan-Usen, however, remains as evidence of the former wider reach of the festival. The contrast with the West Coast of Sumatra, where the festival retained its theatrical and processional character down to Snouck Hurgronje's time, is the more striking: the same tradition that in Padang gave rise to dances, bonfires, and dikrs produced in Acheh only a household pottage and a popular name for the month.
