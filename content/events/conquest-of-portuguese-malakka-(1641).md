The **conquest of Portuguese Malakka in 1641** was the joint [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]]–[[Acheh|Atjeh]]–[[Johor|Djohor]] capture of the city of [[Malakka]] from the Portuguese, ending 130 years of Portuguese rule over the strategic Strait port. The conquest was the immediate political background to [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh]] of the same year: in [[E. C. Godee Molsbergen|Godee Molsbergen]]'s account, "Atjeh, dat een jaar te voren met Djohor bij de Compagnie had samengewerkt, om de Portugeezen uit Malakka te jagen" — Atjeh, which the year before had cooperated with Djohor and the Company to drive the Portuguese from Malakka.

## The long Atjeh-Portuguese conflict

The conquest of [[1641]] closed more than a century of Atjeh-Portuguese conflict. Since the early sixteenth century, [[Acheh|Atjeh]] had been "de groote vijand der Portugeezen in die gewesten" — the great enemy of the Portuguese in those parts — and "een voortdurende bedreiging voor Malakka" — a constant threat to Malakka. The sultanate's naval power and its alliance with other Malay powers had repeatedly brought Portuguese Malakka to the edge of collapse; the [[Great cannon of Atjeh|great cannon of Atjeh]] was the most famous single artefact of that contest.

The Portuguese, for their part, had repeatedly tried to contain Atjeh — by naval expeditions (such as the [[Andrea Furtado de Mendoça|Andrea Furtado de Mendoça]] armada of [[1601]]), by diplomatic interventions (such as the Portuguese monk who sabotaged the [[Pigskin parchment letter incident at Atjeh (1600)|1600 pigskin-letter embassy]]), and by alliances with Atjeh's Malay rivals (especially [[Johor|Djohor]]).

## The VOC-Atjeh-Djohor alliance of 1640

By the late [[1630]]s the strategic situation had shifted. The Dutch-Portuguese truce of [[1641]] (following Portugal's liberation from Spain in [[1640]]) complicated but did not prevent the joint VOC–Atjeh–[[Johor|Djohor]] attack on Portuguese Malakka. The truce was a European diplomatic fact; in Asian waters, the contest continued. The three powers — the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|VOC]], [[Acheh|Atjeh]] and [[Johor|Djohor]] — found a common interest in driving the Portuguese from the Strait, and cooperated to that end in [[1640]]–[[1641]].

## The fall of Malakka, 1641

The joint attack succeeded: Portuguese Malakka fell in [[1641]]. The fall of the city ended 130 years of Portuguese rule (since the city's capture by [[Afonso de Albuquerque|Afonso de Albuquerque]] in [[1511]]) and opened a new era in the Strait's commercial and political geography. The Portuguese nobleman [[Francisco de Souza de Castro|Francisco de Souza de Castro]], held prisoner in Atjeh at the time of the fall, was one of the human residues of the long conflict.

## The Soury embassy and the conquest

The [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy of 1641]] was sent in the immediate aftermath of the conquest — *Commissaris* [[Pieter Soury|Soury]] travelled via Malakka to Atjeh, and the fall of the city was the political fact that framed the new Sultana's diplomacy. The "maidens as ransom" request put to Soury at his audience — see [[Maagden als losprijs]] — and the Atjeh court's interest in the fate of the Portuguese prisoner [[Francisco de Souza de Castro|De Castro]] both reflect the immediate context of the Malakka conquest: the sultanate was consolidating its gains and seeking to extract further advantage from the Portuguese defeat.

The Soury embassy's diplomatic clarification — that Portugal in [[1640]] had been freed from Spain, was no longer the Republic's mortal enemy, and that the Republic had concluded a ten-year truce with Portugal the following year — was also a clarification about the meaning of the Malakka conquest. The Dutch were now, formally, at truce with Portugal in Europe; in Asia, the truce complicated but did not undo the joint VOC–Atjeh–[[Johor|Djohor]] victory.

## Significance

For [[Acheh|Atjeh]]'s history, the conquest of Portuguese Malakka in [[1641]] is the closing episode of the long Atjeh-Portuguese conflict that had defined the sultanate's external relations since the early sixteenth century. The joint VOC–Atjeh–[[Johor|Djohor]] victory was the high-point of Atjeh's seventeenth-century partnership with the Dutch — a partnership that the [[Pieter Soury's mission to Atjeh (1641)|Soury embassy of 1641]] was sent to formalise and consolidate. The conquest also marked the beginning of the end of Atjeh's strategic centrality in the Strait: with Portuguese Malakka gone, the VOC became the dominant European power in the region, and the Atjeh sultanate's room for diplomatic manoeuvre narrowed.
