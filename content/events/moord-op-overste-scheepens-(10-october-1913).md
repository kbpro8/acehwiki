The **murder of overste [[W. B. J. A. Scheepens]]** on [[10 October]] [[1913]] — "the most deeply lamented of the many Acehnese murders," in [[Maurice Henri du Croo|du Croo]]'s words — took place during a sitting of the *[[Landschapsgerecht]]* at [[Sigli]], when the *[[oelèëbalang]]* and *landschapsrechter* [[T. Béntara Titeuë]] stabbed the presiding Civil Authority of [[Pidië]] in the belly after the *[[Moesapat]]* pronounced what he considered an insufficient sentence on the man who had wounded his son. Scheepens died on [[17 October]] [[1913]]; the *oelèëbalang* was cut down on the spot. Du Croo printed the exact course of events as related by eye-witnesses, because "it clearly illuminates how even an Acehnese territorial chief is brought to such a murder."

## The quarrel over the coconuts

In October [[1913]] the son of the *oelèëbalang* of the [[II Moekims Titeuë]] in Pidië, [[Teukoe Béntara Titeuë]], walked with a few followers through the *gampong*. He grew thirsty and ordered one of the followers to climb a coconut palm standing on the yard of an Acehnese and pick a few coconuts.

The owner of those coconut palms, annoyed because the *oelèëbalang*'s son had not asked his permission, refused to allow it. The young Teukoe in his turn grew angry, since in his view this picking was the *oelèëbalang*'s right, whereupon the owner threatened to bring his *[[parang]]* (chopping knife) down on his skull. The Teukoe held to the order once given, whereupon the owner did indeed bring the *parang* down on his skull, causing a bleeding head wound.

The perpetrator was arrested and taken to Sigli to stand trial before the *[[Moesapat]]*, the *adat* court.

## The sitting of the *Moesapat* ("customary-law court")

On the day the case was to be judged, T. Béntara Titeuë went to Sigli. At the entrance to the civil office the armed police servant on sentry duty took from the *oelèëbalang* his gold-ornamented, visibly worn *[[rentjong]]* (dagger).

The Moesapat sat under the presidency of overste Scheepens in his capacity as Civil Authority at Sigli. Scheepens asked the *adjunct-djaksa* (assistant native public prosecutor) what his demand was; he demanded the maximum penalty known to Dutch criminal law for slight wounding, three months' imprisonment.

One of the *oelèëbalangs* sitting in judgement asked the Muhammadan adviser of the court what punishment he considered fitting for the perpetrator. The Teungkoe answered according to Acehnese *[[adat]]*: *"Loeka oelón sipat, darah oelón soekat, lhò' oelón takat, diët lhëë ploh reunggèt"* — that is, "the wound I have measured, the (spilled) blood I have measured, the depth (of the wound) I have sounded; the blood price is thirty dollars."

Hearing this, T. Béntara Titeuë sprang up in fury and abused the Teungkoe as *"[[Kaphé]]"* (unbeliever). "Jij weet heel goed," cried the *oelèëbalang*, "dat ik volgens onzen *adat* ("customary law") het recht heb niet alleen den man zelf, die het bloed van mijn zoon vergoten heeft, af te maken maar zelfs diens geheele familie."

T. Béntara Titeuë grew furious again and told the President that he well understood that the President might no longer apply Acehnese *adat* to the perpetrator, but that if the man were not punished with at least banishment to [[Java]], he, T. Béntara, would be *"[[maloe]]"* (shamed) in the eyes of his people.

In vain the Civil Authority tried to explain to the *oelèëbalang* that his son, by improper conduct, was the principal culprit, and that Dutch law did not permit banishment as a punishment for inflicting a slight wound.

## The stabbing

The *oelèëbalang* persisted in his demand, and when the Moesapat pronounced the penalty of three months, T. Béntara Titeuë suddenly drew a concealed knife from beneath his clothes and stabbed overste Scheepens in the belly.

The Overste fell backwards to the ground. The *adjunct-djaksa* rushed forward, but had as his only weapon a wooden ruler. Then an armed police servant, an Acehnese, drew his *[[klewang]]* and struck T. Béntara Titeuë with it over the head. The *oelèëbalang* fell down and died almost at once.

Overste Scheepens, having got to his feet, forbade any harm to be done to the *oelèëbalang*, but it was already too late.

With his hand pressed on the abdominal wound, overste Scheepens walked on foot to the military sick-bay, saying: "that has gone well a couple of times, but this time it has gone wrong." The *officier van gezondheid* at Sigli gave first aid; Scheepens was bandaged and put to bed in the sick-bay.

## Swart's response

[[H. N. A. Swart]] was at once informed of the event by telephone; by special tram he travelled to Sigli with the well-known surgeon [[Dubinsky|dr. Dubinsky]]. But surgical intervention could no longer help. Scheepens died on [[17 October]] [[1913]].

At the burial in the heroes' cemetery [[Peutjoet]], Swart delivered the funeral oration — "and then, perhaps for the first time in all those hard Atjeh years, Swart lost mastery of his nerves… the address had to be broken off."

The monthly report closed: "Peace and order in the territory of [[Titeuë]] were not disturbed for a moment by what had occurred."

## Consequences

Indirectly the murder brought [[H. J. Schmidt]] back to Atjeh. On leave in Holland, Schmidt wished to be reposted to Atjeh, but the army commander — [[Gotfried Coenraad Ernst van Daalen|Van Daalen]] — would not permit it; between Van Daalen and Swart, then Major-General, matters had come almost to "a painful war." "Only by a chance — and that was, alas, the murder of Scheepens — did Schmidt after all return to Atjeh, and even before his time; Swart could then demand a wholly suitable officer for the difficult [[Meulaboh]] district, and that the army commander could not refuse."

## Significance

The Scheepens case is the classic Dutch illustration of the collision between Acehnese *adat* honour and colonial criminal law: the *oelèëbalang*'s claim to the right of blood-vengeance against the whole family of the man who had shed his son's blood, the *adat* adviser's tariff of thirty dollars blood-price, and the Dutch maximum of three months' imprisonment for slight wounding were incommensurable, and the sense of *maloe* that arose from that incommensurability produced a murder. It fits precisely into du Croo's general explanation of the [[Atjeh-moord]].
