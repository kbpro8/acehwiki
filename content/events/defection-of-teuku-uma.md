The **defection of Teuku Uma** in March [[1896]] was the moment at which the Achehnese chief [[Teuku Uma]] — appointed a commander of friendly Achehnese forces and treated by the Dutch Governor as a trusty councillor — openly transferred his allegiance from the [[Achehnese War|Dutch Government to the Achehnese war-party]], bringing to an end the disastrous episode known as the [[Uma period|"Uma" period]]. The event was, in [[C. Snouck Hurgronje|Snouck Hurgronje]]'s words, more a revelation than a reversal: "since the real purport of his actions had long been as obvious as the day, we cannot speak except in a highly specialized sense of Uma's 'defection'."

## Background: the "Uma" period

The defection was the close of three years during which the adventurer [[Teuku Uma]] — strengthened by Dutch weapons and money lavished upon him by [[General Deykerhoff|General Deykerhoff]], a Governor "absolutely unacquainted with native affairs" — had used his position to enrich the war-party under the guise of fighting its foes. As Hurgronje describes it, civil war had raged "even after the Dutch power was established in Acheh"; and under the guise of fighting against the foes of the Dutch regime, "he dealt out, as his own, plentiful subsidies to his friends in the War-party and tried by all means in his power to acquire the maximum of influence both over the Dutch and their opponents."

## The end of double-dealing

"From the very first", Hurgronje observes, Uma "set himself to winning substantial gains for the War-party and nominal advantages for the Dutch, which last advantages however seriously taken had no real value whatever." The only apparent gain which he brought to his protector was a "line" of greater extent than the original "concentrated" line — and that gain "was due to — and continued to depend on — the slender thread of his good-will." It was clear that the arrangement could not last. "As soon as it ceased to be possible for Uma to continue to combine the satisfaction of the wishes of the War-party with the appearance of advancing the interests of the Government, the barrel would burst — and there was not much doubt on which side Uma would elect to be."

## March 1896

In March [[1896]], Uma thought that the time had come to remove the scales from the eyes of the blindly-trusting General Deykerhoff. "He had then sufficient money and arms to play a leading part among the War-party, and the claims which his friend Deykerhoff pressed upon him began to be burdensome." His secret opposition to Dutch rule thus changed to open hostility. Hurgronje insists, however, that the term "defection" is misleading: the real purport of Uma's actions had long been obvious, and the Governor's eyes had simply refused to see.

## Consequences

The Uma episode, however melancholy in itself, "indirectly exercised a healthy influence upon the management of Achehnese affairs. Its termination opened all eyes to the need of vigorous action if one did not wish to abandon altogether the subjugation of Acheh." In [[1896]] the war against the Achehnese, which had been allowed to be suspended since [[1881]] without any real resultant gain, was resumed. Above all, with [[Van Heutsz's governorship|Governor Van Heutsz in 1898]] the new methodical offensive began that, "in less than six years", brought the subjugation of Acheh to the beginning of its end. The traitor [[Teuku Uma|Uma]] and numbers of adventurers, fanatical leaders, and guerrilla chiefs perished in the campaigns that followed.
