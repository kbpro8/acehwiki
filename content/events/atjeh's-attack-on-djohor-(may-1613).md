**Atjeh's attack on Djohor of May 1613** — the Achehnese naval expedition sent by the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|Sultan of Atjeh]]'s successor against the kingdom of [[Djohor]], which appeared at the mouth of the Djohor river on [[7 May]] [[1613]] while the bulk of the Djohor fleet was away at [[Pahang]], took the city of [[Batoesawer|Batoesawer]] on [[6 June]] [[1613]] after a brief defence by the small Dutch garrison and the lodge personnel, captured twenty-two of the Dutchmen and brought them back to Atjeh as prisoners, and inaugurated the Achehnese occupation of Djohor that lasted until the rapprochement between the two Malay courts in [[1614]] — is the closing military episode of the Atjeh chapter of Johan van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## Background: the Atjeh-Djohor-Portuguese triangle

The attack of [[1613]] was the latest eruption of the long-standing enmity between [[Acheh|Atjeh]] and [[Djohor]] that Van der Woude traces back to the [[1587]] peace between Atjeh and the Portuguese, by which the wingewest of [[Aru]] had been transferred to Djohor. Each of the two Malay kingdoms sought Portuguese support against the other; the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] had sent envoys to [[Goa|Goa]] in [[1598]] asking Portuguese help against Djohor, while the sultan of Djohor had concluded his own peace with the Portuguese in [[1610]] after years of Dutch-supported resistance. The peace of [[1610]] freed the sultan of Djohor to resume his campaign against Pahang, and it was while the Djohor fleet was at Pahang that the Atjeh fleet descended on the unprotected capital.

## The Dutch position at Batoesawer

The Dutch had maintained a factory at [[Batoesawer|Batoesawer]], the capital of Djohor, since the mission of [[Cornelis Pietersz.|Cornelis Pietersz.]] in [[1603]] and the second accord of [[Cornelis Matelieff|Cornelis Matelieff]] with the sultan of [[23 September]] [[1606]]. The factor at the time of the attack was **Jacques Obelaer**, who had succeeded **Van den Broeck** in February [[1609]]. When the Atjeh fleet appeared on [[7 May]] [[1613]], the Dutch ship *Hope*, which had arrived three days earlier to relieve the factor and replenish the factory's capital, lay at anchor in the river. The Achehnese demanded that the Dutch stand aside; the Dutch refused, in fidelity to the Matelieff-sultan accord, and landed thirty-one men under the factory's six-man staff to assist the Djohor defence. "Doende daermede die van Atchijn alle mogelijcke affbreuck. Doch dies nietteghenstaande hebben die van Atchijn den 6en Juny de stadt innegecreghen, sijnde haer door die van Joor opgegeven." Two Dutchmen were killed; twenty-two were taken prisoner and carried off to Atjeh "onder borchtocht van des compagnies goederen"; three wounded men returned to the ship; the rest were missing.

## The aftermath and the rapprochement

The prisoners' fate was bound up with the rapid Achehnese-Djohor reconciliation that followed. Within months the two Malay courts had reached an accommodation: the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] gave his sister in marriage to the deposed sultan of Djohor — now styled **Sultan Sabrang** — and sent him back to Djohor with an Achehnese escort of two thousand men to rebuild the destroyed city. The Dutch prisoners were released as part of the rapprochement. Van der Woude notes that "bijna een jaar lang lag de handel nu geheel stil" before the opperkoopman **Adriaan van der Dussen** arrived at Djohor in [[August]] [[1614]] to negotiate the construction of a fort.

## The Dutch role and its limits

The Dutch position at Batoesawer throughout the attack illustrates the limits of the Dutch commitment to [[Djohor]]. The fleet of [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] had been withdrawn in [[1607]], the fleet of [[Pieter Willemsz. Verhoeff|Verhoeff]] had been unable to relieve the sultan in [[1608]] because of contrary winds, and the two ships left at Batoesawer after Verhoeff's departure in February [[1609]] had stayed only until November. The Dutch factory survived on the sultan's sufferance; the Dutch could not defend him against a major Achehnese attack. Van der Woude's verdict on the Dutch-Djohor alliance is sharp: "ware de vrede tusschen de inlandsche vorsten gesloten, het zou den Hollanders met hulp van beiden weinig moeite hebben gekost Malaka in het Oosten en het Westen te blokkeeren en den Portugeeschen handel op de Noordelijke gebieden te vernietigen." The Dutch failure to maintain a fleet in Djohor waters in force meant that the strategic opening the sultan of Djohor had offered them — to take [[Malaka]] from the Portuguese — was never exploited.

## Significance

Atjeh's attack on Djohor of [[1613]] is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the principal instance of the Achehnese projection of naval power against a Malay rival in the period before the Dutch had established a permanent fleet in the Strait of Malaka. The attack also marks the effective end of the Dutch-Djohor military alliance that [[Cornelis Matelieff|Matelieff]] had constructed in [[1606]]: after [[1613]] the Dutch could no longer count on Djohor as a stable base against the Portuguese, and their attention shifted southward to [[Jacatra|Jacatra]] and the eventual founding of [[Batavia|Batavia]] in [[1619]]. The attack is also the principal instance of the Achehnese use of the Dutch prisoners as diplomatic counters: their release was tied to the Achehnese-Djohor marriage alliance of [[1614]], and the affair showed the Dutch that the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]]'s court was capable of conducting its own regional diplomacy without European mediation. The attack's aftermath — the rapid Achehnese-Djohor reconciliation, the rebuilding of Batoesawer under Achehnese sponsorship — also marks the limit of the Dutch ability to shape Malay politics in this period, and frames the strategic situation that [[Jan Pieterszoon Coen|Coen]] would inherit as director-general in [[1614]].
