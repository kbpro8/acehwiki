The **Adat meulangga** (also written *meudangga*) was a distinctive Achehnese institution for the settlement of disputes arising from an actual injury or slight offered by one gampong to another. It was controlled by an adat of native growth which, as Snouck Hurgronje observes, had clearly lost much of its original severity. In these cases the [[keuchi']]s of the two gampongs concerned appeared in form as the representatives of the two parties, but as a matter of fact as settlers of the dispute. The adat meulangga lay outside the ordinary jurisdiction of the [[uleebalang]], who had little to do with the daily life of the gampong in the narrower sense of the words; it was administered entirely by the village authorities.

Occasion for the application of the adat meulangga was generally given by an actual injury or slight. For example, someone in gampong A had without just cause maltreated or injured a child or relative of someone in gampong B, or had laid hands on something belonging to the latter in a manner clearly indicative of contempt for the owner. Independently of all rights of blood-vengeance or blood-money (which, according to *hukom* and *adat*, were the natural consequences of the deed and were entirely unaffected by the adat meulangga), something had to be done at once to wipe out the insult. To this end the injured party called upon the authorities of his gampong, and the latter summoned all who were able to bear arms to help their brother. The party who had given the offence knew that he had this to expect, either from hearing the rumour of the preparations or because it was an obvious consequence that the offended one should not let the matter pass without meulangga.

In due time the men of the offended gampong appeared in the vicinity of the other, and the *keuchi'* of the latter went forth to meet the uninvited guests and, after greeting them respectfully (*seumbah*), asked what they required. "We have come", they replied, "to uproot by force the trees and courtyard-fence of your fellow-villager X, by reason of the injury he has done to one of our people." Thereupon the other *keuchi'* said that he felt bound to acknowledge the fairness of the complaint, but that this just demand could be satisfied without having recourse to deeds of violence. The father of the offending community then brought to the other one or two plantain-stems from the courtyard and a *glundòng*-tree from the fence of the guilty party, saying: "Here is what you ask for." Sometimes, where the injury was a very serious one, he granted them permission to enter upon the courtyard of the offenders and cut down a certain number of trees. Should the atonement offered by the *keuchi'* not suffice for the satisfaction of honour according to the adat controlling quarrels, long-continued hostilities between two neighbouring gampongs might supervene; but for the most part the mediation effected under the auspices of the two *keuchi'*s was found sufficient.

The day after this symbolical satisfaction, the parties met again to consult as to the guarantees for the establishment of peace. An *idang* of yellow gelatinous rice (*bu kunyèt*) and a piece of white cloth had without fail to be offered by the offender for "cooling of the blood of others" (*peusijuek darah gob*) which he had shed, as the saying was. To this ceremony of "cooling" the Achehnese attached the highest importance, since without it the peace between the gampongs could not be regarded as firm. The meulangga thus enacted, in dramatic form, the three stages by which an Achehnese quarrel was supposed to be closed: threat of violence, symbolic reparation, and the formal "cooling" (*[[peusijuek]]*) which restored social harmony. As Snouck Hurgronje remarks, the adat meulangga was carried out without the intervention of the uleebalang—a striking instance of the self-governing capacity of the Achehnese gampong, even where matters of honour and arms were concerned.
