**Captain Coombs's first Acheh mission** of late [[1817]] – February [[1818]] was the commission dispatched during the governorship of [[Governor Petrie|Governor Petrie]] of [[Penang|Penang]] to [[Acheh]] "to seek the true sultan" amid the [[Acheh succession dispute (1802-1819)|Acheh succession dispute]] between [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] at [[Pedir]] and [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] at [[Telok Semawé]]. The mission is recorded by Van der Kemp as the immediate precursor of the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs commission]] of 1819, and as the source of [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]'s settled conviction that [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] was the recognised sultan — a conviction that would lead directly to the [[Paper war|paper war]] with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] on the roadstead of [[Banda Acheh]].

## The Mission

[[Captain Coombs|Captain Coombs]] was dispatched to Acheh at the end of 1817 or the beginning of 1818. On arrival he was informed by the chiefs that they had forever abjured [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] and had recognised [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] as their sultan. A few days before [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]]'s arrival, however, a British brig under British colours had been boarded and plundered by [[Tunku Pakih|Tuku Paekie of Pedir]] — the [[Capture of British brig by Tunku Pakih|episode Van der Kemp mentions separately]]. [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] raised the matter with the chiefs, who professed indignation at the scandal and promised to summon [[Tunku Pakih|Paekie]] to account for it.

After a stay of fourteen days at [[Banda Acheh|Acheh]], [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] left the inhospitable port in February 1818, having had a meeting with [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]]. He was convinced that [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] was the recognised ruler, but also that the British government would have to support him with a thousand men if he were to maintain his authority.

## Aftermath

[[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] then proceeded to [[Calcutta]] to inform the Bengal government in accordance with the wishes of the [[Penang|Pinang]] administration. His report set the stage for the conflict of authorities that would ensue. When, in early 1819, [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] — lieutenant-governor of [[Bencoolen|Bencoolen]] — arrived at [[Calcutta]] to urge the cause of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] and to demand action against the supposed Dutch intrigues with that prince, he found [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] already there pressing the opposite case on behalf of [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]]. Governor-General [[Lord Moira|Lord Moira]], unable to decide between the conflicting advices, chose to send the two as joint commissioners, with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] enjoying a casting vote — the arrangement that produced the [[Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission (1819)|Raffles-Coombs Acheh Commission]].

## Significance

The mission also established the [[Three Sagis]]'s preference for [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] — a preference they would later reiterate, in unambiguous terms, in the [[Three Sagis' letter to Penang (4 December 1822)|letter to Governor [[W. E. Phillips|Phillips]] of 4 December 1822]]. There they reminded the governor that [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] had first come alone to Acheh and that they had then declared [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]] to be their sultan; that when [[Captain Coombs|Coombs]] had returned with [[Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles|Raffles]] they had given the same answer; and that they wished for no one but [[Sultan Saif ul-Alum|Syf]], requesting his return. Governor [[W. E. Phillips|Phillips]] was forced to refuse, and the recognition of [[Sultan Jauhar Alum|Jauhar Alum]] — to which the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)]] had committed the British — remained a dead letter on the ground.
