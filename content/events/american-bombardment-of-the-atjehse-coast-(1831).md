The **American bombardment of the Atjehse coast** of [[1831]] was a punitive naval action by the United States against the [[Achehnese|Atjehse]] coastal population, undertaken in retaliation for Atjehese piracy against American merchant shipping and explicitly framed by the American government as a punishment of the Atjehse coast population for its piracy and its failure to respect Dutch sovereignty over the [[Acheh|Atjeh]] polity. The action is recorded by [[J. Frijling|Ir. J. Frijling]] in his [[1946]] sketch *Een stukje geschiedenis der Indische Buitengewesten* as one of the two nineteenth-century precedents — the other being the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]] — for the use of foreign naval force against the Atjehse coast in the absence of effective Dutch authority.

## Context

Under the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|Verdrag van Londen]] of [[1814|1814]], by which the [[Netherlands|Netherlands]] recovered most of the colonies that [[Britain|Britain]] had occupied during the Napoleonic period, the Dutch government had promised to combat slavery in the [[Nederlandsch-Indië|Netherlands-Indies]] with vigour. Frijling records that "het bleef bij een belofte" — the promise was not kept. The British government's irritation at this failure was, "althans de reden genoemd," the reason why London broke off the [[1820|1820]] conversations with the Hague over the exchange of [[Billiton|Billiton]] for [[Singapore|Singapore]] and twice refused to resume them.

The Netherlands of [[1814|1814]], after the French occupation, was almost as poor as it would be in [[1945]] after the German occupation. The territory over which it had recovered sovereignty — and in which it was now bound by treaty to combat slavery — extended from the 95th to the 142nd degree of longitude east, more than an eighth of the Earth's circumference measured along the equator. The [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie]], whose rights the Dutch state had inherited, had been able to enforce its authority over no more than ten per cent of the total area of the archipelago; over the whole of Java, but in the Buitengewesten only at a few widely separated posts. Throughout the nineteenth century the Dutch government followed a policy of *niet-inmenging* — non-intervention — which, in the absence of effective authority, allowed the native princes and headmen to fall into increasing mutual discord, "een strijd van allen tegen allen, leidende tot grenzenloze onderdrukking van bevolkingen."

In these conditions slavery flourished, and both piracy (*zeeroverij*) and land-piracy (*landroverij* — rampok- and ketjoepartijen, plundering and extortion) assumed unheard-of proportions. Foreign merchant ships were also among the victims. In [[1831]] the American government, "bij wijze van wraakneming — ontkennende der Nederlandse souvereiniteit," had the Atjehse coastal population chastised by one of its warships.

## The Action

Frijling gives no detail of the warship, the commanding officer, or the precise target of the bombardment; he records only that an American warship "tuchtigde" — chastised — the Atjehse coastal population. "Of schuldigen dan wel onschuldigen door de granaten werden getroffen, vertelt de geschiedenis niet," Frijling observes. The contrast with the later Dutch reluctance to use force — "Onze Angelsaksische neven bezaten in die dagen kennelijk nog niet de lankmoedigheid die zij òns later zouden toewensen" — is the point of his mention.

## Significance

The [[1831]] American bombardment, together with the [[Bombardment of the Atjehse coast by the British (1844)|British bombardment of 1844]], is one of the two precedents for foreign naval action against the Atjehse coast in the period between the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|Verdrag van Londen]] of [[1814]] and the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Sumatra-contract]] of [[1871]]. Both actions illustrate the international pressure on the Dutch to suppress Atjehese piracy and slavery — pressure that ultimately led to the [[Aceh War|Atjeh-oorlog]] of [[1873]]–[[1904]]. The American framing of the action as a punishment "ontkennende der Nederlandse souvereiniteit" is particularly pointed: it asserts that, whatever the paper claims of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London|1814 treaty]], the Atjehse coast was, in the view of the American government, not effectively under Dutch sovereignty and therefore open to direct foreign retribution.
