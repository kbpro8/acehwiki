The **instructions of [[31 August]] [[1824]]** — referred to by P. H. [[P. H. van der Kemp|Van der Kemp]] as the *Indische instructiën* of 31 August 1824 — were the instructions issued by the Netherlands government to the Indian government at [[Batavia]] regarding the execution of the recently ratified [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|Anglo-Dutch treaty of 17 March 1824]]. Drafted under minister [[Elout|Elout]]'s supervision and advised upon by [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]] on [[6 August]] [[1824]], the instructions incorporated — without examination — the British (and Van der Kemp would say, false) interpretation of article 6 of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 Acheh convention]] as excluding the trade of other European powers and Americans from [[Acheh]]. The instructions thus perpetuated, at the level of executive government, the misunderstanding that had entered the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]] by the back door of the annexed notas; and they were followed by the Dutch colonial historians — including [[G. Lauts|Professor Lauts]] and [[Smulders|Smulders]] — who repeated the error in their accounts of the treaty.

## Background

After the ratification of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]], the Netherlands government had to communicate the new arrangements to its Indian government at [[Batavia]]. The instructions for this purpose were drawn up under the supervision of minister [[Elout|Elout]] (whose [[Elout's advice of 12 February 1824|advice of 12 February 1824]] had already influenced the form of the treaty), and were submitted for advice to [[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]], who on [[6 August]] [[1824]] returned them without objection. The instructions were issued on [[31 August]] [[1824]].

## The misinterpretation of article 6

The most important feature of the instructions, for Van der Kemp's purpose, is their treatment of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 Acheh convention]]. The British nota annexed to the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]] had declared that the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] was incompatible with article 3 of the new treaty (prohibiting unequal commercial duties), and that the British plenipotentiaries "would gladly see it modified by a simple arrangement for the hospitable reception of British ships and subjects in the port of Acheh." The Dutch counter-nota, in turn, had declared that the Netherlands would respect the independence of [[Acheh]].

Minister [[Elout|Elout]], in his *Pro Memorie* on the instructions, repeated the British interpretation that the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] had "excluded the trade of other European powers and the Americans from Acheh" — an interpretation that, as Van der Kemp demonstrates at length, was *incorrect*. Article 6 of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] prohibited *residence*, not *trade*. It was therefore not in conflict with article 3 of the new treaty (prohibiting unequal commercial duties), but with article 9 (absolutely excluding British treaties with native states on [[Sumatra]]). The Dutch plenipotentiaries at London had allowed themselves to be drawn into a discussion on the wrong article, and the instructions of [[31 August]] [[1824]] perpetuated the error.

[[Anton Reinhard Falck|Falck]], in advising on the instructions on [[6 August]] [[1824]], "without of course contesting it", accepted the misinterpretation. The instructions of [[31 August]] [[1824]] thus contained, in their treatment of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]], an error that would be reproduced by the Dutch colonial historians for the next half-century.

## The historians' repetition

Van der Kemp notes that the misinterpretation was repeated by the Dutch historians of the colonial period. Professor [[G. Lauts|G. Lauts]] asked: "Had not Raffles and Coombs, in the self-concluded treaty with the sultan of Acheen of the 22nd of Grass-month 1819, article 6, excluded the trade of that kingdom from all other European powers and the Americans?" — to which Van der Kemp replies: "By no means; nor was it self-concluded." [[Smulders|Smulders]], in his otherwise good dissertation on the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 1824]], read the article in the same erroneous sense. The instructions of [[31 August]] [[1824]] thus became the channel through which the British misrepresentation of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 convention]] passed into the Dutch historical canon.

## Significance

The instructions of [[31 August]] [[1824]] are significant in Van der Kemp's analysis as the document by which the misunderstanding of the [[Treaty of Pedir (1819)|1819 Acheh convention]] — introduced at London in February–March 1824 by the British plenipotentiaries and accepted, without examination, by the Dutch plenipotentiaries — was transmitted to the Indian government and so to the colonial historians. They thus form one of the links in the chain of documents by which the Acheh reservation, "smuggled into the treaty without Elout's scrutiny", was transformed from a diplomatic incident into a settled interpretation of the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of 17 March 1824]] — an interpretation that, Van der Kemp argues, paralysed Dutch policy on [[Sumatra]] for nearly half a century and contributed directly to the long Aceh conflict of the late nineteenth century.
