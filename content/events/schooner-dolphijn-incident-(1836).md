**The schooner Dolphijn incident** of [[1836]] — in [[J. Kreemer|Kreemer]]'s history, the plundering by Atjèhnese of the [[Netherlands|Netherlands-Indies government]]'s schooner *Dolphijn*, carrying some ƒ30,000 in specie, and the seizure of the cargo by the [[Sultan of Atjeh|Sultan of Atjèh]] himself — was one of the principal humiliations which, in Kreemer's account, the Dutch government endured between [[1824]] and [[1873]] rather than violate the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty]]'s guarantee of Atjèh's integrity.

## The incident and the Dutch response

Kreemer records the incident in a single passage: "Zelfs toen de Atjèhers in [[1836]] 's lands schooner Dolphijn met ƒ30.000 aan specie aan boord plunderden en het bleek, dat de Soeltan zich had meester gemaakt van de lading; toen het advies der naar Atjèh gezonden commissie luidde, nu toch 'de eer van het Nederlandsch-Indisch Gouvernement met klem te handhaven en den Soeltan door strenge, doch passende maatregelen te dwingen om aan de dezerzijdsche billijke eischen te voldoen', besloot men in de zaak 'voorloopig te berusten'" — "Even when the Atjèhnese in 1836 plundered the government's schooner Dolphijn with ƒ30,000 in specie on board, and it turned out that the Sultan had taken possession of the cargo; when the advice of the commission sent to Atjèh was, now at last, 'to uphold the honour of the Netherlands-Indian Government with force and to compel the Sultan by strict but fitting measures to comply with the reasonable demands of this side', it was decided in the matter 'for the time being to acquiesce.'"

## The pattern of Dutch acquiescence

The Dolphijn incident is, in Kreemer's narrative, one of a sequence of outrages — beginning with the [[1831]] plundering of an American ship at [[Kuala Bathee|Koeala Batèë]] and the [[1833]] American punitive expedition, and continuing through the [[1844]] plundering of two British vessels at [[Kuala Bathee|Koeala Batèë]] and [[Kuala Meureudu|Koeala Meureudoe]] — that revealed the hollowness of the Dutch obligation, undertaken in the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|London treaty of 1824]], to ensure the safety of shipping in Atjèhnese waters. The Dutch government's decision to "for the time being acquiesce" — *voorloopig te berusten* — in the face of the Sultan's seizure of government specie is, Kreemer implies, the most humiliating of these submissions, since the wrong was done not to a foreign merchant but to the Dutch state itself.

## Significance

The Dolphijn incident is, in Kreemer's account of the causes of the [[Aceh War|Atjèh-oorlog]], a key exhibit in the case that the Dutch policy of "schipperen" — trimming — between the 1824 treaty's twin obligations (to ensure safety in Atjèhnese waters and to respect Atjèh's integrity) was unsustainable. The advice of the commission sent to Atjèh — that the government's honour should now at last be upheld — is the first recorded official recommendation of a forceful policy; its rejection marks the depth of the Dutch commitment to the 1824 framework, and thus the depth of the eventual rupture when that framework was finally abandoned in [[1873]].
