The **Dutch–Aceh Treaty of [[1649]]** was an agreement concluded in November of that year between [[Sultan Tajul-alam|Tajul-alam]], the "Sultane of Aceh," and the [[Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) envoy [[Johan Truytman]]. It secured the Company important trade advantages across the entire West Coast of Sumatra, including the exclusion of all other European and indigenous nations (except Acehnese) and the regulation of pepper prices.

## Background

After the VOC's conquest of [[Malakka]] in 1641, the Company's aggressive trade policies on the Malay Peninsula had strained relations with Aceh. By 1649, however, both sides sought a new accommodation. Truytman was dispatched by the High Government in Batavia and succeeded in winning Tajul-alam over with tactful diplomacy.

## Provisions

The treaty:
- Granted the VOC extensive trading privileges along the whole of Sumatra's West Coast.
- Excluded all other European nations, as well as all indigenous peoples except the Acehnese, from trading in these areas.
- Fixed pepper prices, giving the VOC certainty and control over the trade.
- Was an important step in securing the Company's dominance in the region's pepper and other commodities.

## Significance

The treaty marked a high point in VOC-Aceh relations in the mid-17th century. It built on the [[Dutch–Aceh Treaty of 1641|1641 treaty]] and paved the way for the subsequent agreement on tin trade in [[Perak]] ([[Treaty of 1650 on Perak Tin|1650]]). It demonstrated the Company's growing power and its ability to negotiate favourable terms even with strong independent states like Aceh. The treaty remained in force for several years and helped regulate VOC commerce until later conflicts.
