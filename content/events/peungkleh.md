The **peungkleh** (literally "to set apart" or "to give up control of") was the Achehnese ceremony by which the parents or next of kin of a married woman formally relinquished, in the presence of the assembled authorities of the gampong, all direct interference with the concerns of their daughter for the remainder of her married life. The ceremony took place at the end of the *janji jinanèe*—the period of one or more years during which, by Achehnese adat, a bride remained a charge upon her parents rather than upon her husband—and marked the moment at which she was "put forth" and placed under the sole protection of her husband. After the conclusion of the ceremony it was said of the woman that she *ka meungklèh*—"is put forth and placed under the protection of her husband."

## The janji jinanèe

The period during which the wife still remained a charge upon her parents was called the **janji jinanèe** ("the dowry period agreed on"), since the length of this period depended upon the amount of the *jinanèe* (dowry). For every *bungkay* of gold (twenty-five dollars) of the marriage gift, the bride was made dependent for a full year on the support of her parents. During this period, Achehnese adat set the husband free from all obligation to maintain his wife; all that he gave her during this period was regarded as a free gift. These gifts consisted first of all in the monthly *biaya* of three or four dollars or more, which might be almost considered as boarding expenses for the ten to fifteen days in each month which the young man spent in the house of his wife; and secondly in the obligation to "bring home meat" at the [[Two great Mohammedan feasts|two great Mohammedan feasts]], and especially that at the end of the fasting month.

## Inheritance during the jinanèe period

During the janji jinanèe, the Achehnese adat was in conflict with Mohammedan law on the question of inheritance. The Mohammedan law allowed unconditionally to the husband a certain definite portion of the goods of his wife on her decease, and vice versa; in Acheh, this right of inheritance did not become effective until after the janji jinanèe had ceased. Should the wife die during this period, the husband in Acheh had the right to only one of two things: he either got back half of the dowry (a sum known as *ha' balèe*, from the Arabic *haqq* "right" and *balèe* "widow or widower"), or he waived this right and chose rather to marry a sister or other near relative of the deceased. In the latter event the *jinanèe* was mentioned at the marriage but never paid. Marriages of this kind were known as **pulang balèe**. A wife who became a widow during the janji jinanèe had just as little claim to her legitimate portion, but received in its place a sum of money equal to half of her dowry, while in certain cases the walls of her husband offered her a suitor for her hand—preferably of the same family.

## The ceremony of peungkleh

As soon as the year or years of the janji jinanèe were over, the woman was committed to the sole charge of her husband. If she was an orphan, this was done without much formality; but with great ceremony if her father or mother was still living. The parents "put her forth" (*peungkleh*) in the presence of all the authorities of the gampong and other notabilities as witnesses, and marked the occasion by a public feast (*kanduri*) consecrated with prayers. In the set speech in which the father abandoned all direct interference with the concerns of his daughter for the remainder of her married life, he summed up all that she had received from him, so that no unpleasantness might arise on any subsequent distribution of her property. Either he or another in his name delivered himself somewhat as follows:

> "The reason why I have summoned you hither, Teuku Keuchi', Teungku and all ye elders, is that I have now 'put forth' my daughter N; be this known unto all of you, O elders. What I have given her is as follows: a pair of anklets 6 bungkay in weight, one yò of rice land, a pair of earrings, this house and all its equipment. This is what I wished to inform you."

The detailed catalogue of the dowry, pronounced before the assembled authorities of the gampong, was of great practical importance: by thus formally recording the property which the bride took with her into her husband's household, the parents prevented later disputes about its disposition at the time of inheritance.
