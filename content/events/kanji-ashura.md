**Kanji Ashura** (also written *kanji Achura*) was the special dish of porridge prepared in [[Acheh]] on the 10th day of [[Muharram]] (the first month of the Mohammedan year, called in Acheh *Asan-Usen*). The day itself, known as [[Ashura]] (Ach. *Achura*; Jav. *Sura*), passed almost unnoticed in orthodox Achehnese observance, since none but very devout persons observed the *sunat* fast prescribed for it in the books of Mohammedan law. But the custom of cooking special viands on that day survived as a popular usage—an "insignificant adjunct", as Snouck Hurgronje calls it, of the [[Hasan-Husain Festival]] that had been transplanted from the Deccan, retaining in Acheh an independent existence long after the processions and litanies of the original feast had ceased to be a native observance.

The kanji Ashura consisted of rice, cocoanut milk, sugar and pieces of cocoanut, mixed with various fruits cut into small pieces—papayas (*boh peutè*), peas (*reuteu'è*), pomegranates (*boh glima*), plantains, sugarcane and various edible roots. The dish was the Achehnese cognate of the *khichri* of Hindustan, the *hubub* or "seeds" of Cairo, and the *bubur sura* of Java, in which various grains or seeds such as maize and peas were mixed with pieces of cocoanut and placed on top of the rice. As in Java, so in Acheh: the mess was not cooked in every separate house. One or two large pots full sufficed for a whole [[gampong]], and those who undertook the cooking received voluntary subscriptions from their fellow-villagers.

The kanji was then brought to the *meunasah* or sometimes to the junction of the gampong-path with the main road. All who wished fell to and gormandised, generally, Snouck Hurgronje remarks, "to such an extent as to cause indigestion". The blessing of the porridge by a prayer, though common in Java, was not customary in Acheh. In neither country was the feast strictly limited to the 10th of the month, but it often extended some days beyond. The Kanji Ashura thus stands in marked contrast with the more solemn observances of [[Ramadhan]] and the [[maulid sharaful 'anam]]: it was a feast of popular hospitality, almost devoid of overt religious form, surviving from a layer of South-Indian devotional practice that had never quite taken root in Achehnese soil.

A further survival of the old Hasan-Husain commemoration was the belief that the first ten days of Muharram were unlucky. On them no work of importance was begun, no marriage with a virgin consummated (since that would mean speedy separation or the death of one of the pair), no child circumcised, no rice sown or planted out. The name "fire-month" (*buleuën apuy*) given to the Achura-month to account for these adat-rules was peculiarly Achehnese; it may, Snouck Hurgronje suggests, have preserved an allusion to the dances of the Hasan-Husain feasters round fires, as practised to this day in [[Trumon]] and in the Deccan.
