**Van Kol's refutation of the causes of the Atjeh-oorlog** was the historical section of the speech delivered by [[H. van Kol]] in the [[Tweede Kamer]] on [[16 November]] [[1897]], in which the social-democratic deputy for Enschedé examined and rejected each of the three official grounds on which the Dutch declaration of war against the [[Sultanate of Atjeh]] of [[26 March]] [[1873]] had been justified: the practice of piracy on the Acehnese coasts, the continual wars among the Acehnese statelets, and the danger of the interference of foreign powers.

## The three alleged causes

Van Kol stated that three reasons had been given: "1. het plegen van zeeroof op de Atjehsche kusten, 2. de voortdurende oorlogen onder de verschillende Staatjes, en 3. gevaar voor inmenging van Vreemde Mogendheden." — 1. the committing of piracy on the Acehnese coasts, 2. the continual wars among the various petty States, and 3. danger of interference by Foreign Powers. Not one of these pretended causes, he held, could stand the test of unprejudiced investigation.

## Piracy

Piracy could not be denied, but Van Kol asked whether it had been as bad as was so often maintained, chiefly on the authority of vehemently written papers of the [[Straits Settlements|Straits-Settlement]] which had an interest in urging the Dutch to war, since [[Penang|Penang]] owed no small part of its prosperity to it. He held that the register of Acehnese sins had been scrutinised and published with the utmost care, and that even so the only speaking facts of piracy to be found were in [[1851]], when one American, one Netherlands, two English and one Neapolitan ship were plundered. He asked why the request of [[Abraham Kuyper|Kuyper]] for a complete statistic of the cases of piracy had never been met.

Did that give the Dutch, in [[1873]], the right to declare war on the [[Sultan of Atjeh|sultan]]? The man was perhaps unwilling; that was not known, and no single fact demonstrated it conclusively. He had even promised by treaty in [[1857]] to prevent piracy — see [[Tractaat van 1857]] — but he could not, being as powerless in that respect as the Dutch themselves were off [[Sumatra]], off [[New Guinea|Nieuw-Guinea]] and even off [[Java]].

Van Kol supported this with the [[Koloniaal Verslag]] itself, which recorded that in [[1896]] two cases of piracy occurred at [[Japara]], two at [[Madura|Madoera]], and one each at [[Batavia]], [[Surabaya|Soerabaya]] and Probolingo; and that on the [[Mentawai Islands|Mentawei-eilanden]] prauws were "niet zelden" robbed and their crews murdered without the Dutch being able to prevent it. He read from *[[De Locomotief]]* of the previous month that pirates lay constantly in wait in the straat Madoera, that several prauw-masters refused freight for the Oosthoek, and that the *tambangans* which formerly sailed by night to Kamal and [[Gresik|Grissee]] now took care to be in before dark, since the sea-rovers had more than once ventured after sunset even onto the roadstead near the guard-ship.

He asked whether for that which the Dutch themselves could not prevent an independent prince might be thrust from his throne and his land taken from him, recalling that head-hunting still occurred in the part of [[Timor]] under direct Dutch authority, that widows had within a few years been burned on the pyres of the Hindoe-vorsten van Bali, and that slavery still existed in the Gouvernement Sumatra's Westkust, in [[Toba]] and [[Silindoeng]], in the southern and eastern divisions of [[Borneo]], in South [[Celebes]], on Soemba and [[Lombok]] — with f [[1500]] entered in the very budget before the chamber for the limitation of slavery in the self-governing *landschappen* of the government of Celebes. What would be said, he asked, if another power declared war on Netherlands India because the government was unable to prevent piracy, head-hunting, slavery and cannibalism in its own dependencies? Why measure with two measures according to whether princes had a white or a brown skin?

## Internal quarrels

The second ground he dismissed by the same method. Were such wars not equally the order of the day on numbers of islands of the [[Indian Archipelago|Indischen Archipel]] nominally subject to Dutch authority? Did not the [[Gayo|Gajoe]]s and [[Alas|Alla]]s from time to time raid in the Battak landen? Were there not continual fights in [[Toba]] and [[Silindoeng]]? Did not inhabitants of [[Jambi|Djambi]] make incursions into the [[Padangsche Bovenlanden]], in which three Dutch subjects were shot dead? Had the Dutch not often refused to meddle in the internal wars of the island of [[Nias]]? Had the Dutch administration not that very year let the *landschappen* of [[Timor]] fight quietly among themselves so long as Dutch subjects were not involved, just as wars between the tribes were continually in progress on North and East [[Flores]] and in the Solor and Allor islands? Why then must the Dutch intervene in Atjeh, in quarrels between tribes wholly independent of them?

## Foreign interference

The third reason he called somewhat less far-fetched than the others, but held that even before the war began it was known that the so-called *verraad* of the Acehnese consisted in a few wholly or half-invented conversations of doubtful persons and adventurers with the trade consuls of [[Italy|Italië]] and [[United States of America|N.-Amerika]], who exercised not the least power and were at once disavowed by their governments — a point marked in the pamphlet by the marginal note "Tegengesproken" (contradicted). That the sultan sought support was, he said, obvious: as an independent prince he had the fullest right to do so, and against the conduct of the [[Nederlandsch-Indisch Gouvernement|Netherlands Indies Government]] it was for him a requirement of self-preservation.

## The history of encroachment

Van Kol placed the three causes against a narrative of Dutch encroachment. Once the mightiest empire of [[Sumatra]], possessing the greater half of the island, Atjeh had long seen its territory crumble away into Dutch hands. After the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|O.-I. Compagnie]] left [[Sumatra's Westkust]] towards the end of the previous century, withdrew the post at [[Baros]] and delivered [[Padang]] to the English, the whole trade had passed to the English until [[1819]]. In [[1824]] the Dutch concluded the [[Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824|treaty of London]], by which the fruits of earlier robbery were bartered away and Sumatra was obtained in exchange for [[Malacca|Malakka]] under the express condition that relations with Atjeh should be so regulated that the sultan should lose nothing of his independence.

As Atjeh's power declined it soon lost [[Johor|Djohor]] and some dependencies of [[Siak]], one of its vassal kingdoms. The Dutch attacked [[Baros]] in [[1829]] and [[1834]], and were repelled with Acehnese help. In [[1838]] warships went out to extend Dutch power at [[Baros]], [[Tapoes]] and [[Singkel]]; the raja of Singkel, [[Mohammed Aroef]], asked to be left to live in peace, saying "wij zijn een vrij volk en doen niemand kwaad" (we are a free people and do no one harm), and found it "zeer onbillijk, zich eens anders goed toe te eigenen, alleen omdat men meer geld en meer volk voor den oorlog heeft" (most unjust to appropriate another's goods merely because one has more money and more people for war). The government remained deaf, and [[Baros]] was occupied in [[1839]] and [[Singkel]] in [[1840]]. In [[1848]] the Acehnese tried to retake Singkel but were not strong enough; fighting followed in [[1848]]–[[1849]] and [[1851]], and a treaty was concluded with [[Troemon]] to make it independent of Atjeh — that is, said Van Kol, to raise it in revolt against its liege lord.

On the [[Oostkust van Sumatra|East Coast of Sumatra]] Dutch conquest-hunger had roused the jealousy of [[England]], whose remonstrances against the aggressive policy of the day were so serious that the posts at [[Indragiri]] and [[Siak]] were withdrawn in [[1842]]. What the Dutch had done half a century earlier out of fear, Van Kol argued, they ought now to repeat at Atjeh's northern point out of a sense of right alone.

In [[1857]] the Dutch concluded with the sultan the so-called [[Tractaat van 1857|Atjeh-tractaat]] of trade and friendship, and as a proof of friendship concluded a treaty with [[Siak]], over which Atjeh had long asserted rights and whose sultan now recognised Dutch suzerainty. Apart from [[Asahan|Assahan]], Dutch sovereignty had been made to be recognised as far as the northern boundary of [[Tamiang|Tamian]], the Acehnese always resisting, until by an expedition in [[1865]] Assahan too, and thus the whole East Coast of Sumatra, came into Dutch hands. Two expeditions were sent to [[Nias]] in [[1863]] and one to [[Singkel]] in [[1865]]. It was therefore not surprising, Van Kol said, that the sultan sought support against Dutch overwhelming, and in [[1868]] even offered the sovereignty of his lands to his religious head, the Commander of the Faithful, the [[Sultan of Turkey|Sultan of Turkije]] — a wholly comprehensible attempt to preserve his independence.

The Dutch meanwhile did not sit still: by abolishing the differential duties in [[Siak]] English trade was satisfied, by throwing a piece of the coast of Guinea to the English government that government was satisfied, and in November [[1871]] the [[Sumatra Tractaat (1871)|treaty with England]] was concluded which left the Dutch a free hand on Sumatra, that is against Atjeh. Use was made of it with all too much eagerness, and already on [[26 March]] [[1873]] war was declared.

## Kielstra's testimony

Van Kol read into the record the passage from [[E. B. Kielstra|Major Kielstra]]'s *[[Kielstra's Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog (1883-1885)|Beschrijving van den Atjeh-oorlog]]*, volume I, page 27, in which the Dutch historian conceded that Acehnese and Netherlanders had for the last two centuries stood more or less hostile to one another; that impartiality obliged one to add that the Acehnese also had many reasons to distrust the Dutch; that the Dutch conquests on Sumatra's West Coast were at first nothing but hostilities against Atjeh committed exclusively in the interest of the monopoly system of the [[Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie|O.-I. Compagnie]]; that Acehnese merchants had legitimate grievances over the petty vexations of the Dutch tariff and the arbitrary conduct of government officials; that the commanders of the cruising boats permitted themselves many acts of violence at Acehnese expense — as in [[1834]] or [[1835]] near the island of Pontjang, where Acehnese prauws provided with proper passes were detained and their crews partly murdered and partly imprisoned; that the crews of the cruising boats, covered by the Netherlands flag and by their official title and under the appearance of a scrupulous application of the tariff, had repeatedly made themselves guilty of acts differing little from piracy; and that no efforts at all had been made to come to better terms with Atjeh, the only thing Atjeh learned of the Dutch being the confirmation of their authority on Sumatra's West Coast, which it was bound to regard as an act of enmity.

## The ultimatum

Van Kol concluded that the Dutch had sought the war, given the occasion and provoked the outbreak. Despite the time granted the sultan to answer, with [[29 March]] fixed as the ultimatum, the bombardment of the coast and the shooting down of the population began already on [[27 March]] [[1873]]. The decision for war had been taken in a shockingly frivolous manner and the war irresponsibly prepared: Atjeh was believed to be in deep decay, in complete anarchy, with no power, no money and no weapons, unable to count on the help of its vassals, and it was thought probable that victory would be gained without a blow and the whole affair finished before the monsoon.
