**The capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh of 3 October 1602** — the joint Anglo-Dutch naval operation in which Sir **[[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]]**'s four English ships and **[[Joris Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]]**'s two Dutch ships, having sailed from [[Acheh|Atjeh]] on [[21 September]] [[1602]] and spread themselves across the [[Strait of Malaka|Strait of Malaka]], intercepted, attacked and captured the annual Portuguese carrack from the [[Koromandel kust|Koromandel coast]] bound for [[Malaka]], taking 950 bales of calicoes and pintados plus rice and other merchandise — is the first major Anglo-Dutch joint naval prize in the East Indies and the closing episode of the early Dutch-English-Portuguese triangular contest at Atjeh. The capture is the culminating event of the early-Atjeh chapter of Johan van der Woude's [[Coen, Koopman van Heeren Zeventien (1948)|Van der Woude (1948)]].

## Background: Lancaster's plan

When [[James Lancaster|James Lancaster]] arrived off Atjeh on [[5 June]] [[1602]] with the first English East India Company fleet, he found the pepper prices higher than his pilot [[John Davis|John Davis]] had led him to expect and his own capital insufficient for a full cargo. He sent one of his ships to [[Priaman]] on the west coast of Sumatra to make up the deficit, and conceived the plan of intercepting the annual Portuguese carrack that came at this season from the Koromandel coast to Malaka — a prize that would repay the voyage and inflict commercial damage on the common enemy. Lancaster, who had practised such buitvaart in earlier voyages, displayed "veel beleid" in inducing the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] to prevent news of his arrival from reaching Malaka by way of the Portuguese or their agents.

## The Anglo-Dutch squadron

The Dutch yacht *'t Lam under [[Cornelis Spex|Cornelis Spex]] — separated from the Spilbergh fleet near the Comoros and too damaged to continue — had arrived off Atjeh in early September [[1602]] and placed itself under Lancaster's command. A few days later [[Joris Spilbergh|Joris Spilbergh]] himself arrived with the other two ships of his fleet, having called at [[Ceylon]] on the way. When Spilbergh heard of Lancaster's plan, he offered to join with one of his ships for an eighth of the prize. The offer was accepted; on [[21 September]] [[1602]] the combined squadron — four English ships and two Dutch, plus the yacht *'t Lam — sailed from Atjeh and spread themselves across the Strait of Malaka, "al spread our selues a mile and a halfe one from another".

## The capture and the division

On [[3 October]] [[1602]] a sail was sighted: the expected Portuguese carrack from the Koromandel coast. She was quickly taken. The captors set about transshipping her cargo, "Within fiue of sixe daies we had unladen her of nine hundred and fiftie packes of calicoes and pintados, besides many packets of merchandize: she had in her much rice and other goods, whereof we made full account". Spilbergh received 120 bales and his share of the chests for his eighth. The 600 Portuguese crew were allowed to depart unhindered for Malaka in the empty carrack.

## Significance

The capture of the Portuguese carrack off Atjeh is, in Van der Woude's narrative, the high-water mark of the early Anglo-Dutch partnership in the East Indies, and the principal instance of the buitvaart — prize-taking as a legitimate commercial instrument of the pre-monopoly Dutch companies. The capture also shows the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan of Atjeh]] as the diplomatic facilitator of the joint operation: his willingness to keep Lancaster's arrival secret from the Portuguese at Malaka, his consent to the operation, and his hosting of the English and Dutch squadrons at his court together made the capture possible. The capture closed the first phase of the European presence at Atjeh — the phase of free competition among the northern European nations against the Portuguese — and inaugurated the second phase, in which the [[Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie|VOC]] and the [[English East India Company|English East India Company]] would increasingly become rivals rather than partners. The next time English and Dutch squadrons met in the Strait of Malaka, in the [[1610s]], they would be manoeuvring against each other. The capture also sealed the diplomatic rapprochement between the Dutch and the [[Sultan of Atjeh (1589-1604)|sultan]] that the [[Zeeland Company's Atjeh mission (1601)|Zeeland Company's mission of 1601]] had established, and laid the groundwork for the long Dutch-Achehnese commercial relationship of the [[17th-century|seventeenth century]].
